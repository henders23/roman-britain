# The atlas's 40 turning points, AD 43–1066

*Atlas synthesis: a judgement made for the atlas, not a claim of the research pack. Each event links to the pack row that puts it on the map; the row, its narrative and its sources are where the evidence is.*

## How the list was chosen

"Most impactful" is a judgement, so the criteria are set out first. An event is on the list if:

1. **It changed things for a long time afterwards**: who ruled where, what people believed, how the land was governed or settled, or what was written down.
2. **The standard histories treat it as a turning point.** The main surveys checked were: D. Mattingly, *An Imperial Possession: Britain in the Roman Empire* (2006); P. Salway, *Roman Britain* (1981); S. Frere, *Britannia* (3rd edn, 1987); A. S. Esmonde Cleary, *The Ending of Roman Britain* (1989); R. Fleming, *Britain after Rome: The Fall and Rise, 400–1070* (2010); T. M. Charles-Edwards (ed.), *After Rome* (Short Oxford History of the British Isles, 2003); F. M. Stenton, *Anglo-Saxon England* (3rd edn, 1971); J. Campbell (ed.), *The Anglo-Saxons* (1982); T. M. Charles-Edwards, *Wales and the Britons, 350–1064* (2013); A. Woolf, *From Pictland to Alba, 789–1070* (2007); J. E. Fraser, *From Caledonia to Pictland: Scotland to 795* (2009); D. Ó Cróinín, *Early Medieval Ireland, 400–1200* (2nd edn, 2017); P. Stafford, *Unification and Conquest* (1989); G. Molyneaux, *The Formation of the English Kingdom in the Tenth Century* (2015).
3. **It covers the British Isles, not only England.** Ireland, Scotland and Wales each have their own turning points on the list.
4. **The atlas can place it honestly.** Some of the biggest changes were slow processes, such as Anglo-Saxon settlement or the spread of Christianity. Each is represented by its best-dated event or best evidence, and the journey text explains the wider process. Hadrian's Wall and Offa's Dyke are lines the atlas cannot yet draw (see INTENT.md), so the Wall is represented by Hadrian's visit of 122 and Offa's power by the Council of Chelsea of 787.
5. **One entry per story.** Boudica's revolt is one turning point, not five; 1066 has three, because Edward's death, Stamford Bridge and Hastings each changed the outcome.

The list is in date order. Numbers are for reference, not a ranking.

## The list

1. **The Claudian invasion** (AD 43; Britain). `camulodunum-surrender-43`  
   Began nearly four centuries of Roman rule in lowland Britain: towns, roads, Latin writing and a money economy all follow from it. Every survey of Roman Britain starts here (Salway 1981; Mattingly 2006).

2. **Boudica's revolt** (AD 60/61; Britain). `boudica-final-battle-60`  
   The largest rising against Roman rule in Britain; it destroyed the three main towns and led Rome to change how it governed the province, with fewer confiscations and more reliance on local elites (Mattingly 2006; Frere 1987).

3. **Mons Graupius and the limit of conquest** (AD 83; Scotland). `mons-graupius-83`  
   The furthest north Rome's army fought a set battle; the withdrawal that followed (Inchtuthil abandoned c. 87) meant northern Britain was never absorbed, which shaped the later division between Roman and non-Roman Britain (Hanson, Agricola and the Conquest of the North, 1987; Mattingly 2006).

4. **Hadrian's visit and the Wall** (AD 122; England). `hadrian-visit-britain-122`  
   The decision to build a fixed frontier from Tyne to Solway; the Wall defined the province's northern limit for most of Roman rule and is the period's best-known monument (Breeze and Dobson, Hadrian's Wall, 4th edn 2000). The Wall itself is a line the atlas cannot draw, so the visit stands for it.

5. **Severus in Britain** (208–211; England and Scotland). `severus-death-york-211`  
   The last great Roman invasion of Scotland, ended by the emperor's death at York; Britain was soon afterwards split into two provinces and the northern frontier stayed quiet for most of a century (Birley, The Roman Government of Britain, 2005; Salway 1981).

6. **Carausius and the British empire** (286–296; Britain). `carausius-usurpation-286`  
   The first time Britain broke away from Rome under its own emperor, for ten years; after its recovery in 296 Britain was reorganised into four provinces and the coastal defences of the later empire took shape (Casey, Carausius and Allectus, 1994; Mattingly 2006).

7. **Constantine proclaimed at York** (AD 306; England). `constantine-proclaimed-york-306`  
   Began the career of the emperor who made Christianity the favoured religion of the empire, with consequences for Britain and everywhere else (Salway 1981; Thomas, Christianity in Roman Britain to AD 500, 1981).

8. **Magnus Maximus** (AD 383; Britain). `magnus-maximus-proclaimed-383`  
   The first of the British army's bids for the western throne that ended with Constantine III in 407; each drew troops off the island. Gildas made Maximus's departure the start of Britain's ruin, and Welsh dynasties later claimed him as a founder (Gildas, De excidio 13–14, trans. Winterbottom 1978; Matthews, Western Aristocracies and Imperial Court AD 364–425, 1975).

9. **The end of Roman rule** (407–410; Britain). `rescript-of-honorius-410`  
   The end of Roman government in Britain: no more pay, coin or officials, and within a generation towns, villas and the money economy collapsed (Esmonde Cleary 1989; Fleming 2010). The date rests on one disputed sentence, which is why the row is low-certainty.

10. **Anglo-Saxon settlement** (c. 420s–500s (chronicle entry 441); England). `gallic-chronicle-saxons-441`  
   Stands for the Anglo-Saxon settlement, the change that turned lowland Britain into England. The chronicle entry is the only near-contemporary statement that Saxons had taken control, and anchors every modern chronology; how far it speaks for all of Britain is debated (Muhlberger, Britannia 14, 1983; Jones and Casey, Britannia 19, 1988). The Spong Hill cemetery, also added in r06, is the archaeology beside it (Hills and Lucy, Spong Hill IX, 2013).

11. **Patrick's mission to Ireland** (5th century; Ireland). `patrick-mission-ireland`  
   Patrick's two letters are the earliest writing from Ireland and the only first-hand account of Christianity carried beyond the Roman frontier from Britain; the Irish church that grew from his and others' work later evangelised much of Britain (Charles-Edwards, Early Christian Ireland, 2000; Hanson, Saint Patrick: His Origins and Career, 1968).

12. **The siege of Mount Badon** (c. 480–520; Britain). `battle-of-mons-badonicus`  
   The one battle of the fifth or early sixth century recorded by a contemporary; Gildas presents it as ending the wars and beginning a generation of peace, and historians use it to explain a pause in English expansion. Its later attachment to Arthur is legend, kept off the map (Dumville, in Lapidge and Dumville (eds), Gildas: New Approaches, 1984; Halsall, Worlds of Arthur, 2013).

13. **Columba founds Iona** (AD 563; Scotland). `columba-iona-563`  
   Iona became the mother house of a network of monasteries across Gaelic Scotland, Pictland and Northumbria, and the source of much of what is known about the north (Fraser 2009; Charles-Edwards, Early Christian Ireland, 2000).

14. **Augustine's mission to Kent** (AD 597; England). `augustine-canterbury-597`  
   The start of the Roman mission to the English, the archbishopric of Canterbury and, with it, written law and Latin learning in English kingdoms (Mayr-Harting, The Coming of Christianity to Anglo-Saxon England, 3rd edn 1991; Stenton 1971).

15. **Sutton Hoo and the first great overkings** (c. 610–635; England). `sutton-hoo-mound1`  
   The richest burial in early medieval Europe, and the clearest picture of the power of the first English overkings at the moment of conversion; its discovery in 1939 changed how the period is understood (Carver, Sutton Hoo: A Seventh-Century Princely Burial Ground, 2005; Campbell 1982).

16. **Aidan and the Irish mission to Northumbria** (AD 635; England). `aidan-lindisfarne-635`  
   Brought Iona's Irish Christianity to the most powerful English kingdom, and with it the monastic culture that produced the Lindisfarne Gospels and Bede (Mayr-Harting 1991; Fleming 2010).

17. **The battle of the Winwaed** (AD 655; England). `battle-of-the-winwaed-655`  
   Penda's death ended the career of the last powerful pagan English king, left Oswiu of Northumbria the strongest king in Britain and opened the Midlands to Christian mission (Kirby, The Earliest English Kings, 1991; Higham, The Kingdom of Northumbria AD 350–1100, 1993).

18. **The Synod of Whitby** (AD 664; England). `synod-of-whitby-664`  
   Northumbria chose Roman over Irish church custom, tying the English church to Rome and Canterbury rather than Iona (Mayr-Harting 1991; Charles-Edwards 2000).

19. **Theodore and the Synod of Hertford** (AD 672/3; England). `synod-of-hertford-672`  
   The first council of the whole English church, under Archbishop Theodore; its canons began the diocesan and synodal structure that bound the English kingdoms into one church long before they were one kingdom (Cubitt, Anglo-Saxon Church Councils c. 650–c. 850, 1995; Mayr-Harting 1991).

20. **Dun Nechtain (Nechtansmere)** (AD 685; Scotland). `dun-nechtain-685`  
   The Picts killed King Ecgfrith and ended Northumbrian rule north of the Forth, so that the north developed as Pictish and later Scottish, not English (Fraser 2009; Woolf, "Dún Nechtáin, Fortriu and the geography of the Picts", Scottish Historical Review 85, 2006).

21. **Bede's Ecclesiastical History** (AD 731; England). `bede-historia-731`  
   The foundation of English history-writing: much of what is known of the period 597–731 comes from it, and it gave the English a single story as one people (Wormald, "Bede, the Bretwaldas and the origins of the gens Anglorum", 1983; Campbell 1982).

22. **Offa's supremacy: the Council of Chelsea** (AD 787; England). `council-of-chelsea-787`  
   Stands for Offa of Mercia's supremacy over southern England, the greatest English kingship before Wessex. At Chelsea Offa split the province of Canterbury and had his son consecrated king, the first recorded royal anointing in England (Brooks, The Early History of the Church of Canterbury, 1984; Story, Carolingian Connections, 2003). Offa's Dyke is a line the atlas cannot draw.

23. **The Viking raid on Lindisfarne** (AD 793; England). `lindisfarne-raid-793`  
   The first dated Viking raid on Britain, conventionally the start of the Viking age in the British Isles, and a shock recorded by contemporaries such as Alcuin (Richards, Viking Age England, 2004; Downham, Viking Kings of Britain and Ireland, 2007).

24. **The Norse base at Dublin** (AD 841; Ireland). `dublin-longphort-841`  
   Marks the change in Ireland from seasonal raiding to permanent Viking bases; Dublin became the main Scandinavian town in Ireland and its kings were a power across the Irish Sea, in northern Britain and at York for two centuries (Downham, Viking Kings of Britain and Ireland, 2007; Simpson, in Duffy (ed.), Medieval Dublin VI, 2005).

25. **Cináed mac Ailpín, king of the Picts** (c. 842–843; Scotland). `cinaed-mac-ailpin-king-of-picts`  
   Cináed founded the dynasty that ruled the kingdom later called Alba, then Scotland. Modern work treats his accession as the symbolic start of a gradual change from Pictland to Alba rather than a conquest (Woolf, From Pictland to Alba, 2007; Broun, Scottish Independence and the Idea of Britain, 2007).

26. **The great army takes York** (AD 866; England). `great-army-takes-york-866`  
   The moment the Viking presence in England turned from raiding to conquest: within a decade the army had destroyed or divided three of the four English kingdoms, and York became the capital of a Scandinavian kingdom for nearly a century (Keynes and Lapidge 1983; Downham 2007).

27. **Edington** (AD 878; England). `battle-of-edington-878`  
   Alfred's victory saved Wessex, the only English kingdom left standing, and led to the settlement with Guthrum that divided England (Keynes and Lapidge, Alfred the Great, 1983; Stenton 1971).

28. **Alfred's burhs** (c. 878–899; England). `wallingford-burh`  
   A planned network of fortified places within a day's march of every part of Wessex; many became the towns and mints of later England, and they were the base for the conquest of the Danelaw (Hill and Rumble (eds), The Defence of Wessex: The Burghal Hidage, 1996).

29. **Athelstan at Eamont** (AD 927; England). `eamont-submission-927`  
   With York taken and the rulers of Bamburgh, the Scots, Strathclyde and Wales bound to him, Athelstan became the first king of all the English and claimed overlordship of Britain; many historians date a single English kingdom from 927 (Foot, Æthelstan: The First King of England, 2011; Molyneaux 2015).

30. **Brunanburh** (AD 937; England). `battle-of-brunanburh-937`  
   Athelstan's defeat of the kings of Dublin, Alba and Strathclyde kept the new kingdom of the English together; contemporaries treated it as the greatest battle of the age (Livingston (ed.), The Battle of Brunanburh: A Casebook, 2011; Foot, Æthelstan, 2011).

31. **The end of the kingdom of York** (AD 954; England). `eric-bloodaxe-expelled-954`  
   Ended the independent kingdom of York for good; from then on Northumbria was ruled by earls for the West Saxon king, and England had one king (Downham, 'Eric Bloodaxe – axed?', Mediaeval Scandinavia 14, 2004; Stafford 1989).

32. **Edgar's coronation at Bath** (AD 973; England). `edgar-coronation-bath-973`  
   Edgar's consecration, the Chester meeting and the reform of the coinage show a kingdom governed from the centre and claiming supremacy in Britain; the rite used became the basis of later English coronations (Scragg (ed.), Edgar, King of the English 959–975, 2008; Molyneaux 2015).

33. **Maldon and the return of the Vikings** (AD 991; England). `battle-of-maldon-991`  
   Opened the second Viking age under Æthelred II: the defeat was followed by the first great tribute payment of 10,000 pounds, beginning the cycle of payments and invasions that ended in the Danish conquest (Keynes, 'The historical context of the Battle of Maldon', in Scragg (ed.), The Battle of Maldon AD 991, 1991).

34. **Clontarf** (AD 1014; Ireland). `battle-of-clontarf-1014`  
   Killed Brian Bóruma and his heirs and ended the most successful attempt yet to make the kingship of Ireland a real overlordship. It did not drive out the Vikings (Dublin stayed Norse); its fame owes much to later propaganda (Ó Corráin, Ireland before the Normans, 1972; Duffy, Brian Boru and the Battle of Clontarf, 2013; Ní Mhaonaigh, Peritia 9, 1995).

35. **Assandun and the Danish conquest** (AD 1016; England). `battle-of-assandun-1016`  
   Cnut's decisive victory over Edmund Ironside gave him all England in 1017; England became the centre of a Danish North Sea empire until 1042, and its aristocracy was remade with new earls such as Godwine (Lawson, Cnut, 1993; Bolton, Cnut the Great, 2017).

36. **Carham and the Tweed frontier** (AD 1018; Scotland and England). `battle-of-carham-1018`  
   Most often linked with the Scots' final hold on Lothian and so with the Tweed as the eastern Anglo-Scottish border, though recent work treats that as only partly supported (Duncan, Scottish Historical Review 55, 1976; McGuigan and Woolf (eds), The Battle of Carham: A Thousand Years On, 2018).

37. **Gruffudd ap Llywelyn, king of all Wales** (1055; Wales). `gruffudd-sacks-hereford-1055`  
   The high point of Gruffudd ap Llywelyn, the only ruler ever recognised as king over all Wales. His fall in 1063 left Wales divided just before the Normans came, which is why R. R. Davies begins his history of medieval Wales in 1063 (Maund, Ireland, Wales and England in the Eleventh Century, 1991; Davies, Conquest, Coexistence and Change, 1987).

38. **Edward the Confessor's death and Harold's coronation** (January 1066; England). `edward-dies-harold-crowned-1066`  
   Edward's childless death and Harold's immediate coronation set off the succession crisis in which Norwegian and Norman claimants invaded; whether Edward had promised the throne to William is still argued from sources that divide on national lines (Barlow, Edward the Confessor, 1970; Baxter, in Mortimer (ed.), Edward the Confessor: The Man and the Legend, 2009).

39. **Stamford Bridge** (September 1066; England). `battle-of-stamford-bridge-1066`  
   Harold's defeat of Harald Hardrada ended the last great Norwegian bid for the English throne, but drew the English army north just as William landed (Huscroft, The Norman Conquest: A New Introduction, 2009; DeVries, The Norwegian Invasion of England in 1066, 1999).

40. **Hastings** (October 1066; England). `battle-of-hastings-1066`  
   The Norman Conquest replaced the English ruling class, transformed the church, language and law, and closes the atlas's period (Huscroft 2009; Stenton 1971).

## Considered and left out

- **The Antonine Wall (142)**: A line the atlas cannot draw, and held for only about twenty years; its story is told at the Severus stop.
- **The "Barbarian Conspiracy" (367)**: A serious crisis, but its scale is debated and Roman Britain recovered; it stays on the map and in the Defending the late Roman province journey.
- **Edwin of Northumbria's baptism (627)**: Undone by Edwin's death in 633; the lasting conversion of Northumbria came with Oswald and Aidan, which is on the list.
- **The Lindisfarne Gospels and the Book of Kells**: Great achievements, but works of art rather than turning points; the Gospels are on the map and in three journeys.
- **The Staffordshire Hoard**: A spectacular find that changed what we know, not what happened; it is in two journeys.
- **Æthelflæd and Edward the Elder's conquest of the Danelaw (910–920)**: Essential, but a campaign of many years; it is told as the lead-up at the Eamont stop.
- **The laws of Hywel Dda (mid-10th century)**: Important for Welsh law, but the surviving texts are 13th-century and the link to Hywel is debated; on the map and in the Wales journey.
- **The Benedictine reform and the Regularis Concordia (c. 970)**: Folded into Edgar's reign at the Bath stop.
- **The St Brice's Day massacre (1002) and Sweyn Forkbeard's conquest (1013)**: Added to the map in r06 as stops in the Danish conquest journey, leading up to Assandun.
- **Rhodri Mawr (d. 878) and the rise of Gwynedd**: Important for Wales, but Gruffudd ap Llywelyn's brief rule of all Wales is the clearer turning point.

## The list against the journeys

Before this round, 21 of the 40 were in the pack and 20 featured in a journey; Augustine's mission was on the map but in no journey, and Ireland had no events at all. Now all 40 are on the map and each is in at least one journey. In its home journey each is written in three parts: what led up to it, what happened, and what changed afterwards. Neighbouring stops show the chain, and the atlas prints the time and distance between stops.
