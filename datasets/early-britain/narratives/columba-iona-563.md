## Summary
Columba, an Irish abbot of royal descent, crossed to Britain and founded a monastery on Iona, by tradition in 563. It became the mother house of a federation of churches across Ireland and northern Britain.

## Detail
The date comes from the Irish annals: the Annals of Ulster enter Columba's voyage to Iona under 563, in his forty-second year, and Adomnán, abbot of Iona writing about a century later, says he sailed two years after the battle of Cúl Drebene. Bede, writing in Northumbria, puts Columba's arrival in 565 and says the Pictish king Bridei gave him the island, whereas Irish tradition says it was the king of Dál Riata; the gap probably reflects different reckonings rather than two events. Adomnán's Life is a collection of miracles, prophecies and visions, so it is valuable for the monastery and its setting rather than as a chronicle. On the ground, the early monastery lay inside a large earthwork enclosure (the vallum) around the site of the medieval abbey. Charcoal from a small hut on the rocky knoll of Tòrr an Aba, excavated by Charles Thomas in 1957 and dated only in 2017, falls between about AD 540 and 650; it fits Columba's time, but identifying the hut as his own writing cell, as Adomnán's description invites, remains interpretation.

## Significance
Iona was the base from which Irish monks spread Christianity among the Picts and, through Aidan, the Northumbrians. Its abbots and their writings shaped the church of northern Britain until the Synod of Whitby in 664 and beyond.
