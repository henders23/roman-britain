## Summary
At Bath, the Romans enclosed a hot spring in a reservoir and built beside it a classical temple to Sulis Minerva and a large bathing complex.

## Detail
The Roman remains at Bath have been uncovered piecemeal since the 18th century, when the temple pediment with its bearded head was found during building work, and more systematically by Barry Cunliffe and others in the 1960s–80s. The first temple and the engineered spring are placed by the excavators in the late Neronian or early Flavian years; T. F. C. Blagg argued from the architecture for a somewhat later Flavian date, so the pack uses a window. The cult is attested by altars such as RIB 146, set up by a centurion of the Second Legion in the later 2nd century, and by more than a hundred curse tablets of lead and tin thrown into the spring, published by Roger Tomlin as the Tabellae Sulis. Most tablets ask the goddess to punish thieves. The complex was rebuilt and enlarged over the following centuries, so the dates here refer to its beginning only.

## Significance
Bath is the clearest example in Britain of a native deity joined with a Roman one at a monumental sanctuary. The curse tablets preserve the names and worries of ordinary people who rarely appear in other sources.
