## Summary
Cassius Dio tells of a hard-fought, two-day battle at a river crossing early in the invasion of AD 43. By tradition it is placed on the Medway in Kent, although Dio gives the river no name.

## Detail
Dio's account, written some 170 years after the event, is the only source. He says the Britons camped on the far bank of a river, trusting it to protect them. Troops trained to swim in armour, probably auxiliaries, crossed and attacked the chariot horses. Other forces, including a group under the future emperor Vespasian, followed. The fighting went into a second day, and Gnaeus Hosidius Geta, who was nearly captured, won the battle and was later given triumphal honours.

The identification with the Medway is a modern inference. It depends on the army having landed at Richborough and marched west towards the Thames. Scholars who favour a landing near Chichester have proposed other rivers. No battlefield has been found by archaeology. For that reason the atlas shows the traditional area of the lower Medway as a halo, not a point, and the location should be read as a hypothesis.

## Significance
Dio presents it as the battle that opened south-east Britain to the Roman army. It is also a clear case of an event that is well attested in writing but cannot be placed on the ground.
