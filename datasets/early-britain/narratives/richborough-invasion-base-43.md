## Summary
At Richborough in east Kent, excavators found a pair of Claudian ditches that fortified a stretch of shoreline. They are widely taken to be the defended base where the invasion army of AD 43 came ashore.

## Detail
Richborough (Rutupiae) lay on the Wantsum Channel, which then divided the Isle of Thanet from mainland Kent. When the Society of Antiquaries dug there between the 1920s and 1930s under J. P. Bushe-Fox, the team uncovered two closely spaced V-shaped ditches, with a rampart on the side facing the sea. They ran north to south for at least 650 metres, parallel to the old shoreline. Their dating to the start of the Roman period rests on Claudian finds and on their position beneath everything later on the site. Richborough went on to be a supply base, a port with a great monumental arch and, in the late Roman period, a Saxon Shore fort, and the whole complex is a scheduled monument.

That Richborough was where the army landed is an interpretation. No ancient writer names the place. Some scholars argue that the main landing, or a second one, came ashore in the Chichester and Fishbourne area, where rulers friendly to Rome held power, and the question is still open.

## Significance
The ditches are the most direct physical trace of the moment Britain became part of the Roman empire. They also show how the army secured a bridgehead before moving inland. Where the army landed shapes how historians reconstruct the whole campaign of AD 43.
