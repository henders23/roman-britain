## Summary
On 24 September, probably in 672, Archbishop Theodore of Canterbury met the bishops of the English kingdoms at Herutford, usually identified with Hertford. They agreed ten rules that began the regular organisation of the English church.

## Detail
Theodore, a Greek monk from Tarsus, was consecrated archbishop by Pope Vitalian in 668 and reached Canterbury in May 669. Bede says he travelled through every part of the island where the English lived, and was the first archbishop the whole English church obeyed. Bede's Ecclesiastical History (IV.5) copies what appears to be the synod's own record. The record dates the meeting to 24 September in the first indiction and the third year of Ecgfrith's reign in Northumbria. Both point to 672, but Bede puts it in 673. Most historians now accept 672. Bede lists the bishops present: Bisi of the East Angles, Putta of Rochester, Leuthere of the West Saxons and Winfrith of the Mercians. Wilfrid of the Northumbrians sent representatives. Theodore read out ten chapters from a book of church canons. They required Easter to be kept on the Roman reckoning. They also said bishops were not to act in one another's dioceses, monks and clergy were not to wander from their monastery or their bishop, a synod was to meet once a year at Clofesho on 1 August, bishops were to rank by date of consecration, and marriage was to be lawful and permanent. A proposal to create more bishoprics as the number of Christians grew was discussed but put off. Herutford is usually taken to be Hertford on the River Lea, close to Ermine Street. Hartford near Huntingdon has also been proposed, and nothing has been found on the ground at either place. The map shows a small halo over Hertford.

## Significance
Hertford was the first council of the whole English church. Its canons were the first written rules made for that church as one body, and they set the pattern of bishops, dioceses and regular synods that Theodore then built up, splitting the large early dioceses into smaller ones over the following decade. Historians treat it as the start of a single English church across several kingdoms, two and a half centuries before a single English kingdom. That is an interpretation, but a widely shared one.
