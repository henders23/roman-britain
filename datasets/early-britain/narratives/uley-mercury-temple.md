## Summary
On West Hill above Uley, a stone temple to Mercury replaced an Iron Age shrine in the early 2nd century. Visitors left well over a hundred lead tablets asking the god to punish thieves.

## Detail
The site came to light when a water main was laid in 1976, and Ann Woodward and Peter Leach excavated it in 1977–9 (merged here), publishing it in 1993. A Romano-Celtic temple was built in the early 2nd century over an earlier shrine and enlarged in the 4th century. Mercury is identified as the main god from the head of the limestone cult statue (British Museum 1978,0102.1), carved in the later 2nd century in a fully classical style and later carefully buried on the site, and from an altar, figurines and the tablets themselves. The tablets, read and published by Roger Tomlin, are mostly complaints about stolen goods, such as a pair of gloves or an animal, asking Mercury to take the thief's health until the property is returned; some are blank but were folded and offered like the rest. They were left over a long period and many cannot be closely dated. The finds are in the British Museum.

## Significance
With Bath, Uley holds most of the curse tablets known from Roman Britain, and they record ordinary people's worries, names and Latin. The site also shows a sacred place used from prehistory through the Roman period and beyond.
