## Summary
On St Brice's Day, 13 November 1002, King Æthelred II ordered the killing of all the Danish men in England. The one place where a contemporary source describes what happened is Oxford, where Danes who fled into St Frideswide's minster died when the church was burned.

## Detail
The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle (manuscripts C, D and E, in annals for Æthelred's reign that most historians think were written up after it ended) says that in 1002 the king was told the Danes meant to take his life, and then those of his councillors, and seize the kingdom, so he ordered all the Danish men in England to be killed on St Brice's Day. It names no places and gives no numbers. The fullest account is in a charter Æthelred issued at Headington on 7 December 1004 (Sawyer S 909), renewing the lost title deeds of St Frideswide's minster in Oxford; it survives only in later copies. The king explains that he had decreed, with the advice of his leading men, that the Danes who had sprung up in the island like weeds among the wheat should be destroyed by a most just extermination. Danes in Oxford, trying to escape, broke into the church and barricaded themselves inside, and when the townsmen could not drive them out the church was burned with its ornaments and books; he had since rebuilt it. Translations differ over exactly who set the fire. How widely the order was carried out elsewhere is unknown, and it cannot have been aimed at the whole long-settled population of the Danelaw; who exactly counted as 'Danish' is debated. In 2008 Thames Valley Archaeological Services, digging ahead of building work at St John's College, about a kilometre north of the cathedral, found the remains of some 35 young men, mostly aged 16 to 25 (reports vary between 34 and 38 individuals), thrown into the ditch of a prehistoric henge. Many had been stabbed repeatedly, and some bones were charred. The excavators suggested they were victims of 1002, and isotope analysis published by Mark Pollard and colleagues in 2012 found their diet and origins closer to Scandinavian than to lowland English populations. But the same study notes that its radiocarbon dates favour the tenth century, and it raises the possibility that the men were captured raiders who were executed; the link to the massacre is therefore possible, not proven. A comparable find, the pit on Ridgeway Hill near Weymouth in Dorset holding about fifty decapitated men of Scandinavian origin, radiocarbon dated to about 970–1025, shows that Scandinavians were put to death in England in these decades, but it is not linked to the massacre by any source.

## Significance
The massacre is the starkest sign of how far the renewed Viking attacks of the 990s had strained Æthelred's kingdom: a king ordering the killing of a group of his subjects defined by origin, and then describing it in a charter as just. Some historians, medieval and modern, have seen it as a provocation for King Swein of Denmark's attacks on England in the following years, which ended in the Danish conquest of 1013–16; that connection is an interpretation, not something the Chronicle states.
