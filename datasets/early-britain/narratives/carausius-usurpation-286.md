## Summary
In about 286 Carausius, commander of the Channel fleet, declared himself emperor and ruled Britain and part of northern Gaul as a breakaway state until his murder in 293.

## Detail
Aurelius Victor and Eutropius, both writing in the 360s, say that Carausius, a Menapian of humble birth, was given a fleet based at Boulogne to clear the Channel of Frankish and Saxon raiders, but was suspected of letting them raid so as to seize their plunder. Condemned to death by Maximian, he took the purple and held Britain. Two speeches in the Panegyrici Latini, delivered in 289 and 297, describe him as a pirate and refer to failed and then successful campaigns against him. His own coinage, minted in Britain at London and a second mint, is the best contemporary evidence: it includes silver coins with legends drawn from Virgil promising a returning golden age, and coins claiming partnership with Diocletian and Maximian, which they never conceded. The year of his seizure is disputed; Victor's figure of seven years before Allectus killed him in 293 points to 286, but 287 is also argued. No place of proclamation is known.

## Significance
The Carausian revolt was the first time Britain was ruled as an independent Roman state, and it led to Constantius's reconquest in 296 and to the strengthening of the coastal forts later called the Saxon Shore. His coins are an unusual case of a usurper speaking in his own words.
