## Summary
When the emperor Constantius I died at York on 25 July 306, the army in Britain proclaimed his son Constantine emperor in his place.

## Detail
Lactantius, a Christian writer at the court of Constantine, tells how Constantius, seriously ill, asked Galerius to send his son to him, and how Constantine escaped and rode across the empire to reach his father, who named him successor on his deathbed. Eusebius's Life of Constantine gives a similar deathbed scene and says the army proclaimed the son at once. Neither names the place. The Origo Constantini, an anonymous short history of the later 4th century, and Eutropius say that Constantius died at York after a campaign against the Picts and that the soldiers then made Constantine emperor. The day is the anniversary Constantine himself later kept for his accession. The sources differ on whether he was first hailed as Augustus or Caesar; Galerius accepted him only as Caesar. Where in York the events took place is unknown; a statue of Constantine now stands beside York Minster, near the site of the legionary headquarters, but no building is identified with the proclamation.

## Significance
An acclamation by the army in Britain began the reign of the emperor who legalised Christianity and founded Constantinople. It shows York's importance as a base for imperial campaigns in the north.
