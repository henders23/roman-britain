## Summary
In about AD 240 a temple to the eastern god Mithras was built beside the Walbrook stream in Londinium. Its excavation in 1954 made it the most famous Roman building in London.

## Detail
The temple was a rectangular hall with an apse at the west end, a central aisle and raised side benches, the plan used for Mithraic worship elsewhere in the empire. W. F. Grimes and Audrey Williams excavated it in 1954 during rebuilding on the bombed Bucklersbury House site (merged here); the find of a marble head of Mithras in a Phrygian cap identified the building and drew huge crowds. Other marble sculptures from the site include Minerva, Serapis, Mercury and a Bacchic group, and a marble relief of Mithras killing the bull, found nearby in 1889, carries a dedication by Ulpius Silvanus, a veteran of the Second Legion (RIB 3). John Shepherd's full report of 1998 dates the building to about 240 from finds in its construction levels. Several sculptures had been carefully buried inside the temple in the 4th century, and its last phase is debated: some see continued Mithraic use, others a change to another cult, perhaps Bacchus. The remains were moved to Queen Victoria Street in 1962 and returned close to their original position in 2017.

## Significance
The Walbrook temple is the best evidence from Britain for the mystery cults that spread through the army and merchants of the empire. Its sculptures, imported marble of high quality, show the wealth of 3rd-century London, and its excavation was a turning point in public interest in the archaeology of the City.
