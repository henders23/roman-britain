## Summary
On the Tintagel headland, excavation found buildings and a large quantity of pottery imported from the eastern Mediterranean in the centuries after Rome.

## Detail
C. A. Ralegh Radford dug on the headland in the 1930s and interpreted the buildings as a Celtic monastery. Later work, including the Glasgow University excavations of 1990–99 published by Barrowman, Batey and Morris, and more recent English Heritage projects, has instead supported a secular high-status settlement. Its dating rests on imported pottery: Phocaean Red Slip bowls from the Aegean and amphorae from the eastern Mediterranean, found in larger quantity here than at any other site in Britain or Ireland. These are conventionally dated to the late 5th and 6th centuries, though some forms have recently been re-dated later, which is why the window here is broad. The visible castle ruins are 13th-century and belong to a much later period. The site's later fame as Arthur's birthplace comes from literature and is kept out of this row.

## Significance
Tintagel shows that parts of western Britain kept long-distance contacts with the Mediterranean world after Roman rule ended. It is a key site for understanding who held power in the south-west in the 5th and 6th centuries.
