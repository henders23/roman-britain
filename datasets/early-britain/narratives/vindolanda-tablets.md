## Summary
Hundreds of thin wooden tablets, written in ink or scratched on wax, survived in waterlogged layers at the fort of Vindolanda. They are the largest body of everyday Latin writing from Roman Britain.

## Detail
The tablets were excavated from the 1970s onwards in the damp, oxygen-poor deposits of successive timber forts near the Stanegate, a little south of the later line of Hadrian's Wall. They include strength reports of units, accounts, requests for leave, and private letters between officers' households. The best known, Tab. Vindol. II 291, is an invitation from Claudia Severa to Sulpicia Lepidina, wife of the commander Flavius Cerialis, to a birthday celebration; its closing lines appear to be in Severa's own hand, which would make them among the earliest known Latin written by a woman. The texts are dated by the fort period in which they lay, and by the officers and units they name, to a span from about the 80s to the 120s, with most from the years around 100. They are edited in the Tabulae Vindolandenses volumes by Alan Bowman and David Thomas, and the first two volumes are online. Debate concerns the reading of damaged texts and how typical this frontier community was of the province, not the date or place.

## Significance
The tablets let us see the Roman army in Britain from the inside: its paperwork, its supplies and the social life of its officers' families. They also show how widely writing was used on the frontier.
