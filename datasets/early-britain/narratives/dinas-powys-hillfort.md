## Summary
Dinas Powys, a small hillfort near Cardiff, was the home of a wealthy household in the 5th to 7th centuries that imported wine, pottery and glass from the Mediterranean and Gaul. It is the type site for post-Roman high-status settlement in Wales.

## Detail
Leslie Alcock excavated the site between 1954 and 1958 (merged into this row) and published it in 1963. Expecting an Iron Age fort, he found instead sherds of imported post-Roman pottery like those from Tintagel, fragments of Frankish and Anglo-Saxon glass, and moulds, crucibles and scrap from making fine metalwork, along with a large collection of animal bones. He read the site as the court of a local ruler, and thought its main ramparts were added much later, by the Normans.

Later work has changed the dating. Andy Seaman and Alan Lane re-examined nearby earthworks in 2011–14, and in 2023 Ewan Campbell, Seaman, Lane and Gordon Noble published a new radiocarbon programme with Bayesian modelling. It identifies a 5th-century phase of hearths, a post-built structure and perhaps a palisade, then a developed promontory fort with new banks in the 6th and 7th centuries, with activity ending in the 7th century. The identity of the household is unknown; links with the kingdom of Glywysing are suggestion only.

## Significance
Dinas Powys shows that, after Roman rule, western British elites kept up trade with the Mediterranean world. Its redating is a good example of how new radiocarbon work can overturn a long-accepted site history.
