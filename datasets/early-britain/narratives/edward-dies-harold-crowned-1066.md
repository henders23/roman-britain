## Summary
Edward the Confessor died at Westminster on 5 January 1066, days after his new abbey church there had been consecrated, and was buried in it the next day. On the same day, 6 January, Harold Godwinson was crowned king.

## Detail
Manuscripts C and D of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle record that Edward came to Westminster at midwinter 1065 and had the minster he had built consecrated on Holy Innocents' Day (28 December), and that he died on the eve of Twelfth Night and was buried on Twelfth Day, 6 January 1066. Their entries include a poem on his death which says that the wise king entrusted the kingdom to Harold; the Chronicle also says that Harold succeeded to the kingdom as the king had granted it to him and as he was chosen. The Life of King Edward (Vita Ædwardi Regis), written by a Flemish monk for Edward's widow Queen Edith, partly before and partly soon after the Conquest, describes Edward on his deathbed stretching out his hand to Harold and commending to him the queen and the kingdom. John of Worcester, writing in the early twelfth century, says Harold was chosen by the leading men of England and crowned the same day by Ealdred, archbishop of York. Norman writers told it differently: William of Poitiers, writing in the 1070s, says Edward had long before designated Duke William as his heir and that Harold, on a visit to Normandy, had sworn an oath to support William's claim, so that Harold's coronation was usurpation. The Bayeux Tapestry shows Harold's oath, Edward's body carried to the church of St Peter at Westminster, the dying king speaking to his followers, and Harold enthroned with Archbishop Stigand, whose position was irregular in the eyes of the papacy. Edward's church survives only as foundations beneath the present abbey, which Henry III began to rebuild in 1245; his shrine is still there.

## Significance
Harold's accession was the trigger for the crisis of 1066: both Harald Hardrada of Norway and William of Normandy invaded to press their own claims, leading to the battles of Fulford, Stamford Bridge and Hastings. Whether Edward truly bequeathed the throne to Harold, or had earlier promised it to William, cannot be settled from the sources, which divide along English and Norman lines; historians still weigh them differently.
