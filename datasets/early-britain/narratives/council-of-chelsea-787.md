## Summary
In 787 a 'contentious synod' met at Cealchyth, usually identified with Chelsea on the Thames. There Archbishop Jænberht of Canterbury lost part of his province to a new archbishopric at Lichfield, and Offa of Mercia's son Ecgfrith was consecrated king. Ecgfrith's consecration is the first recorded consecration of an English king.

## Detail
The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle reports the synod in one sentence. It enters it under 785, but the Chronicle's dates for these decades run two years early, and the events belong to 787. It says the synod was contentious, that Jænberht gave up part of his province, that Hygeberht was chosen by King Offa, and that Ecgfrith was consecrated king. Papal letters show that Pope Hadrian I agreed to raise Lichfield to an archbishopric, and Hygeberht, already bishop there, became the archbishop of the Mercian dioceses. The year before, papal legates led by George of Ostia had visited Offa's court and Northumbria, but there is no good evidence that they were at Chelsea. Nicholas Brooks argued that the new archbishopric and the consecration were linked: Jænberht would not consecrate Ecgfrith, so Offa had an archbishop created who would. The rite probably copied Pope Hadrian's anointing of Charlemagne's sons in 781. The meeting is part of the story of Offa's reign (757–796). Offa took direct control of Kent and Sussex and was overlord across most of southern England. He issued a new, broad, thin silver penny in his own name, which became the basic English coin, and he exchanged letters and gifts with Charlemagne, whose letter of 796 about pilgrims, merchants and cloaks survives. Asser, writing a century later, says Offa had a great dyke built from sea to sea; it is a line, not a point, and is not drawn here. Cealchyth is usually taken to be Chelsea, where later councils also met, but this is not proved. Older scholars suggested Chalk in Kent. The map shows a small halo at Chelsea only.

## Significance
Chelsea shows how far Offa's power reached: he could redraw the church's provinces in his own interest and have a successor anointed while he was still alive. Lichfield's archbishopric was abolished in 803, after Offa's death, but royal anointing became a lasting part of English kingship. Calling the consecration the start of that tradition, and reading Offa as overlord of the English south, are historians' interpretations of thin evidence.
