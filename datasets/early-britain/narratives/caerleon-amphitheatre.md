## Summary
Outside the legionary fortress at Caerleon, Roman soldiers built an amphitheatre about AD 90 for displays, training and shows.

## Detail
The amphitheatre stands just outside the west wall of Isca, the fortress of Legio II Augusta. It is oval, about 90 by 70 metres, with a stone outer wall braced by buttresses, and earth banks that carried timber seats. Mortimer Wheeler and Tessa Verney Wheeler excavated it in 1926–27, removing tens of thousands of tonnes of soil, and published it in Archaeologia in 1928; the monument then passed into state care and is now managed by Cadw. Its construction is dated to about AD 90 from finds and building sequence rather than from an inscription, and it was repaired and altered during the following centuries.

The structure was presumably used for gladiatorial and animal shows as well as military drill, but how often and for whom is inferred, not recorded. Its capacity, often put at about 6,000, is a modern estimate. The medieval legend that the grassed-over ruin was King Arthur's Round Table is recorded separately as an excluded row.

## Significance
The amphitheatre shows the permanence of the legionary base at Caerleon by the late first century, and how a fortress provided for the leisure and discipline of its soldiers. Its excavation was an early landmark of public archaeology in Wales.
