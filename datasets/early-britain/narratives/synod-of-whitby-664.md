## Summary
In 664 King Oswiu of Northumbria held a meeting at Abbess Hild's monastery at Whitby to settle a dispute over how to calculate the date of Easter, and decided for the Roman method.

## Detail
Northumbria had been converted largely by monks from Iona, who dated Easter by an older system; others in the kingdom, including Oswiu's queen, followed Rome, so the court sometimes kept Easter on different days. The two accounts of the meeting are by Stephen of Ripon, in his life of Wilfrid written soon after 710, and by Bede, who finished his history in 731. Bede describes Bishop Colman of Lindisfarne speaking for Iona's practice and Wilfrid for Rome's, with the king choosing Rome because its tradition claimed the authority of St Peter. Colman then left for Iona and Ireland. The speeches are the authors' reconstructions, and historians debate how far the meeting was about church custom and how far about politics within the royal family. The meeting place, Streanæshalch, is generally identified with Whitby, where the headland holds remains of the early monastery beneath and around the later abbey ruins.

## Significance
The decision tied the Northumbrian church more closely to Rome and to Canterbury. Bede treated it as a turning point, and it is often used, perhaps too simply, as the moment the English churches chose Rome over Iona.
