## Summary
In summer 1013 King Swein of Denmark brought his fleet up the Trent to Gainsborough, where the north of England submitted to him. Within months the rest of the country had accepted him as king and Æthelred had fled to Normandy, but Swein died at Gainsborough on 3 February 1014.

## Detail
The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle (manuscripts C, D and E) tells the story in its annals for 1013 and 1014. Before August 1013 Swein came with his fleet to Sandwich, then sailed round East Anglia into the Humber and up the Trent to Gainsborough. There Earl Uhtred and the Northumbrians, the people of Lindsey, the Five Boroughs and soon all the army north of Watling Street submitted to him and gave hostages. Leaving the ships and hostages with his son Cnut, Swein marched south: Oxford and Winchester submitted, London at first held out, and at Bath the western thegns submitted too, so that, in the Chronicle's words, the whole nation regarded him as full king. Æthelred sent his queen Emma and their sons to her brother Duke Richard II in Normandy, spent Christmas on the Isle of Wight and then followed them. Swein died at Candlemas 1014, which the Chronicle dates to the third day before the nones of February (3 February), and the fleet chose Cnut as king; the English councillors instead recalled Æthelred. The Chronicle places Swein's arrival and his death at Gainsborough but does not say where in or near the town he was based. Local tradition puts his army's camp at Castle Hills, Thonock, and a royal hall where the late medieval Old Hall now stands, but neither has been confirmed by excavation, so the pin marks the town only.

## Significance
Swein was the first Danish king to be accepted over the whole of England, though only for about five weeks. His campaign showed that English resistance had collapsed after years of raiding and tribute, and it gave his son Cnut the footing from which he conquered England in 1015–16. Historians treat 1013 as the beginning of the Danish conquest that ended with Cnut's accession.
