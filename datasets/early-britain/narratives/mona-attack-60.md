## Summary
The governor Suetonius Paulinus attacked the island of Mona (Anglesey), a refuge for enemies of Rome and a centre of the druids. While he was there, the great revolt broke out in the east.

## Detail
Tacitus says Paulinus built flat-bottomed boats to take his infantry across the shallow, shifting channel, while the cavalry forded or swam beside their horses. On the far shore stood armed men, women in black carrying torches, and druids calling down curses. After a moment of hesitation the Romans attacked, won, and cut down the sacred groves. Then news came of the rising in the east. Tacitus's Agricola and Dio also mention the campaign, briefly.

The crossing is usually placed on the Menai Strait, but no landing place or battlefield has been identified, so the atlas uses a halo over the island. Tacitus's vivid scene is literary and may be heightened. As for the date, Tacitus puts the attack and the revolt under the consuls of AD 61, but many historians think the revolt began in AD 60. The atlas uses 60 for every event of the revolt, marked as approximate.

## Significance
The attack explains why the governor and much of the army were far away when the revolt began. It is also the main ancient account of druids in Britain.
