## Summary
In 407 the army in Britain made a soldier named Constantine emperor; he took the province's mobile troops to Gaul and held the western provinces until 411.

## Detail
Zosimus, drawing on the lost history of Olympiodorus, says the troops in Britain first raised a certain Marcus, killed him, then raised Gratian, a local man, and killed him after four months, before turning to Constantine. Orosius, writing in 417, adds that Constantine was a common soldier chosen for the hope his name inspired, and Sozomen tells a parallel story. Constantine crossed to Gaul, won over the armies there in the wake of the barbarian crossing of the Rhine, and for a time was recognised by Honorius, before his general Gerontius rebelled against him and he was captured and executed in 411. The chronology is muddled: Zosimus gives two different years for the first mutiny, and Orosius puts the whole episode in 408. No source says where in Britain any of the three usurpers was proclaimed, so the atlas draws a halo over the province. Zosimus later reports that the Britons expelled Constantine's officials, which belongs to the end of Roman rule.

## Significance
The departure of the field army with Constantine is usually seen as the last step before Roman government in Britain ended about 410. The story comes from writers far from Britain, which is why its dates and details are uncertain.
