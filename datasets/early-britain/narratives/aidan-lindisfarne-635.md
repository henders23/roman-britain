## Summary
Soon after becoming king of Northumbria, Oswald asked Iona for a bishop; the monk Aidan came and was given the island of Lindisfarne for his see and monastery, around 635.

## Detail
Bede is the source: he tells how Oswald, who had spent his exile among the Irish, sent to the elders of the Scots for a bishop, how a first, harsh missionary failed, and how Aidan was sent instead and given Lindisfarne, a tidal island within sight of the royal fortress at Bamburgh. Bede describes Aidan's humble way of life, his travelling on foot and Oswald himself acting as interpreter. He gives no year for Aidan's arrival; 635 is worked out from Oswald's accession after the battle of Heavenfield in 634 and from Bede's statement that Aidan died in 651 in the seventeenth year of his episcopate. The monastic buildings of Aidan's day have not been securely located: the remains of the early monastery are thought to lie under and around the later Benedictine priory, which is scheduled with them. Bede wrote nearly a century later and admired Aidan while disapproving of his Irish Easter reckoning, so his portrait is affectionate but shaped by later debate.

## Significance
Lindisfarne became the chief church of Northumbria and a centre of learning, later associated with Cuthbert and with the Lindisfarne Gospels. Aidan's mission, rather than Rome's, was the main route by which the northern English were converted.
