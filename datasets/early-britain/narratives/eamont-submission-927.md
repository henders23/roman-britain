## Summary
On 12 July 927, soon after he took York, Æthelstan met the kings of the north and west at Eamont, near Penrith, where they made peace with him under oath and accepted his overlordship.

## Detail
The meeting is recorded in one version of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, the northern 'D' manuscript, which enters it under 926 straight after the death of Sihtric, king of York; Irish annals date Sihtric's death to 927, so the meeting is placed in 927. D names Hywel, king of the West Welsh, Constantine, king of the Scots, Owain, king of the people of Gwent, and Ealdred son of Eadwulf from Bamburgh, and says they made peace 'with pledge and oaths' at Eamont on 12 July and renounced idolatry. The names raise questions: 'West Welsh' usually means the Cornish, but many scholars take this Hywel to be Hywel Dda of south-west Wales, and the Owain of Gwent may be a slip for Owain of Strathclyde (the Cumbrians), whom William of Malmesbury names in the twelfth century. William also places part of the meeting at Dacre, a short distance west of the river, where he says Æthelstan stood sponsor at the baptism of Constantine's son; his account was written to glorify Æthelstan and is treated with caution. Eamont lay on the frontier between Northumbria and Cumbria, and the meeting may have been held near the crossing at Eamont Bridge, where prehistoric earthworks such as Mayburgh Henge have been suggested as a setting, though nothing has been found to prove it. The map therefore draws a halo covering Eamont Bridge and Dacre.

## Significance
With York in his hands and the other rulers of Britain bound to him, Æthelstan was the first king to rule all the English kingdoms, and his charters soon called him 'king of the English' and, at times, 'king of all Britain', a style also used on his coins. Many historians treat 927 as the moment a single English kingdom was formed, though that is a judgement on what followed: York was lost again after his death in 939 and only finally secured in 954.
