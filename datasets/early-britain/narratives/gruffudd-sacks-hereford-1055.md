## Summary
On 24 October 1055 Gruffudd ap Llywelyn, king of Gwynedd and Powys, and the exiled English earl Ælfgar routed the army of Earl Ralph outside Hereford, then plundered and burned the town and its new minster.

## Detail
Gruffudd became king of Gwynedd and Powys in 1039 and almost at once beat a Mercian army at Rhyd-y-groes on the Severn. In 1055 he killed his southern rival Gruffudd ap Rhydderch and took Deheubarth, and in the same year Ælfgar, son of Earl Leofric of Mercia, was outlawed, went to Ireland for ships and men, and joined him. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle (most fully in the C manuscript, with shorter notices in D and E) says that Earl Ralph, King Edward's nephew, gathered an army at Hereford; before a spear was thrown the English fled, because they had been made to fight on horseback, and the raiders burned the town and the minster that Bishop Æthelstan had built, stripped it of relics and treasures, killed many people and carried others off. John of Worcester, writing in the early 12th century from a version of the Chronicle, gives the battle as two miles from the city on 24 October, blames the flight on Ralph's French and Normans, and adds that seven canons defending the church doors were killed. The Welsh chronicle Brut y Tywysogion also records the attack. Earl Harold then led an English army to the border, peace was made, and Ælfgar was restored to his earldom. The date and the town are secure; the map pins Hereford at the cathedral, which stands on the site of the minster that was burned.

## Significance
Hereford marks the height of Gruffudd's power. By about 1057 he was accepted as king over the whole of Wales, the only ruler ever to achieve that,. His success did not last: Earl Harold and his brother Tostig invaded by land and sea in 1063, and in August that year Gruffudd was killed by his own men (an Irish annal names Cynan ab Iago) and his head was sent to Harold. Wales then broke apart again into separate kingdoms, which is why historians often treat his death, rather than the Norman conquest, as the start of a new period in Welsh history.
