## Summary
Ipswich grew from a 7th-century landing place on the River Orwell into an 8th-century trading town, Gipeswic, where Ipswich ware, England's first post-Roman wheel-turned and kiln-fired pottery, was made.

## Detail
The Ipswich excavation programme begun in 1974, now published in full by Keith Wade (2025), together with Christopher Scull's report on the Boss Hall and Buttermarket cemeteries (2009), shows how the town developed. In the 7th century there was a small riverside area of occasional occupation, with cemeteries near it. The Buttermarket burials include some with continental-style goods. In the 8th century the settlement grew to some 50 hectares, with streets, buildings and a pottery industry in the north-east quarter around Carr Street and Cox Lane. Ipswich ware was turned on a slow wheel and fired in kilns. Paul Blinkhorn's study of it (2012) moved the start of production from the mid 7th century to about 720 and dated its end to about 850. It is found across East Anglia and as far as Kent, Yorkshire and London. Part of the Middle and Late Saxon town off Cox Lane is protected as a scheduled monument. The pots' shapes and the kiln technology resemble continental, especially Frisian, wares. Whether migrant potters brought the craft is debated.

## Significance
Ipswich is the East Anglian counterpart of Lundenwic and Hamwic, and unlike them it carried on as a town without a break. Ipswich ware is one of the clearest signs of specialised production and regional trade in Middle Saxon England.
