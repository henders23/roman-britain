## Summary
Wallingford, on the Thames, was one of the fortified towns (burhs) of Wessex listed in the Burghal Hidage; its earth rampart still survives around much of the town.

## Detail
The Burghal Hidage is a short administrative document listing the burhs of Wessex with the number of hides assigned to each, on the principle that each hide supplied one man and four men held a pole of wall. Wallingford is given 2,400 hides, one of the largest assessments, close to the Domesday figure for the whole of Berkshire. The list survives in later copies and is usually dated 914–919, under Edward the Elder, though it has been argued that it reflects arrangements made soon after Edington. The defences themselves are generally thought to have been built under Alfred in the 880s or 890s, but no written source records their construction, so the map gives a window from 878 to 919. The town forms a large rectangle beside the river crossing, with a bank and ditch that survives best on the west side and is scheduled. Excavations by the Burh to Borough project (2001–2010) studied the defences, streets and later castle.

## Significance
Wallingford is one of the best-preserved examples of the burh system by which Alfred and his successors defended Wessex and organised towns, markets and mints. It shows how a defensive plan became a lasting town.
