## Summary
Gildas, writing in the sixth century, says that the Britons won a victory over the Saxons at the siege of Mount Badon, the last and greatest of a series of British successes, which was followed by a long peace from foreign wars. Neither its date nor its place is known.

## Detail
The only near-contemporary source is a single sentence of Gildas's De excidio Britanniae (chapter 26). After describing the British recovery under Ambrosius Aurelianus, Gildas says that victory went now to one side and now to the other until the year of the siege of Mount Badon, and that this was the year of his own birth, with forty-four years and one month since elapsed. The Latin is ambiguous: it can mean that Badon was fought forty-four years before Gildas wrote, or forty-four years after some earlier point such as the coming of the Saxons. Since Gildas is usually thought to have written between about 500 and 550, Badon is placed somewhere between about 480 and 520; the Annales Cambriae give a year equivalent to 516 or 518, but that entry was compiled in the 10th century and probably derives from earlier texts rather than independent knowledge. Gildas does not say who commanded the Britons. He gives no location, and many sites have been proposed by their names or position, among them the hills around Bath (Bathampton Down), Liddington Castle above Badbury in Wiltshire and Badbury Rings in Dorset; none has produced archaeological evidence of a battle. The map therefore shows a large halo over southern Britain, which marks where most proposals lie rather than where the battle was fought.

## Significance
For Gildas, Badon ended the first phase of conflict and began a generation of peace, and historians often take it to explain why English expansion seems to have paused in the early sixth century. That is an inference from one ambiguous sentence and from patterns in the archaeology, not a recorded fact.
