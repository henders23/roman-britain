## Summary
Zosimus, a Greek historian writing about a century later, reports that in 410 the emperor Honorius wrote to the cities of Britain telling them to look to their own defence.

## Detail
The report is a single clause in book 6 of Zosimus's New History, in a passage otherwise about Honorius's troubles in Italy, where Alaric's Goths sacked Rome in the same year. Zosimus had earlier described how the Britons threw off Roman officials and defended themselves after Constantine III took troops to Gaul. The clause has often been treated as the formal end of Roman rule in Britain, and is known in modern writing as the 'Rescript of Honorius', though no text of any letter survives. Because the Greek names for Britain and Bruttium, the toe of Italy, differ by only a few letters and the sentence sits among Italian events, some scholars, including Philip Bartholomew, have suggested it concerned Bruttium; E. A. Thompson and others defend the reading 'Britain'. The pack therefore records the event with low certainty and as a halo over the province, since letters to its cities would not belong to one place.

## Significance
Whatever its original meaning, the passage is the nearest thing to a written date for the end of Roman government in Britain. The debate over it shows how thin the written evidence for this turning point is.
