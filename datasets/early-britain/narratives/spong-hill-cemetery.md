## Summary
Spong Hill, south of North Elmham in Norfolk, is the largest early Anglo-Saxon cemetery yet excavated: more than two thousand cremation burials in pottery urns and 57 inhumation graves, used from the early decades of the fifth century to about the middle of the sixth.

## Detail
Urns had been found on the hill before modern excavation (one reached Norwich Castle Museum in 1954), and the Norfolk Archaeological Unit, directed by Catherine Hills, excavated the whole cemetery between 1972 and 1984, with annual seasons until 1981 and a final phase in 1984. The results were published in a series of East Anglian Archaeology catalogues from 1977 onwards: the cremations in several volumes and the inhumations in Part III (1984). The total of cremations is given as 2,259 in the early reports and as over 2,500 in later ones, depending on how fragmentary deposits are counted. The urns, often stamped and decorated, contained burnt bone with personal objects such as combs and brooches, and close parallels in cemeteries in northern Germany, such as Issendorf and Schmalstede, link the community with the North Sea coast of the Continent; one urn lid modelled as a seated figure, the 'Spong Man', is unique. In Spong Hill IX (2013) Hills and Sam Lucy combined correspondence analysis of the urns and grave goods with the wider dating of comparable objects to produce a phased chronology. They conclude that the cemetery is largely fifth-century, that its earliest burials belong to the early decades of that century, and that the inhumations belong to a later phase. Cremations cannot be precisely dated by radiocarbon, so the start date rests on typology and is approximate. The site is located exactly, so it is drawn as a pin.

## Significance
Spong Hill gives the clearest picture of a large community in eastern England burying its dead in a new, North Sea way within a generation or so of the end of Roman rule. Its revised dating supports the view that this began earlier in the fifth century than Bede's date for the coming of the Saxons, which is an inference from the archaeology rather than a written record.
