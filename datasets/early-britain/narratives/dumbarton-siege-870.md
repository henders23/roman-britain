## Summary
In 870 Scandinavian kings from Dublin besieged Alt Clut, the rock fortress of the Britons on the Clyde, for four months and destroyed it. The fall ended the oldest British stronghold in the north.

## Detail
The Annals of Ulster record that Amlaíb and Ímar, two kings of the Northmen, laid siege to Ail Cluaithe and at the end of four months destroyed and plundered it; another annal account adds that the defenders gave in when their water supply failed. The next year the annals report the two kings returning to Dublin with two hundred ships and a great haul of captives, English, British and Pictish. The Welsh annals separately note that the fortress of Alt Clut was broken by the heathens. The siege was unusually long by the standards of Viking warfare in Britain and Ireland.

The rock itself is a volcanic plug at the meeting of the Leven and the Clyde, later crowned by a medieval and Georgian castle. Leslie Alcock's excavation of 1974–75 (merged into this row) found an early medieval timber-and-rubble rampart above the isthmus, with sherds of 6th-century Mediterranean amphorae and Germanic glass. After 870 the British kings appear centred further up the Clyde around Govan, and their kingdom becomes known as Strathclyde, though how quickly that shift happened is debated.

## Significance
The fall of Alt Clut marks the moment when Scandinavian power reached deep into northern Britain and reshaped its kingdoms. It is also one of the few early medieval sieges in Britain that both the written record and an excavated site can speak to.
