## Summary
In 597 a group of monks sent from Rome by Pope Gregory I and led by Augustine arrived in Kent and were allowed by King Æthelberht to settle in Canterbury.

## Detail
Bede, writing in 731, says the party of about forty landed on the Isle of Thanet, met the king in the open air and was then given a house in Canterbury. There, he says, they first worshipped in an old church to the east of the city dedicated to St Martin, which the king's Frankish Christian wife, Bertha, already used. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle also enters Augustine's arrival under 597. The mission went on to found a cathedral in the city and a monastery outside its walls, later called St Augustine's Abbey. Bede drew on letters and traditions preserved at Canterbury, so the outline is well founded, but details such as the meeting on Thanet come to us only through him. Whether today's St Martin's church is the building Bede meant is likely rather than proven, although it contains Roman-period brickwork. The cathedral, the abbey ruins and St Martin's together form a World Heritage Site, inscribed in 1988.

## Significance
The mission linked the English kingdoms to the church of Rome and made Canterbury the seat of the senior English archbishop. Kent was not the first place in Britain with Christians, but this is the conversion that Bede placed at the centre of his story.
