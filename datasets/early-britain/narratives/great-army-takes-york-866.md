## Summary
In late 866 the Viking 'great army', which had wintered in East Anglia, crossed the Humber and took York. When the Northumbrian kings Osberht and Ælla tried to win it back on 21 March 867, both were killed.

## Detail
The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, under 867, and Asser's Life of King Alfred, written in 893, tell the same story: the army moved from East Anglia to York, the Northumbrians were divided because they had deposed Osberht and accepted Ælla, a king not of royal birth, and only late in the year did the two rivals combine. They forced their way into the city but were heavily defeated, and both kings died with many of their men; the survivors made peace with the Vikings. Because the Chronicle's year began in the autumn, its annal for 867 places the capture in late 866. The day of the capture, 1 November (the feast of All Saints), and the date of the counter-attack, 21 March 867, come from northern tradition preserved in the twelfth-century Historia regum attributed to Symeon of Durham, as discussed by Alfred Smyth; some modern accounts give 21 November instead, so the map gives the month only. Archaeology at York, especially the Coppergate excavations, shows the city growing into a busy Scandinavian town in the following decades, but nothing dated securely to the capture itself. The later Scandinavian story that Ælla was put to death by the 'blood eagle' is saga tradition and is excluded.

## Significance
Taking York gave the army a base in the richest city of the north, and within a few years it had overthrown or reduced East Anglia and Mercia as well, leaving Wessex as the only English kingdom standing. York became the centre of a Scandinavian kingdom that lasted, with interruptions, until 954. Historians commonly treat 866–7 as the point at which Viking activity in England turned from raiding to conquest and settlement.
