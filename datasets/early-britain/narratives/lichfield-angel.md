## Summary
In 2003 excavation under the nave of Lichfield Cathedral found three fragments of a carved and painted limestone panel of about 800, showing an angel. It is thought to come from a shrine of St Chad.

## Detail
The fragments were found by the cathedral archaeologist Warwick Rodwell in 2003, during excavation ahead of a new platform in the nave. That discovery is merged into this row. The same dig found the remains of earlier, pre-Norman churches on the site. The panel is of Ancaster limestone from Lincolnshire. It shows a winged angel raising his right hand in blessing and holding a leafy sceptre in his left. Unusually, traces of the original paint survive, with signs that the halo may have been gilded. Its full publication by Rodwell, Hawkes, Howe and Cramp (2008) shows that it formed half of one end of a hollow box with a low sloping lid. The date of about 800 comes from comparison with other Mercian sculpture and manuscripts, not from scientific dating. The angel is usually identified as Gabriel, with the Virgin on a lost matching piece, as part of an Annunciation. The box is taken to be a shrine chest for St Chad, Lichfield's first bishop, who died in 672. Both identifications are widely accepted but remain interpretation.

## Significance
The angel is one of the finest pieces of Anglo-Saxon sculpture, and its surviving paint shows how brightly coloured such carving once was. It is direct evidence of the cult of St Chad at Lichfield, the seat of the Mercian bishops.
