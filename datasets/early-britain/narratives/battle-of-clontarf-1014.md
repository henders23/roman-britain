## Summary
In 1014, at Clontarf on the north shore of Dublin Bay, the army of Brian Bóruma defeated the men of Leinster and the Norse of Dublin and their overseas allies, but Brian, his son Murchad and his grandson Tairdelbach were all killed.

## Detail
The earliest records are the Irish annals. The Annals of Ulster and the Annals of Inisfallen, both kept close to the time, describe Brian, whom the Ulster annalist calls king of Ireland, leading an army to Dublin, a hard-fought battle in which the Leinstermen and the foreigners were routed with great slaughter, and the deaths of Brian, Murchad and Tairdelbach along with the Leinster king Máel Mórda; Sigurd, earl of Orkney, is also among the dead in Irish and Norse tradition. Neither of these early annals gives the day or names the field. The date of Good Friday, 23 April, comes from Cogadh Gáedel re Gallaib ('The War of the Irish against the Foreigners'), a vivid prose history written in the early 12th century, probably for Brian's great-grandson Muirchertach Ua Briain, which casts the battle as the climax of a long national struggle against the Vikings; the 13th-century Icelandic Njáls saga tells a similar story, with Brian refusing to fight on Good Friday and being cut down in his camp by the Manx Viking Bróðir. The name Clontarf, and an early king-list's 'battle of the weir of Clontarf', place the fighting north of the Liffey, probably around the mouth of the Tolka and along the Clontarf shore, but the exact ground is unknown and nothing has been found by archaeology, so the map shows a small halo rather than a pin. Dublin's own king, Sigtrygg Silkbeard, did not fight and kept his throne.

## Significance
Clontarf ended Brian's attempt to make the kingship of Ireland a reality for his own dynasty: with his son and grandson dead, power passed back to rival kings for a generation. It did not end Norse Dublin, which stayed a Scandinavian town under its own kings for much of the next century and a half. Since the 1970s historians have usually treated the battle as a war over Brian's overkingship, Leinster and Dublin resisting him with Scandinavian help, though Seán Duffy has argued that the involvement of Orkney and the Isles made it a wider Viking venture too. Its later fame as the battle that drove out the Vikings owes much to the Cogadh and to modern national memory.
