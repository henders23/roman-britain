## Summary
Dunadd, a rocky hill rising from the flat Moine Mhor in Argyll, was a major stronghold of the Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata between about the 6th and 9th centuries. Carvings on its summit, including a footprint, hint at royal ceremonies.

## Detail
The hill was walled into a stepped series of enclosures, with the citadel on the top. Excavations by Alan Lane and Ewan Campbell in 1980–81, published in 2000 with older finds, recovered moulds, crucibles and debris from making fine brooches, along with imported pottery and glass from Gaul and further afield, showing a wealthy household with long-distance contacts. Finds and radiocarbon place the main occupation from roughly the 6th to the 9th century. The Irish annals mention a siege of Dún Att in 683 and its capture by the Pictish king Óengus son of Fergus in 736.

Near the summit a footprint and a basin are cut into the bedrock, beside a boar carving and an ogham inscription that Historic Environment Scotland dates to the 9th or 10th century. Many scholars link the footprint with king-making, by comparison with later Gaelic inauguration rites, but no early source describes a ceremony here. Whether Dunadd was the single 'capital' of Dál Riata or one of several royal centres is still discussed.

## Significance
Dunadd is the best-excavated royal site of early Gaelic Scotland, and its finds show how a small kingdom drew on Irish, British and continental contacts. It also illustrates how readily a striking monument attracts interpretations, such as inauguration rites, that the evidence suggests but does not prove.
