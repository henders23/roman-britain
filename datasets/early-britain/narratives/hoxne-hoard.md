## Summary
A chest packed with late Roman gold and silver, including almost fifteen thousand coins, was buried near Hoxne in Suffolk some time after 407/408.

## Detail
The hoard was found on 16 November 1992 by Eric Lawes, who was searching with a metal detector for a farmer's lost hammer. He stopped and reported it, and archaeologists from Suffolk County Council excavated it the next day, recording that the objects had been packed in a wooden chest, some in smaller boxes or wrapped in cloth; this merged record of the discovery is why the hoard is so well understood. It holds 14,865 coins, mostly silver siliquae, with gold solidi and some bronze, and about 200 other objects, including a gold body-chain, bracelets, and silver spoons and pepper-pots. The latest coins are two siliquae of the usurper Constantine III, struck in 407–408, so the hoard cannot have been buried earlier. Many of the silver coins are heavily clipped, which some take as a sign that burial came some years later. The coins were published by Peter Guest and the other objects by Catherine Johns.

## Significance
Hoxne is the largest late Roman hoard of gold and silver from Britain and dates from the very end of Roman rule. It shows great private wealth in the province at the moment when imperial coin supply ended.
