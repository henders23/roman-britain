## Summary
In about 285–290 a square coastal fort was built on a promontory in Portsmouth Harbour. Its walls are the best preserved of any Roman fort in northern Europe.

## Detail
The fort's walls, with their projecting D-shaped towers, still stand almost to their full height round about three and a half hectares; a Norman keep and an Augustinian priory church were later built inside them. Barry Cunliffe excavated much of the interior in 1961–72 (merged here). Coins show that the fort was built after about 268, and Cunliffe concluded that it was founded in the time of Carausius, who commanded the Channel fleet from about 285 before seizing power in Britain. Occupation inside was uneven, and at times included families as well as soldiers. Portchester belongs to the chain of forts listed in the Notitia Dignitatum, a register of offices compiled around 400, under the Count of the Saxon Shore. Its Roman name is usually taken to be Portus Adurni, home in the list to a unit of scouts, but this identification is not certain, and Walton Castle in Suffolk has also been suggested.

## Significance
Portchester shows how the late Roman state fortified the coast against seaborne raiders and protected the fleet. Its walls were reused for a thousand years, as a Saxon settlement, a Norman castle and a royal fortress.
