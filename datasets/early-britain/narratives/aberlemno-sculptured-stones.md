## Summary
At Aberlemno in Angus stands one of the finest groups of Pictish sculpture still in the open air, including a churchyard cross-slab carved with a battle scene. The cross-slab is usually dated to the 8th century on grounds of style.

## Detail
By the roadside are an incised symbol stone, the Serpent Stone, carved with a serpent, a double disc and Z-rod, and a mirror and comb, and a tall cross-slab carved in relief. In the churchyard a cross-slab of Old Red Sandstone about 2.3 m high has a cross decorated with interlace and beasts on one face. On the other, below a Z-rod, a triple disc and other symbols, three rows of figures show horsemen and foot soldiers fighting, with a fallen warrior at the bottom right watched by a bird.

None of the stones is dated by inscription or excavation. Their dates come from comparing their decoration with other Pictish and Insular art, which puts the churchyard slab in the 8th century; the symbol stone may be older. Because the riders on one side wear helmets with nose-guards, the scene has often been read as the Picts' victory over the Northumbrians at Dun Nechtain in 685, fought, on the traditional view, a few miles away at Dunnichen. That reading is now less certain, since the battle site is disputed. The roadside cross-slab blew down in a storm in March 2025.

## Significance
The Aberlemno stones are key evidence for Pictish art, belief and warfare, a people with few surviving written sources of their own. The battle scene is one of the few narrative images from early medieval Scotland, whatever event it shows.
