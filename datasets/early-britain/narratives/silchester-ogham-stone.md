## Summary
A small Roman stone column found in a well at Silchester carries an ogham inscription, an Irish script, naming a man usually read as Ebicatos or Tebicatos. It is the only ogham stone found east of the Severn.

## Detail
The stone was found upside down at the bottom of a well in Insula IX during the Society of Antiquaries' excavations of 1893. It is a reused greensand baluster from a Roman portico, cut with ogham strokes along one edge, and is now in Reading Museum. The text is read as the name of a man and 'son of the tribe of' a group whose name is lost; earlier editors read Ebicatos, more recent work Tebicatos.

Its date is debated. Ogham stones in Ireland and western Britain are usually placed in the fifth to seventh centuries, and the Silchester stone was long dismissed as an intruder. Fulford, Handley and Clarke re-excavated the well's context and in 2000 argued for an earlier date: timber from the well gave radiocarbon dates suggesting it went out of use around 350–425, and Reading's later excavation of Insula IX found it lay behind a cottage occupied in the fifth century. The window here starts at 410, but a slightly earlier date is possible.

## Significance
The stone suggests that Irish-speaking people lived in Calleva as Roman rule ended, and that the town was still occupied in the fifth century. It is a rare written trace from Britain's least documented period.
