## Summary
In about 1018 Máel Coluim (Malcolm) II, king of Scots, and Owain the Bald, king of the Strathclyde Britons, defeated the Northumbrians in a great battle at Carham on the Tweed.

## Detail
No contemporary source mentions the battle, and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle is silent. It is known from two 12th-century Durham works. The Historia Regum, a chronicle compiled at Durham and attributed to Symeon, records under 1018 a great battle 'at Carrum' between Uhtred son of Waltheof, earl of the Northumbrians, and Malcolm son of Kenneth, king of Scots, who had with him Owain the Bald, king of the Clyde Britons. Symeon's history of the church of Durham (the Libellus de exordio, written early in the 12th century) does not name Uhtred, but adds that a comet had shone over Northumbria for thirty nights as a warning, that the Northumbrian army was almost wiped out, and that Bishop Ealdhun died of grief soon afterwards. The problem is Uhtred: the Chronicle says he was killed on Cnut's orders in 1016. Some historians, following Plummer and Stenton, have therefore moved the battle to 1016; others, including A. A. M. Duncan, keep 1018 and either doubt the date of Uhtred's death or suppose that the English leader was his brother Eadwulf Cudel. A separate Durham tract, De obsessione Dunelmi, says that Eadwulf, afraid of the Scots, surrendered Lothian to them, but it does not mention Carham. Carham itself is a small village on the south bank of the Tweed west of Coldstream; no battlefield has been identified, so the map shows a halo around it.

## Significance
Carham has traditionally been seen as the moment when Lothian, the land between the Tweed and the Forth, passed finally from Northumbria to the kings of Scots, making the Tweed the border. That is an interpretation: an English chronicler claims Lothian was granted to the Scots by King Edgar in 973, and some recent historians argue that the earls of Bamburgh kept a hold there until the late 11th century. What is clear is that the battle broke Northumbrian power north of the Tees for a time and shows the Scots and Strathclyde Britons acting together against the English.
