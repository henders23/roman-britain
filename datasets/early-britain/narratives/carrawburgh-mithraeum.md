## Summary
Early in the 3rd century soldiers built a small temple to Mithras outside the fort of Brocolitia (Carrawburgh) on Hadrian's Wall. It is the best preserved mithraeum on the frontier.

## Detail
The temple was found in the dry summer of 1949 and excavated in 1950 by I. A. Richmond and J. P. Gillam (merged here). Waterlogging preserved timbers, and the excavators traced several building phases, beginning with a small building in the early 3rd century that was later lengthened and refitted. At its north end stood three altars to Mithras (RIB 1544–1546), set up by prefects of the First Cohort of Batavians, the fort's garrison; one shows Mithras with a radiate crown, cut through so that a lamp behind could shine through it. The altars are now in the Great North Museum: Hancock, and replicas stand on the site. In the 4th century the cult images were damaged and the temple abandoned, which has often been read as Christian hostility, though that is an interpretation. The temple lies about 73 metres from the fort's south-west corner; the map pin sits on the fort. Hadrian's Wall itself is excluded from the map as a linear monument.

## Significance
Carrawburgh shows how an eastern mystery cult was practised by officers of a garrison recruited from the Rhine delta, on the edge of the empire. With the nearby shrines of Coventina and the nymphs, it shows the variety of religion around a single frontier fort.
