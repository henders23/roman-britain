## Summary
On 15 November 655 Oswiu of Northumbria defeated and killed Penda of Mercia beside a river Bede calls the Winwaed, in the district of Loidis around Leeds. Penda's army broke up, and many of the men who fled drowned in the flooded river.

## Detail
Bede, who finished his Ecclesiastical History in 731, gives the fullest account (III.24). Penda had raided Northumbria for years. Oswiu first tried to buy him off, and when that failed he vowed that if he won he would give his infant daughter Ælfflæd to the religious life and twelve small estates for monasteries. Bede says Oswiu's much smaller army won, that Penda and nearly all of his thirty royal commanders were killed, among them Æthelhere of the East Angles, and that more of the fleeing men drowned in the swollen Winwaed than died in the fighting. He dates the end of the war to 15 November in the thirteenth year of Oswiu's reign, in the district of Loidis. The Welsh Historia Brittonum, compiled in 829–30 (ch. 64–65), tells the story from the British side. It says Oswiu killed Penda 'in the field of Gai', calls the battle the 'slaughter of the field of Gai' (Strages Gai Campi), and says the British kings who had marched with Penda died there, except Cadafael of Gwynedd, who slipped away the night before. The Welsh annals list the slaughter and Penda's death a year or two later. 655 is the usual date, although some historians, D. P. Kirby among them, allow 656, depending on when Bede began his year. Philip Dunshea has pointed out that Bede's wording dates the end of the campaign, so the battle could have come earlier and been fought further from Leeds. The river has never been identified. Cock Beck east of Leeds, the Aire and the Went north of Doncaster (proposed by Andrew Breeze in 2004) have all been suggested, so the map shows a halo over the Leeds district rather than a battlefield.

## Significance
Penda was the last great pagan king among the English, and with his death Oswiu became overlord of Mercia and much of southern England. Northern Mercia was ruled directly from Northumbria for about three years, and southern Mercia went to Penda's Christian son Peada, which opened the kingdom to missionaries; Diuma became its first bishop. Mercian independence came back quickly when Wulfhere was made king in 658. Historians generally see the battle as the high point of Northumbrian power and as decisive for the conversion of the Midlands, although that is an interpretation of Bede's narrative.
