## Summary
In 2003 archaeologists found an undisturbed burial chamber at Prittlewell, Southend, where a high-status man had been buried around 580–605 with feasting gear, weapons and two small gold-foil crosses.

## Detail
Museum of London Archaeology excavated the site at Priory Crescent ahead of road widening; Anglo-Saxon graves had been found there when the road was built in 1923. The timber chamber had collapsed, but the stains in the soil and careful recording allowed its layout to be reconstructed: a coffin, a lyre, a folding stool, drinking horns, a Byzantine flagon, a hanging bowl, a gold buckle and two Frankish gold coins. The crosses lay where the man's eyes would have been. Radiocarbon dates from horn and wood fall between about 575 and 605, and the coins cannot be earlier than about 580. The full report was published by MOLA in 2019 and the finds are displayed at Southend Central Museum. The crosses suggest Christian belief, although the other goods follow older burial customs. The man is usually thought to be a member of the East Saxon ruling family; an early suggestion that he was King Sæberht, who died about 616, fits the radiocarbon dates poorly.

## Significance
Prittlewell is the earliest dated princely burial in England and the only one of its rank excavated to modern standards since Sutton Hoo. It shows Christian symbols in Essex before or around the time of Augustine's mission.
