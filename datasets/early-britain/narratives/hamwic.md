## Summary
Hamwic was a planned trading and craft town of the West Saxons on the River Itchen, in the St Mary's district of modern Southampton, occupied from about 700 to the mid 9th century.

## Detail
Excavations since 1946, published by Morton (1992) and, for the large Six Dials site, by Andrews (1997), have mapped a settlement of at least 42 hectares. It had a regular grid of gravelled streets, closely packed timber buildings, rubbish pits and wells, and evidence of iron, bone, textile and glass working. Imported pottery and glass point to links with northern France and the Low Countries, and many silver sceattas were found, some of types thought to have been struck locally. The finds and coins support a foundation about 700, on a new site away from the Roman settlement at Bitterne (Clausentum), although an earlier start is possible. Written sources are few. Willibald's Life, written down in the 8th century, mentions a market called Hamwih near the mouth of the Hamble, where he sailed for the Continent in 720. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle names Hamtun under 840, when Ealdorman Wulfheard defeated a Viking fleet there. By the later 9th century the town was in decline. That its planned layout reflects a royal foundation, perhaps under King Ine, is a common interpretation.

## Significance
Hamwic is one of the best-excavated Middle Saxon towns in Europe and is central to the debate on how early English kings drew wealth from trade. Its collection of finds is a key reference for the period.
