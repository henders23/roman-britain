## Summary
All Saints' at Brixworth, Northamptonshire, is one of the largest Anglo-Saxon churches still standing, an aisled basilica whose arches are turned in reused Roman brick.

## Detail
Most of the nave, with its blocked arcades of Roman brick and tile, and the lower stage of the west tower survive from the Anglo-Saxon church. The bricks were taken from one or more Roman buildings nearby. The Historic England list entry records the building as 7th century, following a tradition first written down in the 12th century by Hugh Candidus of Peterborough: that Cuthbald, abbot of Medeshamstede (Peterborough) from about 675, founded monasteries at Brixworth and elsewhere. The long research programme begun in 1972 and published by Parsons and Sutherland (2013) changed the picture. Radiocarbon dates from charcoal in the construction mortar point to the later 8th century for the main body of the church, with the tower, stair turret and polygonal apse added before about 900. Eric Fernie had already argued from continental parallels for 800–860. The row therefore gives a window from the traditional foundation to the radiocarbon date. Whether an earlier church of Cuthbald's time stood on the site, and how the building relates to Mercian kings such as Offa, are still open questions.

## Significance
Brixworth shows how much architectural ambition there was in Mercia, on a scale close to continental churches. It is also a case where scientific dating has challenged a long-held date drawn from a late chronicle.
