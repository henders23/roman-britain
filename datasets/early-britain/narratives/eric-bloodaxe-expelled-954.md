## Summary
In 954 the Northumbrians drove out Eric, the last independent Scandinavian king of York, and the English king Eadred took over Northumbria. A thirteenth-century chronicle adds that Eric was then betrayed and killed at Stainmore.

## Detail
The D and E versions of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle give the event a single sentence under 954: the Northumbrians drove out Eric and Eadred succeeded to the kingdom of the Northumbrians. They give no place, so the map draws a halo over the kingdom of York rather than a point. Eric had ruled York briefly around 947–8 and again from about 952, competing with Olaf Sihtricson and with English kings who claimed the north. Roger of Wendover, writing at St Albans in the early thirteenth century and probably drawing on lost northern annals, says Eric was betrayed by Oswulf, the lord of Bamburgh, and killed by one Maccus 'in a lonely place called Stainmore', the high pass over the Pennines where the A66 now runs; Oswulf then ruled Northumbria as Eadred's earl. Because Roger is late, the Stainmore killing is reported here but not mapped, and a local tradition linking the Rey Cross on Stainmore to Eric's grave found no support when the cross was excavated in 1990. Since the late nineteenth and early twentieth century Eric of York has usually been identified with Eiríkr blóðøx, Eric Bloodaxe, son of Harald Finehair of Norway, as Old Norse sagas and poems suggest; in 2004 Clare Downham argued that the identification rests on weak evidence, while other scholars still defend it.

## Significance
After 954 York was never again ruled by an independent king, and Northumbria was governed for the West Saxon king by earls; from this point one king ruled the whole of England. Historians therefore often treat 954 rather than 927 as the end of the long process by which the kings of Wessex became kings of the English, although the north kept its own laws, customs and Scandinavian character for long afterwards.
