## Summary
Around 905–910 a lead chest holding about 40 kg of silver was buried by the River Ribble at Cuerdale in Lancashire. It is the largest Viking silver hoard known from Britain and Ireland.

## Detail
Workmen repairing the Ribble embankment found the hoard in May 1840 (a merged row). It held around 7,000 coins and more than 1,000 pieces of ingots, ornaments and hack-silver, with some bone pins thought to have fastened cloth bags. The coins include Anglo-Saxon, Viking issues from York and East Anglia, Carolingian and Islamic pieces, and the latest among them date the burial to about 905–910. Most of the hoard was registered by the British Museum in 1841 (1841,0711.1–741), and parts went to other collections, including the Ashmolean. The find is published in full by James Graham-Campbell (2011). Why so much silver was buried at a crossing of the Ribble is interpreted rather than known: a common view links it to the Norse expelled from Dublin in 902, or to a war-chest for a campaign, but the hoard itself cannot say whose it was.

## Significance
Cuerdale shows the scale of silver wealth moving through the Irish Sea world and the mix of coinage in use. It is also a key dated group for Viking-age coins and metalwork.
