## Summary
In a villa in the Darent valley, painted plaster from an upper room shows figures praying with raised arms and chi-rho monograms, evidence for Christian worship in a private house.

## Detail
Lullingstone villa was excavated by G. W. Meates from 1949. Fragments of painted plaster that had fallen from a room above the cellar were painstakingly reassembled. They show a row of figures standing between painted columns with their arms raised in the early Christian posture of prayer, and wreathed chi-rho monograms on other walls; these are now in the British Museum (1967,0407.1). The paintings belong to a late phase of the villa and are dated from its building sequence and style to the second half of the 4th century. The rooms, with what appears to be an ante-room, are read as a house church used by the household and perhaps neighbours. That reading is widely accepted but remains an interpretation. Earlier in the villa's life, a room below had been used for pagan rites, and the villa was abandoned after a fire in the 5th century.

## Significance
Lullingstone gives rare, direct evidence of Christian worship inside a Romano-British house. It shows how the new religion could take root among the rural elite.
