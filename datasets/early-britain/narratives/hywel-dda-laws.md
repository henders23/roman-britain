## Summary
Medieval Welsh law was known as the law of Hywel, after Hywel Dda (Hywel the Good), who ruled most of Wales and died in 950. The lawbooks say he had the laws reviewed at an assembly at Whitland, but they survive only from the 13th century.

## Detail
Hywel ap Cadell built up rule over much of Wales in the first half of the 10th century, visited Rome in 928, and attended the courts of English kings; the Welsh annals record his death in 950 as king of the Britons. The prologues of the Welsh lawbooks say that he summoned men from every district of his realm to the White House on the Taf, in Dyfed, where the laws were examined, some kept, some changed and some abolished, and the result written down.

No law text survives from Hywel's time. About forty manuscripts survive from about 1230 to 1500, in Welsh and Latin, and they differ considerably, reflecting centuries of change by later lawyers. The earliest is Peniarth MS 28 at the National Library of Wales, a small Latin copy of about the mid-13th century, probably made in south-west Wales, with drawings of the king and his court officers. Historians generally accept that Hywel's name was attached to the law as a mark of authority, but how much he actually did, and whether the Whitland assembly happened, cannot be shown. The exact site of the White House is not known.

## Significance
The laws of Hywel are the foundation of medieval Welsh legal and social history and a symbol of Welsh identity today. They also show how a later legal tradition could be credited to a famous king, so the gap between event and evidence must be kept in view.
