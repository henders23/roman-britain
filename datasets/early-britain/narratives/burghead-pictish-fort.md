## Summary
Burghead, on the Moray coast, was the largest known fortified site of early medieval Scotland, a Pictish promontory fort occupied from at least the 6th century until it burned in the 9th or 10th. It is famous for stones carved with bulls.

## Detail
Massive timber-laced ramparts cut off a headland, enclosing an upper and a lower ward. Most of the fort was levelled for the new village and harbour in the early 19th century, when up to thirty slabs incised with bulls were found; only a few survive, in museums and locally. A rock-cut chamber with a pool, the Burghead Well, lies inside the defences and has been suggested as an early Christian baptistery, though its date is not established.

Radiocarbon dates published in the 1980 Proceedings of the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland suggested building could have begun as early as the 3rd or 4th century. Excavations led by Gordon Noble of the University of Aberdeen since 2015 found that much more of the interior survives than was thought, with buildings and hearths, and produced over forty radiocarbon dates. These show occupation from at least the 6th century and destruction by fire in the 9th or 10th, possibly at Scandinavian hands. Its role as a chief centre of the kingdom of Fortriu is an interpretation from its size.

## Significance
Burghead shows that the Picts could build and maintain fortifications on a scale unmatched elsewhere in early medieval Scotland. Its bull carvings are among the most distinctive works of Pictish art, though their meaning is unknown.
