## Summary
Inside an Iron Age hillfort above the Severn at Lydney, a temple to the god Nodens was built with a guest-house and baths. When it was built is still debated.

## Detail
Mortimer and Tessa Wheeler excavated the complex in 1928–9 (merged here) and published it in 1932; the report included a note by J. R. R. Tolkien on the god's name. The god is known from inscriptions: a dedication to Mars Nodons by Flavius Blandinus, an arms instructor (RIB 305), and a lead tablet in which Silvianus, who had lost a ring, asks Nodens to deny health to anyone named Senicianus until it is returned (RIB 306). Votive bronzes include several dogs, which with the long building and the baths have led to the site being read as a healing sanctuary. Wheeler dated the temple to soon after 364–7 from coins under its floors, making it one of the latest pagan temples in Britain. Re-excavation by P. J. Casey and B. Hoffmann in 1980–81 and a restudy of the coins put its start in the second half of the 3rd century, with a 4th-century refurbishment and decline after about 350. The map shows a window covering both views.

## Significance
Lydney shows a native British god worshipped in a large Roman-style sanctuary. The dating dispute matters because it decides whether the temple shows pagan cults still being founded in the later 4th century, when Christianity was the religion of the emperors.
