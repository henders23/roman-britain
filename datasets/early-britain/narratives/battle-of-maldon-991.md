## Summary
In August 991 a Viking army defeated the men of Essex at Maldon on the Blackwater estuary and killed their leader, Ealdorman Byrhtnoth. In the same year the English, on Archbishop Sigeric's advice, paid the raiders 10,000 pounds, the first of a series of such tributes.

## Detail
Versions C, D and E of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle record under 991 that Ipswich was raided and that Byrhtnoth was killed at Maldon very soon afterwards, and that in that year it was first decided to pay tribute to the Danes, 10,000 pounds, as Sigeric advised. Version A, under 993, adds that Olaf (Anlaf) came with 93 ships to Folkestone and then went on to Sandwich, Ipswich and Maldon; the identification of this Olaf with Olaf Tryggvason, later king of Norway, is an inference. The day is not in the Chronicle but in church calendars that commemorate Byrhtnoth: Ely, where he was buried, gives 10 August and calendars from Winchester and Ramsey give 11 August. The Old English poem The Battle of Maldon, which survives only through an eighteenth-century transcript made before the manuscript burned in 1731, describes Byrhtnoth allowing the Vikings to cross a tidal causeway to fight on the mainland and his retainers dying beside him; it is a literary work, and its speeches are not a record. In 1925 E. D. Laborde identified the causeway with the one to Northey Island, east of the town, and the mainland opposite is now a registered battlefield. The identification is widely accepted but not proved, so the map shows a halo over the registered battlefield and the town.

## Significance
Maldon is the best-known English defeat of the renewed Viking attacks of Æthelred II's reign, and the tribute that followed began the large payments, later called Danegeld, that marked the next quarter-century and ended with the Danish conquest of 1016. The poem has shaped how later generations imagined Anglo-Saxon loyalty and heroism, which is a literary legacy rather than evidence for the battle.
