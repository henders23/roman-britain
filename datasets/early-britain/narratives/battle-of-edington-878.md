## Summary
In 878 Alfred of Wessex, having regrouped after a winter in the Somerset marshes, defeated Guthrum's army at Ethandun. Guthrum accepted baptism soon afterwards.

## Detail
The Chronicle and Asser's Life of Alfred, written in 893, tell the same story: in the seventh week after Easter Alfred rode to Egbert's Stone, where the men of Somerset, Wiltshire and part of Hampshire joined him, moved on to Iley Oak, and the next day fought at Ethandun, putting the Viking army to flight and besieging it in its fortress until it gave hostages. Easter fell on 23 March in 878, which places the battle in early to mid May. Ethandun is usually identified with Edington below the scarp of Salisbury Plain in Wiltshire, near the hillfort at Bratton, which suits the likely routes, but no battlefield finds confirm it, and a Somerset Edington on the Polden Hills has also been argued for. The fortress where the army took refuge is likewise unidentified; Chippenham is often suggested. The map therefore shows a halo, not a point.

## Significance
Edington turned Alfred's near-defeat into a settlement with Guthrum that left Wessex independent. Later writers treated it as the founding moment of Alfred's reputation, which is a later reading of the event.
