## Summary
Driven into the Somerset marshes by Guthrum's surprise attack early in 878, Alfred fortified the island of Athelney after Easter and raided the Viking army from it before his victory at Edington.

## Detail
The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, compiled in Alfred's later years, says that after the Viking army seized Chippenham at midwinter Alfred went with a small band into the woods and fens, and that after Easter he built a fortification at Athelney and fought the army from it with the nearest men of Somerset. Asser, writing in 893 and drawing on the Chronicle, adds detail, and later describes Athelney as an island of firm ground in the marshes reached by a causeway, where Alfred founded a monastery. Easter fell on 23 March in 878, and seven weeks later Alfred rode out to gather his army for the battle (battle-of-edington-878). No remains of the 878 work have been certainly identified. Athelney Hill has two low summits; the fort is thought to have stood on the western one and the later abbey on the eastern, where earthworks and finds of Anglo-Saxon date are protected as a scheduled monument. The well-known story of the burnt cakes is a later legend and is excluded from the map.

## Significance
Athelney marks the low point and turning point of Alfred's reign: from here he regrouped and won at Edington. The site became a symbol of his recovery, later reinforced by his monastery there and the jewel found nearby.
