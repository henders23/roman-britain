## Summary
In AD 83 or 84 the army of the governor Agricola defeated a large Caledonian force at a hill Tacitus calls Mons Graupius, somewhere in north-east Scotland. It is the most northerly pitched battle recorded in Roman Britain.

## Detail
Our only account is the biography Tacitus wrote of his father-in-law Agricola. He sets the battle in Agricola's seventh campaigning season, which falls in 83 or 84 depending on when the governorship began. He describes Caledonian warriors drawn up on rising ground, an opening fight carried by auxiliary infantry and cavalry while the legions waited in reserve, and a rout ending in heavy British losses. He gives the British leader Calgacus a famous pre-battle speech, which is Tacitus's own composition, and his casualty figures are not independent counts.

The site has never been found. Tacitus gives no place that can be fixed on a map, and no battlefield finds have been recovered. Scholars have argued from marching camps and terrain: J. K. St Joseph's discovery of a very large camp at Durno, beneath Bennachie near Inverurie, made Bennachie the most popular candidate, while Raedykes and Kempstone Hill near Stonehaven, Duns Dish in Angus and sites further north have their supporters. The halo covers the main north-east candidates rather than choosing one.

## Significance
The battle marks the furthest point of Roman conquest in Britain under the Flavians, and Agricola was recalled soon afterwards. It also shows the limits of a single literary source: the event is accepted, but its place and its details depend on how far we trust Tacitus.
