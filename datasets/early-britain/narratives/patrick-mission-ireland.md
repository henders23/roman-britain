## Summary
Patrick, a Christian Briton from a family of Roman landowners and clergy, was carried off to Ireland as a slave, escaped, and later returned as a bishop to preach there. His own two writings, the Confessio and the Letter to the soldiers of Coroticus, are the only fifth-century texts from Ireland.

## Detail
In the Confessio Patrick says that his father Calpurnius was a deacon and town councillor with a small estate near a place he calls Bannavem Taburniae, which has never been securely identified, and that he was taken captive at about sixteen. After six years tending flocks he escaped, travelled a long way to a ship and returned to his family; later, in a dream, he heard the voice of the people 'of the wood of Foclut, which is near the western sea' calling him back. He returned as a bishop, baptised thousands, ordained clergy and lived among peoples he describes as at the edge of the world. The Letter, written in anger at a British ruler, Coroticus, whose soldiers had killed and enslaved newly baptised converts, confirms the picture. Neither text gives a date or names any other place in Ireland. The dates that appear in older books, arrival in 432 and death in 461 or 493, come from Irish annals compiled centuries later, which seem to have merged Patrick with Palladius, whom Pope Celestine sent as the first bishop to the Christian Irish in 431. Scholars place Patrick's mission somewhere in the fifth century; several, including David Dumville and Thomas Charles-Edwards, favour the second half. Foclut was located by Tírechán in the 7th century near Killala in County Mayo, and the map shows a halo over the north and west of Ireland because that is all his writings and the earliest tradition support.

## Significance
Patrick's writings show a British Christian church sending clergy beyond the empire's frontier, and they are the earliest documents written in Ireland. The later belief that he converted the whole island single-handed is a creation of the 7th-century church of Armagh; historians see his mission as one part of a wider, gradual conversion.
