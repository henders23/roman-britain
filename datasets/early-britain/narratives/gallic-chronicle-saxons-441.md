## Summary
An anonymous chronicle written in southern Gaul and completed in 452 records, under a year equivalent to about 440–441, that the British provinces, long troubled by disasters and upheavals, had been brought under the power of the Saxons.

## Detail
The Gallic Chronicle of 452 is a continuation of Jerome's world chronicle by an unknown writer, probably in Provence, and the British entry is one short line placed among the regnal years of Theodosius II. Mommsen's edition of 1892 assigned it to 441–2; Steven Muhlberger argued that the best manuscripts place it a year earlier, and in modern editions it is usually dated 440–441. A second Gallic chronicle, the Chronicle of 511, has a parallel entry under 440 saying that the Britains, lost to the Romans, passed into the power of the Saxons; it is later and may depend partly on the same tradition. The entry names no place, so the map shows it as a halo over the province. Scholars disagree about how much weight it can bear. Molly Miller argued in 1978 that the date was an artefact of the edition and that the chronicle had been reworked later; Muhlberger defended it as a genuine fifth-century record of how events looked from Gaul, and Michael Jones and John Casey built a chronology of the Saxon settlement on it. Few now take it to mean that the whole island fell in one year: a writer in southern Gaul probably knew most about the parts of Britain facing the Continent, and the archaeology of the west and north shows British societies continuing for generations.

## Significance
The entry is the only near-contemporary statement that Saxons had gained control in Britain, and it sits about a decade earlier than the date Bede later gave for their arrival. How far it describes all of Britain, rather than a region seen from Gaul, is a matter of interpretation.
