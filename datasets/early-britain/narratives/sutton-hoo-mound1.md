## Summary
Under the largest mound at Sutton Hoo, above the River Deben in Suffolk, a man was buried in the early 7th century in a chamber built inside a ship about 27 metres long, with some of the richest grave goods known from early medieval Europe.

## Detail
Basil Brown, working for the landowner Edith Pretty, began opening the mounds in 1938 and reached Mound 1 in May 1939. The ship's timbers had rotted away, leaving the rivets and the shape of the hull in the sand; the chamber was cleared that summer by a team led by Charles Phillips. No body was found, probably because the acid soil dissolved it. The finds include a helmet, a gold buckle, shoulder-clasps and a purse-lid in gold and garnet, silver from the eastern Mediterranean, weapons and a purse of 37 Frankish gold coins. Mrs Pretty gave the whole assemblage to the British Museum, where it is registered as 1939,1010, and Rupert Bruce-Mitford published it in several volumes from 1975. The coins date the burial: it cannot be earlier than the latest coin, and studies of their gold content suggest they had been gathered by around 613, though earlier work preferred the 620s. The burial is often linked to King Rædwald of East Anglia, who died about 624, but no inscription names him, so that remains an interpretation.

## Significance
Mound 1 showed that the rulers of early 7th-century East Anglia could command craftsmanship and long-distance contacts on a scale few had expected. It remains the benchmark against which other princely burials, such as Prittlewell, are compared.
