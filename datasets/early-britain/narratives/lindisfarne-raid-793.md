## Summary
In 793 raiders from the sea attacked the monastery on Lindisfarne, the island community of St Cuthbert. It is the first Viking raid on a major English church that the sources record in detail.

## Detail
The raid is entered under 793 in the northern versions of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle (MSS D and E), which describe portents over Northumbria followed by famine and the destruction of God's church on Lindisfarne by heathen men. The Chronicle's day reads as 8 January, a time of year when sea raiding was unlikely, so historians usually correct it to 8 June; the June date is a scholarly emendation, not a reading of the surviving text. The raid is also independently attested by Alcuin of York, then at Charlemagne's court, who wrote to King Æthelred of Northumbria and to Bishop Higbald and the community soon afterwards. He treated the attack as a judgement on Northumbrian sins, which is his interpretation rather than a report. None of these sources names the raiders' homeland, though they are generally taken to have come from Norway. The community survived and remained on the island for another eighty years.

## Significance
The raid is the conventional start of the Viking Age in England and of the atlas's final phase. What makes it significant is less its scale than the shock it caused, as shown by Alcuin's letters written at the time.
