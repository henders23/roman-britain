## Summary
On 20 May 685 a Pictish army under Bridei son of Beli destroyed the army of King Ecgfrith of Northumbria at a place the Irish annals call Dún Nechtain. Ecgfrith was killed, and Northumbrian power over the Picts collapsed.

## Detail
Bede, writing about forty-five years later, says that Ecgfrith rashly led an army into Pictish territory against his friends' advice, that the enemy feigned flight and drew him into narrow places among inaccessible mountains, and that he fell there with most of his men in the fifteenth year of his reign. The Annals of Ulster give the battle's name, the date of 20 May, and Ecgfrith's death, independently of Bede. The name Nechtanesmere, the 'lake of Nechtan', first appears in the 12th-century Historia Regum attributed to Symeon of Durham.

The site is debated. From the 19th century it was placed at Dunnichen near Forfar, whose early name forms in the Arbroath cartulary derive from Dún Nechtain, and a memorial stands there. In 2006 Alex Woolf pointed out that Dunachton by Loch Insh in Badenoch has the same origin and sits among real mountain passes, which fits Bede better, and that this also bears on where the Pictish kingdom of Fortriu lay. The halo covers both candidates. The battle scene on the Aberlemno churchyard cross-slab has been read as a picture of this battle, but that identification is interpretation.

## Significance
Dun Nechtain ended Northumbrian overlordship north of the Forth; Bede saw it as the start of the decline of Northumbrian power. The argument over its location has reshaped how historians map the Pictish kingdoms.
