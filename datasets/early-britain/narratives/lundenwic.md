## Summary
From the 7th to the 9th century, London's trading town lay outside the old Roman walls, along the Strand and around Covent Garden. Archaeologists call it Lundenwic.

## Detail
Bede, writing in 731 about the mission of 604, describes London as a market for many peoples coming by land and sea. For a long time no such town could be found inside the Roman walls. Since the 1980s excavations to the west have explained why: the Middle Saxon settlement lay along the Strand, from about Trafalgar Square to Aldwych, and covered some 55–60 hectares. The largest site was the Royal Opera House, dug by the Museum of London Archaeology Service between 1989 and 1999 and merged into this row. It produced gravelled streets of the 8th century, timber buildings, debris from metal and jewellery working, and imported pottery. A 9th-century ditch held a hoard of Northumbrian copper-alloy coins in its bank. Coins, pottery and stratigraphy date the occupation from the 7th century to the later 9th. The ditch is usually read as a defence against Viking attack. After Alfred took control of London in 886, settlement moved back inside the walls. The old site later became known as Ealdwic, 'old settlement', the origin of the name Aldwych.

## Significance
Lundenwic shows that the London Bede described was a new, unwalled trading town beside the Roman city, not the Roman city itself. With Hamwic, Ipswich and York, it is one of the main English emporia linking the kingdoms to trade across the North Sea and the Channel.
