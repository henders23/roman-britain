## Summary
Suetonius Paulinus reached London but decided he could not hold it. The rebels then burnt the town and killed the people who had stayed.

## Detail
Tacitus calls Londinium a busy trading town, not a colony. Paulinus hurried there from Wales, judged his force too small to defend it and left, taking with him anyone who could keep up. The rebels then destroyed the town. Tacitus gives a death toll of about 70,000 for London and Verulamium together, a figure historians treat as unreliable.

Excavators across the early city, in the area around Cornhill and Lombard Street, find a destruction horizon of burnt clay and daub with scorched pottery and fused debris. It dates to about AD 60. Writing tablets from the Bloomberg excavations, one of them dated AD 57, show that London was already a busy commercial settlement a few years before the revolt. The atlas follows the common view that the revolt began in AD 60 and marks the date as approximate.

## Significance
The burnt layer is one of the clearest dating markers in London's archaeology. It also shows how young and undefended the town was. London's rapid rebuilding afterwards helped it become the province's main centre.
