## Summary
On 18 October 1016 Cnut defeated Edmund Ironside at Assandun in Essex. The two kings then divided England, and when Edmund died at the end of November Cnut became king of the whole country.

## Detail
The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle (manuscripts C, D and E), written within a few years of the events, describes a year of hard fighting between Cnut and Edmund, who had succeeded his father Æthelred in April 1016. It says Edmund overtook the Danish army at the hill called Assandun in Essex, where Ealdorman Eadric of Mercia fled the field with his men, as the Chronicle says he had done before, and Cnut won. Among the many English leaders killed it names Bishop Eadnoth and Ulfcytel of East Anglia. John of Worcester, writing in the early twelfth century, follows and adds to the Chronicle. The date of 18 October is the generally accepted one, and a late twelfth-century Ely calendar gives the same day for Bishop Eadnoth's death. Afterwards the two kings met at Alney (Ola's island) near Deerhurst in Gloucestershire and agreed to share the kingdom, Edmund keeping Wessex. Edmund died on St Andrew's Day, 30 November 1016, and was buried at Glastonbury, and Cnut was accepted as king of all England in 1017. In 1020, the Chronicle adds, Cnut went to Assandun and had a minster built there for the souls of the men killed in the battle. Where Assandun was is disputed. Ashingdon, near Rochford in south-east Essex, is the long-standing identification, and its hilltop church has been linked to Cnut's minster; Ashdon, near Saffron Walden in north-west Essex, has been argued for on the grounds of the place-name and the armies' likely movements, and the Battlefields Trust is investigating further candidates outside Essex. No battle archaeology has settled the question, so the map shows a halo covering both Essex sites.

## Significance
Assandun decided the war for England: it destroyed much of the English leadership and led directly to Cnut's rule over the whole kingdom from 1017. England became for two decades part of a North Sea empire under a Danish king, a result most historians regard as the culmination of the Viking attacks that had begun again in the 980s.
