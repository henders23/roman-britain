## Summary
In 937 Æthelstan and Edmund defeated an alliance of Olaf of Dublin, Constantine of Alba and the Strathclyde Britons at Brunanburh. Where the battle was fought is still disputed.

## Detail
The Chronicle's entry for 937 in MSS A, B, C and D is a poem celebrating the victory, and the battle is noted in Irish, Welsh and later English chronicles. None locates it clearly. The poem says the defeated Norse fled by ship over Dingesmere to Dublin, which suggests a western coast. Bromborough on the Wirral is the leading candidate because its name derives from Brunanburh, and a Wirral team has reported 10th-century weapon fragments and fittings from fields around Brimstage, Clatterbridge and Storeton. These finds are promising but not yet fully published, and their link to the battle is interpretation. Other proposals include Burnswark in Dumfriesshire, Lanchester in County Durham and several sites in Yorkshire and Lancashire. The map shows a halo on the Wirral only, as the leading hypothesis.

## Significance
Brunanburh confirmed Æthelstan's dominance over the other rulers of Britain. Later writers have treated it as the battle that made England, which is an interpretation of its long-term effects.
