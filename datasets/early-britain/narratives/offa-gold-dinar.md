## Summary
A unique gold coin in the British Museum copies an Islamic dinar of 773–774 but carries the Latin words OFFA REX, naming the Mercian king Offa (757–796).

## Detail
The coin weighs about 4.3 grams. Its die-cutter copied the Arabic of a dinar of the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur closely enough for the model to be identified as struck in AH 157 (AD 773–774). That gives the earliest possible date, and Offa's death in 796 gives the latest. The Latin OFFA REX was set into the copied Arabic upside down relative to it, which suggests the engraver could not read the script. The coin has no mint name. Nineteenth-century writers assumed London, but Canterbury is also possible. It was recorded in Rome, where the Duc de Blacas acquired it, and Adrien de Longpérier read a paper on it to the Numismatic Society of London in November 1841; this discovery history is merged into the row. Why it was made is debated. Gold dinars were the leading currency of the Mediterranean, so it may have been meant for trade. Its appearance in Rome has also suggested a link with the 365 mancuses a year that Offa promised to the papacy. The modern claim that it shows Offa had become a Muslim has no support and is excluded.

## Significance
The dinar is direct evidence that Islamic gold coinage was known and valued in 8th-century England. It also shows Offa presenting himself as a ruler on a European stage, in touch with Rome.
