## Summary
Near Hammerwich in Staffordshire, in the heart of the kingdom of Mercia, someone buried a mass of broken gold and silver war-gear in the mid 7th century; it is the largest such hoard known.

## Detail
The metal detectorist Terry Herbert found the first pieces on 5 July 2009, and further finds came from excavation that year and from later ploughing. Declared Treasure, the hoard was bought jointly by Birmingham Museums and the Potteries Museum and Art Gallery in Stoke-on-Trent. It numbers nearly 4,600 fragments, mostly from sword hilts, a helmet and other fittings, many decorated with garnets and filigree, together with a few Christian objects such as crosses and an inscribed strip. The objects are dated by their style and manufacture, not by coins; the published study of 2019 identifies several phases of making, from the 6th century to around 660, and suggests the hoard was buried about 650–675. The items had been stripped from their weapons and many were bent or broken. Why this was done, and why they were buried, is debated: spoils of battle, tribute, or an offering are all proposed.

## Significance
The hoard shows how much gold was concentrated among the warrior elite of 7th-century England and how far Mercia's power could reach. Its mix of weapon fittings and Christian objects raises questions about warfare and belief in the period.
