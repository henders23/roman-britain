## Summary
Excavation at 16–22 Coppergate in York uncovered 10th-century property plots with timber houses and workshops, preserved in waterlogged ground. They are the best evidence for everyday life in Viking-age York.

## Detail
York Archaeological Trust excavated the site between 1976 and 1981 under Richard Hall (a merged row), recovering some 40,000 finds. Four long plots ran back from the street, laid out around the 930s; they carried post-and-wattle buildings that were replaced later in the century by plank-built structures with semi-basements. Waterlogging preserved wood, leather, textiles, plant remains and insect remains, showing woodturning, metalworking, jewellery-making, leatherworking and textile production, and trade contacts reaching the Baltic and beyond. The phases are dated by dendrochronology, coins and stratigraphy, and the results are published in The Archaeology of York series. The phase dates given here come from that series and have not been checked online. The site is often presented as 'Viking' York, but the finds point to a mixed Anglo-Scandinavian population, so labels of ethnic identity are interpretation.

## Significance
Coppergate transformed understanding of York as a manufacturing and trading town in the 10th century. It also shows how waterlogged urban deposits can preserve what dry sites lose.
