## Summary
A hoard of silver vessels and thin silver plaques, many marked with the chi-rho, was ploughed up inside the Roman town of Durobrivae.

## Detail
The hoard came to light during ploughing within the walled town of Durobrivae, beside the modern village of Water Newton; the British Museum and most accounts give February 1975, though Historic England's list entry gives 1974. It comprises nine silver vessels, including a jug, bowls and a two-handled cup, and a set of triangular silver plaques shaped like leaves, with one small gold disc. Several pieces carry the chi-rho, and some bear Latin inscriptions naming the people who gave them, such as Publianus on a bowl and Anicilla on a plaque. Kenneth Painter published the treasure in 1977. It is dated by style and lettering to the 4th century; when it was buried, and why, is unknown. Its use in Christian worship is an inference from the inscriptions and symbols, and the leaf-shaped plaques resemble votive offerings made at pagan shrines, which suggests how older practice carried over into the new religion.

## Significance
Water Newton is the earliest known group of probable church silver from anywhere in the Roman Empire. It shows an organised, reasonably wealthy Christian community in a Romano-British town.
