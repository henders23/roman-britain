# Picts, Scots and Britons: the making of Alba
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Order: chronological

North of the old Roman frontier, the centuries after Rome saw kingdoms of Picts, of Gaelic-speaking Scots in Dál Riata, and of Britons on the Clyde, with the Northumbrian English pressing from the south. By the eleventh century most of this land had become a single Gaelic kingdom, Alba, the core of later Scotland. This journey asks how that happened, and how much of the familiar story is later legend.

The peoples of the north left few written records of their own, so the journey depends on fortresses, carved stones and the annals kept by Irish and Iona monks, with later chronicles that often tell us more about the time they were written than about the events.

## Stop: dunadd-dal-riata
Dunadd, a rocky hill rising from the flat Moine Mhor in Argyll, was a stronghold of the kings of Dál Riata, the Gaelic kingdom that spanned the sea between Argyll and the north of Ireland. Its slopes were walled into a series of terraced enclosures, and excavation in 1980–81 found moulds and crucibles from making fine brooches, with pottery and glass imported from Gaul and beyond: a wealthy household with long-distance contacts, occupied from roughly the 6th to the 9th century. The Irish annals record a siege of Dún Att in 683 and its capture in 736 by Óengus son of Fergus, king of the Picts. A footprint carved in the rock near the summit is often linked with king-making ceremonies; that is an interpretation, not a record. The story that Fergus Mór brought the Scots from Ireland about 500 comes from much later origin legends and is excluded in the pack.

## Stop: burghead-pictish-fort
On the Moray coast, Burghead was the largest known Pictish fort, its timber-laced ramparts cutting off a headland. Most of it was levelled for a new village and harbour in the early 19th century, when up to thirty slabs carved with bulls were found; only a few survive, and what they meant is unknown. Excavations since 2015 have shown that much more of the interior survives than was thought, and over forty radiocarbon dates show occupation from at least the 6th century until the fort burned in the 9th or 10th, possibly at Scandinavian hands. Its size suggests a chief centre of Fortriu, the most powerful Pictish kingdom, which many historians now place around the Moray Firth rather than further south; that is interpretation, and the argument returns at Dun Nechtain.

## Stop: columba-iona-563
From Iona, Columba's monks reached the Picts as well as the Scots. Columba, an Irish abbot of royal descent, founded the monastery by tradition in 563, on an island that Bede says the Pictish king Bridei gave him, while Irish tradition credits the king of Dál Riata. Adomnán's Life of Columba, written about a century later, describes Columba visiting Bridei at his fortress near the River Ness, which shows Iona's reach into Pictland, though the Life is a book of miracles rather than a chronicle. Most of what we know about the north in this period comes from writers connected with Iona, and many of the earliest annal entries about northern Britain are thought to have been kept there.

## Stop: dun-nechtain-685
Before: From the mid 7th century the Northumbrian kings Oswiu and Ecgfrith were overlords of much of the north, and Bede says the Picts were subject to them. Northumbria set up a bishopric for the Picts at Abercorn on the Forth, and Stephen of Ripon describes Ecgfrith crushing a Pictish rising early in his reign. In 684 Ecgfrith sent an army to raid Ireland, which Bede condemned, and the next year, against his friends' advice, he led his army north against the Picts under their king, Bridei son of Beli.

What happened: On 20 May 685 the Picts destroyed Ecgfrith's army at a place the Irish annals call Dún Nechtain, and the king was killed with most of his men. Bede, writing about forty-five years later, says the enemy feigned flight and drew him into narrow places among inaccessible mountains; the Annals of Ulster give the name and the day independently. Where it was fought is disputed. The traditional site is Dunnichen near Forfar, but Dunachton in Badenoch has the same name and lies among real mountain passes, which fits Bede better, so the map draws a region covering both.

After: Bede says that from this time the hopes and strength of the English began to ebb: the Picts recovered their land, the bishop at Abercorn fled, and some of the Scots and Britons regained their freedom. Ecgfrith's half-brother Aldfrith, educated among the Irish, took the Northumbrian throne and, in Bede's words, restored the kingdom within narrower bounds. The argument over the site has reshaped how historians map the Pictish kingdoms, since a battle in Badenoch would put Fortriu in the north.

## Stop: aberlemno-sculptured-stones
At Aberlemno, in Angus, a carved cross-slab in the churchyard shows warriors in battle. On one face is a cross with interlace and beasts; on the other, below Pictish symbols, three rows of horsemen and foot soldiers fight, and a fallen warrior at the bottom is watched by a bird. The stone is dated to the 8th century by its style, not by inscription or excavation. Because some riders wear helmets with nose-guards, the scene has often been read as Dun Nechtain, on the traditional view fought a few miles away at Dunnichen; others see a more general image, and the dispute over the battle site has weakened the link. Like most Pictish carving, it is powerful evidence that is hard to read. Beside the road stand an older symbol stone and another cross-slab, which blew down in a storm in March 2025.

## Stop: cinaed-mac-ailpin-king-of-picts
Before: By the 9th century the Pictish and Gaelic kingdoms were already entangled: kings such as Óengus son of Fergus, who took Dunadd in 736, and Constantín son of Fergus, who died in 820, seem to have ruled Dál Riata as well as the Picts. Then in 839 the Annals of Ulster record a great defeat by the Vikings in which the king of Fortriu, Eóganán son of Óengus, his brother Bran, Áed son of Boanta and others almost without number were killed. Several rivals seem to have competed for the kingship in the years that followed.

What happened: In about 842 or 843 Cináed mac Ailpín, known in English as Kenneth MacAlpin, became king of the Picts. The record is thin: the Annals of Ulster report his death in 858 as king of the Picts, and the Chronicle of the Kings of Alba, compiled more than a century later, says he had ruled Dál Riata for two years before ruling the Picts for sixteen, raided Northumbria, suffered Danish attacks, moved relics of Columba to a church he had built, and died at the palace of Forteviot in Strathearn. No source says where he became king, so the map draws a halo over southern Pictland, centred on Forteviot, where he died.

After: From the 12th century Scottish writers told his reign as a Scottish conquest of the Picts, and the tale of 'MacAlpin's treason', in which the Pictish nobles were killed at a feast by a trap under their benches, first appears three centuries after his time; both are later legends, and the treason story is excluded in the pack. His heirs were still called kings of the Picts for the rest of the century, and only in 900 do the Annals of Ulster call his grandson Domnall king of Alba. Over the following centuries Gaelic replaced the Pictish language, which leaves almost no written trace, though how fast and why are debated. Historians now see the change from Pictland to Alba as gradual, with Cináed's accession a symbolic starting point rather than its cause.

## Stop: dumbarton-siege-870
In 870 Viking leaders from Dublin besieged Dumbarton Rock, the fortress of the Britons of the Clyde, for four months, took it and carried off captives. The Annals of Ulster name the kings as Amlaíb and Ímar, and another annal account says the defenders gave in when their water supply failed. The next year the two kings returned to Dublin with two hundred ships and a great haul of English, British and Pictish captives. Excavation on the rock in 1974–75 found an early medieval rampart, with sherds of Mediterranean amphorae and Germanic glass showing the stronghold's own long-distance contacts. The kingdom survived, but its old centre had fallen, and its kings next appear further up the Clyde around Govan, ruling the kingdom known as Strathclyde.

## Stop: battle-of-carham-1018
Before: Lothian, between the Tweed and the Forth, had been part of Northumbria since the 7th century, but the kings of Alba had been pushing south: the Chronicle of the Kings of Alba says Edinburgh was abandoned to the Scots in the reign of Indulf, in the 950s or 960s. Máel Coluim (Malcolm) II became king of Alba in 1005, and an early attack on Durham, probably in 1006, was beaten off by Uhtred of Bamburgh, who rose to rule all Northumbria. In England, Cnut's conquest in 1016 brought Uhtred's death and unsettled the north.

What happened: Máel Coluim and his ally Owain the Bald, king of the Strathclyde Britons, defeated the Northumbrians in a great battle at Carham on the Tweed. No contemporary source mentions it; it is known from two 12th-century Durham works, which date it to 1018, describe a comet that shone for thirty nights as a warning, and say the Northumbrian army was almost wiped out. One names Uhtred as the English leader although the Chronicle says he was killed in 1016, so some historians move the battle to 1016 and others suppose that his brother Eadwulf led the English. No battlefield has been identified, so the map shows a halo around the village.

After: Carham has traditionally been seen as the moment Lothian passed for good to the kings of Scots, making the Tweed the border. That is an interpretation: one English chronicler says King Edgar granted Lothian to the Scots in 973, and some historians argue that the earls of Bamburgh kept a hold there until the late 11th century. Cnut came north about 1031 and received Máel Coluim's submission, but the kingdom of Alba, now reaching the Tweed and in time absorbing Strathclyde, was taking the shape of medieval Scotland.
