# Picts, Scots and Britons
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Order: chronological

North of the old Roman frontier, the centuries after Rome saw kingdoms of Picts, of Gaelic-speaking Scots in Dál Riata, and of Britons on the Clyde. They left few written records of their own, so this journey depends on fortresses, carved stones, and the annals kept by monks.

## Stop: dunadd-dal-riata
Dunadd, a rocky hill in Argyll, was a stronghold of the kings of Dál Riata. A footprint carved in the rock is often linked with king-making ceremonies; that is an interpretation, not a record.

## Stop: burghead-pictish-fort
On the Moray coast, Burghead was the largest known Pictish fort. Carved stone bulls found there are among the best-known Pictish images, though what they meant is unknown.

## Stop: columba-iona-563
From Iona, Columba's monks reached the Picts as well as the Scots. Most of what we know about the north in this period comes from writers connected with Iona.

## Stop: dun-nechtain-685
In 685 the Picts defeated and killed King Ecgfrith of Northumbria, ending Northumbrian power north of the Forth. Bede records the battle; where it was fought is disputed, so the map draws a region covering the candidates.

## Stop: aberlemno-sculptured-stones
At Aberlemno, a carved cross-slab shows warriors in battle. Some scholars link the scene with Dun Nechtain; others see a more general image. Like most Pictish carving, it is powerful evidence that is hard to read.

## Stop: dumbarton-siege-870
In 870 Viking leaders from Dublin besieged Dumbarton Rock, the fortress of the Britons of the Clyde, for four months, took it and carried off captives. The kingdom survived, but its old centre had fallen.
