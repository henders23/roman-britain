# Christianity comes again
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: gods-saints-and-the-church

Christians seem to have been meeting in a villa in Kent in the fourth century. So why did missionaries have to come to Britain from Iona and from Rome two centuries later? This journey follows Christianity from Roman Britain to the meeting at Whitby where two missions settled their differences.

## Stop: lullingstone-house-church
In the later fourth century an upper room in the villa at Lullingstone was painted with figures praying and chi-rho monograms. The plaster was found in pieces, fallen into the cellar below, during G. W. Meates's excavations from 1949, and was patiently reassembled; it is now in the British Museum. The figures stand between painted columns with their arms raised in the early Christian posture of prayer. Reading the room and its ante-room as a house church, used by the household and perhaps its neighbours, is an interpretation, though a widely accepted one. It is evidence of Christian worship in a wealthy Roman household. Yet a room beneath had earlier been used for pagan rites, and the villa was abandoned after a fire in the fifth century, as the Roman way of life in Britain came apart.

## Stop: columba-iona-563
Columba, an Irish abbot, crossed to Britain and founded a monastery on Iona, by tradition in 563. The Annals of Ulster give that year, and Adomnán, a later abbot of Iona, places the voyage two years after the battle of Cúl Drebene in Ireland; Bede gives 565 and says the Picts gave him the island, while Irish tradition credits the king of Dál Riata. Ireland had itself been converted partly through Britain, by Patrick and others, so the faith was in a sense coming back. The early monastery lay inside a great earthwork enclosure around the later abbey, and charcoal from a hut on the knoll of Tòrr an Aba is dated to about AD 540–650; that it was Columba's writing cell is an attractive guess, not a fact. Iona became the mother house of churches across Ireland and northern Britain, and its monks would carry the faith further.

## Stop: augustine-canterbury-597
In 597 monks sent from Rome by Pope Gregory and led by Augustine arrived in Kent. Bede says the party of about forty landed on Thanet and met King Æthelberht in the open air. The king allowed them to settle in Canterbury, where they first worshipped in an old church of St Martin that his Frankish Christian wife, Bertha, already used; whether today's St Martin's is that building is likely but not proven. Bede also tells how Augustine met bishops of the Britons and failed to win their help, a sign that the British church and the English kingdoms still stood apart. A second mission, from a different tradition, had begun in the south. In the same year Columba died on Iona.

## Stop: aidan-lindisfarne-635
King Oswald of Northumbria, who had spent his exile among the monks of Iona, asked them for a bishop. A first mission from Kent, under Paulinus, had won over King Edwin, but it collapsed when Edwin was killed in 633. Oswald won the kingdom in 634, and Bede tells how a first, harsh missionary from Iona failed before Aidan came and was given the island of Lindisfarne, within sight of the royal fortress at Bamburgh. Bede describes him walking from village to village, with the king himself acting as his interpreter. Bede gives no year, so 635 is worked out from Oswald's accession and from Aidan's death in 651, and 634 is also possible. The Iona mission now reached the English.

## Stop: synod-of-whitby-664
The two traditions dated Easter differently. Within one royal household the difference was felt: King Oswiu followed Iona's practice while his queen followed Rome's, so the court could keep Easter on different days. In 664 Oswiu held a meeting at Abbess Hild's monastery at Whitby, heard Bishop Colman of Lindisfarne speak for Iona and Wilfrid for Rome, and decided for the Roman method. Bede says he chose Rome because its tradition claimed the authority of St Peter, and that Colman then withdrew to Iona and Ireland. Bede's account of it is vivid, but it was written two generations later, and historians still debate how far the meeting was about church custom and how far about royal politics. A few years later Archbishop Theodore of Canterbury gathered the bishops of the English at Hertford, showing a church that was beginning to act as one.

## Closing
The Christianity of Roman Britain survived among the Britons, but the English kingdoms had to be converted by missions from Iona and from Rome. At Whitby the two met, and Northumbria chose Rome's way. The written story comes almost entirely from Bede, writing in that Roman tradition.
