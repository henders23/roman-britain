# Rome's northern frontier
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: conquest-and-frontiers

How far north did Rome reach, and why did it stop? This journey moves between a lost battlefield, an unfinished fortress and the forts behind the later frontier. The walls themselves are lines the atlas cannot yet draw, so they appear in the pack as excluded rows rather than on the map.

## Stop: mons-graupius-83
Tacitus, the son-in-law of the governor Agricola, describes a great victory at Mons Graupius in the far north. Nobody knows where it was fought. The map draws a region over the candidate sites, and the card names them.

## Stop: inchtuthil-fortress
At Inchtuthil, on the Tay, the army began a full legionary fortress, then dismantled it before it was finished. Unworn coins of AD 86 date the withdrawal. The abandoned fortress is the clearest sign that Rome gave up the far north.

## Stop: vindolanda-tablets
A little later, at Vindolanda, officers and their households wrote letters on thin wooden tablets. They show a frontier that was a community as well as a line of defence: birthday invitations, requests for socks and beer, and reports on the local Britons.

## Stop: hadrian-visit-britain-122
Hadrian's visit is usually linked with the decision to build his Wall. The visit is recorded, but the reasons for the Wall are interpretation, argued over by historians ever since.

## Stop: severus-death-york-211
A century later, Severus campaigned beyond the Wall and died at York. After him, the frontier settled on Hadrian's line for good.

## Closing
Rome reached far into Scotland, won a battle there and began a fortress it never finished. The lasting frontier settled further south, where the Vindolanda letters show an army at home. Even so, emperors kept coming north, and Severus died at York after his own campaigns. The frontier was a choice Rome kept revisiting, not a line it reached once.
