# Rome's northern frontier
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: conquest-and-frontiers

How far north did Rome reach, and why did it stop? This journey moves between a lost battlefield, an unfinished fortress and the forts behind the later frontier. The walls themselves are lines the atlas cannot yet draw, so they appear in the pack as excluded rows rather than on the map.

## Stop: mons-graupius-83
Before: Agricola, governor from 77 or 78, pushed the army north season by season, and his son-in-law Tacitus describes campaigns reaching the Tay and a chain of forts across the narrow neck between Forth and Clyde. Beyond it lay the peoples Tacitus calls Caledonians, who in the end combined against him under several leaders. Tacitus names one of them, Calgacus.

What happened: In Agricola's seventh season, AD 83 or 84 depending on when his governorship began, the two armies met at a hill Tacitus calls Mons Graupius. He describes the Caledonians drawn up on rising ground, auxiliary infantry and cavalry winning the fight while the legions waited in reserve, and a rout with heavy British losses. Tacitus is the only source and is writing in praise of his father-in-law, so Calgacus's famous speech is his own composition and his casualty figures are not counts. Nobody knows where the battle was fought: the map draws a region over the north-eastern candidates, among them Bennachie with the great marching camp at Durno, Raedykes and Kempstone Hill near Stonehaven, and Duns Dish in Angus.

After: Agricola was recalled soon afterwards, and Tacitus says his fleet sailed round the north of Britain, showing that it was an island. The army began a permanent fortress at Inchtuthil, the next stop, so for a short time Rome seems to have meant to hold the far north. Within a few years it gave the idea up; Tacitus later complained that Britain was completely conquered and at once let go.

## Stop: inchtuthil-fortress
At Inchtuthil, on the Tay, the army began a full legionary fortress, then dismantled it before it was finished. Excavations between 1952 and 1965 recovered its whole plan from the timber foundation trenches: barracks, granaries, a hospital, a workshop and a small headquarters, but no commander's house, which had not yet been built. Because nothing was ever built over it, it is the only complete plan of a legionary fortress known anywhere in the empire. The demolition was orderly: timbers were taken out, pottery and glass smashed, and a great hoard of iron nails buried in a pit so that no enemy could reuse them. Unworn coins of AD 86 date the withdrawal, which is usually linked to the transfer of a legion from Britain to the Danube. The abandoned fortress is the clearest sign that Rome gave up the far north.

## Stop: vindolanda-tablets
A little later, at Vindolanda, officers and their households wrote letters on thin wooden tablets. The fort lay on the Stanegate, the road between Carlisle and Corbridge that marked the frontier zone after the pull-back from Scotland, a little south of where Hadrian's Wall would run. Waterlogged layers of successive timber forts preserved the tablets, found from the 1970s onwards and dated by their layers and the officers they name to between the 80s and the 120s, most of them to around 100. They show a frontier that was a community as well as a line of defence: strength reports, accounts and requests for leave beside birthday invitations, requests for socks and beer, and reports on the local Britons. The best known, an invitation from Claudia Severa to Sulpicia Lepidina, the commander's wife, ends in lines that seem to be in Severa's own hand, among the earliest Latin known to have been written by a woman.

## Stop: hadrian-visit-britain-122
Before: After the withdrawal from Scotland the army held the Stanegate line, where the Vindolanda letters were written. Hadrian became emperor in 117, gave up some of his predecessor's conquests in the east and preferred to secure the empire's borders; his biography says that at his accession the Britons could not be kept under Roman control. In 122 he toured the western provinces, and came to Britain after reforming the army in Germany.

What happened: The visit is recorded in one sentence of the Life of Hadrian, a late 4th-century biography: he put right many abuses and was the first to build a wall, eighty miles long, to separate the Romans from the barbarians. It gives no route, no places and no length of stay, so the atlas draws a halo over the north rather than a point. The year comes from a military diploma of 17 July 122 showing that Platorius Nepos, Hadrian's friend, had just taken over as governor; he probably came with the emperor.

After: Nepos built much of the Wall, and his name appears on inscriptions from its milecastles. It ran 80 Roman miles from Wallsend on the Tyne to Bowness-on-Solway, in stone in the east and at first in turf in the west, with small forts every mile, turrets between them and larger forts added on the line. The atlas can draw only points and regions, so the Wall, which is a line, is listed in the pack as an excluded row and is not on the map; the halo here marks the visit, not the Wall. The visit is recorded, but the reasons for the Wall (defence, control of movement and trade, or a show of imperial power) are interpretation, argued over by historians ever since.

## Stop: severus-death-york-211
Before: Under Antoninus Pius, Hadrian's successor, the frontier moved north to a turf wall between Forth and Clyde, then fell back to Hadrian's line within a generation. In the later 2nd century Dio reports trouble from the Caledonians and the Maeatae beyond it. Septimius Severus, who had won the empire in civil war, came to Britain in 208 with his sons Caracalla and Geta and his court.

What happened: A century after Hadrian, Severus campaigned beyond the Wall. Dio, a senator who knew him, says the wars were hard going through forest and bog, with heavy losses and no pitched battle, and that a peace was made which the Britons soon broke. Worn out by illness, Severus died at York on 4 February 211, the first emperor to die in the province; the day is worked out from Dio's figures for his life and reign, and no building in York has been identified as his residence. Marching camps across eastern Scotland, a base at Carpow on the Tay and granaries at South Shields are assigned to these wars, though dating individual camps is debated.

After: His sons made peace, gave up the new conquests and took his ashes to Rome, where Caracalla soon had Geta murdered. After him, the frontier settled on Hadrian's line for good, and the north seems to have been largely quiet for most of the 3rd century. At about this time Britain was divided into two provinces, though whether by Severus or by Caracalla is debated.

## Closing
Rome reached far into Scotland, won a battle there and began a fortress it never finished. The lasting frontier settled further south, where the Vindolanda letters show an army at home. Even so, emperors kept coming north, and Severus died at York after his own campaigns. The frontier was a choice Rome kept revisiting, not a line it reached once.
