# Alfred's kingdom
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: rulers-and-kingdoms

In 878 King Alfred of Wessex was nearly defeated. Within a generation his family ruled much of England. This journey follows the recovery and the kingdom he built: the victory, the learning and the fortified towns, and keeps the famous legend about the cakes off the map. The Alfred Jewel can be dated only to his reign as a whole and the burh at Wallingford to a window of some forty years, so both overlap the events of 878 and stand where they belong in the story, after the victory that made them possible.

## Stop: alfred-athelney-878
After a surprise attack early in 878, Alfred retreated into the Somerset marshes and built a fortification at Athelney. The Chronicle says that Guthrum's army seized the royal estate at Chippenham at midwinter, and that Alfred went with a small band into the woods and fens while many of his people submitted. After Easter he fortified Athelney, an island of firm ground in the Levels, and raided the Viking army from it with the men of Somerset. The Chronicle and his biographer Asser tell the story; no remains of the 878 works have been certainly identified, though the fort is thought to have stood on the western of the island's two low summits. The tale of the burnt cakes appears only much later, so the pack excludes it.

## Stop: battle-of-edington-878
Before: Since the great Viking army landed in 865, Northumbria, East Anglia and much of Mercia had fallen under Viking control, and Alfred had become king of Wessex in 871 in the middle of a year of battles. The attack on Chippenham at midwinter 878 nearly ended the last independent English kingdom. From Athelney, Alfred spent the spring raiding the enemy and gathering support.

What happened: In the seventh week after Easter, early to mid May, Alfred rode to Egbert's Stone, where the men of Somerset, Wiltshire and part of Hampshire met him, moved on to Iley Oak, and the next day defeated Guthrum's army at Ethandun. He pursued the survivors to their fortress and besieged them until they gave hostages. The Chronicle and Asser tell the same story, so the battle is secure, but Ethandun is only usually identified with Edington below Salisbury Plain; no battlefield finds confirm it, and a Somerset Edington has also been proposed.

After: Three weeks later Guthrum and some thirty of his leading men were baptised, with Alfred as his godfather, and his army withdrew, first to Cirencester and then to East Anglia, where Guthrum ruled as a Christian king. A treaty between the two kings later set out a frontier running up the Thames and the Lea, to Bedford and along the Ouse to Watling Street, with English rule to the south and west and Scandinavian rule beyond. Edington saved Wessex as an independent kingdom; that it was the founding moment of England, as later writers have presented it, is an interpretation made with hindsight.

## Stop: alfred-jewel
Found in 1693 a few miles from Athelney, the jewel carries the words 'Alfred ordered me to be made'. A teardrop of rock crystal covers an enamel figure, set in gold with filigree and ending in an animal head with a small socket; it has been in the Ashmolean Museum in Oxford since 1718. It was probably the handle of a pointer for following a text, perhaps one of those Alfred said he sent with his English translation of Gregory's Pastoral Care to every bishop in his kingdom. That book belonged to his plan, in the later years of his reign, to have the books "most needful for all men to know" put into English. That use, and the identity of the Alfred named, are interpretation, though the find-spot and the style of the goldwork make the king by far the likeliest owner.

## Stop: wallingford-burh
Before: The Viking armies of the 860s and 870s had moved fast, seized places such as Chippenham by surprise and sheltered in fortified camps, while Wessex had no network of strongholds to hold them off. After Edington Alfred had a breathing space. He used it to build a system of fortified places, or burhs, and to find the men to garrison them; Asser says that some of his people resisted the burden.

What happened: The system is known from the Burghal Hidage, a list of some thirty burhs across Wessex with the number of hides of land assigned to each, on the rule that every hide sent one man and four men held each pole of wall. Wallingford, at a crossing of the Thames, was given 2,400 hides, one of the largest figures, and its rectangular earth bank and ditch still survive best on the west side of the town. Neither the building of the defences nor the date of the list is recorded: the works are usually put in Alfred's later reign, and the list in its surviving form is usually dated 914–919, which is why the map shows a window.

After: Many burhs became lasting towns, with planned streets, markets and mints; Æthelstan's laws later ordered that coins be struck only in towns, and Wallingford had a mint and remained a leading town of the region into the Middle Ages. Alfred's son Edward the Elder and his daughter Æthelflæd, Lady of the Mercians, built more burhs in the 910s and used them to advance step by step into the lands under Scandinavian rule, until by the end of the 910s the Scandinavian settlers south of the Humber had submitted to Edward. That the burhs made this conquest possible, and turned a defensive plan into a framework for towns, is how most historians read them; how far Alfred planned all of it from the start is debated.

## Stop: battle-of-brunanburh-937
Alfred's grandson Athelstan defeated a coalition of northern kings at Brunanburh, a victory celebrated as the moment the English kingdom held. Ten years earlier he had taken York and claimed lordship over the other rulers of Britain; in 937 Olaf Guthfrithson of Dublin, Constantine II of Alba and the Strathclyde Britons combined against him and his brother Edmund. The Chronicle marks the victory with a poem, but no source fixes the site: Bromborough on the Wirral is the leading candidate, and others in northern England and southern Scotland have been argued for. From the marshes of Athelney to Brunanburh is less than sixty years. That the battle made England is a later interpretation of its long-term effects.

## Closing
From a marsh fort to a kingdom ringed by fortified towns took less than sixty years. Much of the written record of Alfred's recovery comes from his own court, so it tells the story his way. At Brunanburh his grandson won the battle that held the English kingdom together.
