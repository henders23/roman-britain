# Voices from the past: who wrote things down?
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Order: chronological

Most people in Roman and early medieval Britain left no writing. This journey visits the places where writing survives, from debts and curses scratched in wax and lead to the great books of the monasteries, and asks whose voices each one preserves.

## Stop: bloomberg-tablets
Under the City of London, waterlogged ground preserved hundreds of wooden writing tablets. One records a debt and is dated to 8 January AD 57, the earliest dated handwritten document from Britain. These are the voices of traders and clerks in a young town.

## Stop: aquae-sulis
At Bath, people threw lead tablets into the sacred spring asking the goddess to punish thieves. The curses name ordinary people, and some may be in a British language written in Roman letters: voices that no historian recorded.

## Stop: vindolanda-tablets
On the northern frontier, the tablets include letters between officers' wives, among them one of the earliest known pieces of writing in Latin by a woman.

## Stop: silchester-ogham-stone
In the ruins of a Roman town, someone set up a stone carved in ogham, an Irish alphabet of strokes. Its date is disputed, but it shows a new kind of writing arriving after Rome.

## Stop: jarrow-dedication-685
The dedication stone at Jarrow gives the very day the church was dedicated in 685, carved in Latin. Writing has moved from everyday business into the church.

## Stop: lindisfarne-gospels
The Lindisfarne Gospels are writing as a work of art, made in a monastery for worship and display. A note added centuries later names the scribe.

## Stop: bede-historia-731
Bede, at Jarrow, wrote the first history of the English people. Much of what is on this map after 597 depends on him, so it is worth remembering that his voice, too, had a point of view.
