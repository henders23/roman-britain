# Voices from the past: who wrote things down?
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: how-do-we-know

Most people in Roman and early medieval Britain left no writing. This journey visits the places where writing survives, from debts and curses scratched in wax and lead to the great books of the monasteries, and asks whose voices each one preserves.

## Stop: bloomberg-tablets
Under the City of London, waterlogged ground preserved hundreds of wooden writing tablets. One records a debt and is dated to 8 January AD 57, the earliest dated handwritten document from Britain. These are the voices of traders and clerks in a young town. The tablets were once coated with wax, which has perished, so the texts are read from the scratches where the stylus cut through into the wood, and only 87 of about 405 could be read. The dated one records that a man called Tibullus owed 105 denarii to Gratus; the tablet itself names both men as freedmen, and that they were doing business for their former masters is the editor's reading. Another is addressed to a man in Londinium, the earliest written use of London's name.

## Stop: aquae-sulis
At Bath, people threw lead tablets into the sacred spring asking the goddess to punish thieves. The curses name ordinary people, and some may be in a British language written in Roman letters: voices that no historian recorded. More than a hundred of these tablets, of lead or tin, have been recovered from the spring. Most were written by the victims of small thefts, often of clothing, who asked Sulis Minerva to punish whoever had taken it and sometimes listed the suspects by name. They are personal and angry in a way that the formal altars and tombstones of the same sanctuary are not.

## Stop: vindolanda-tablets
On the northern frontier, the tablets include letters between officers' wives, among them one of the earliest known pieces of writing in Latin by a woman. In it Claudia Severa invites Sulpicia Lepidina, wife of the fort's commander, to her birthday celebration; the letter was written out by a scribe, but its closing lines appear to be in Severa's own hand. Other tablets carry the army's paperwork, such as strength reports, accounts and requests for leave, and private letters, one of them sending socks, sandals and underpants. Most were written around the year 100, and they let us hear a frontier community speaking to itself rather than to posterity.

## Stop: silchester-ogham-stone
In the ruins of a Roman town, someone set up a stone carved in ogham, an Irish alphabet of strokes. Its date is disputed, but it shows a new kind of writing arriving after Rome. The stone is a reused Roman column, found upside down in a well in 1893, and its strokes give a man's name, read as Ebicatos or Tebicatos, followed by 'son of the tribe of' a group whose name is lost. It is the only ogham stone found in England outside the south-west, and it is usually taken to show that Irish-speaking people lived in Calleva as Roman rule ended. Here, for once, the voice is not Latin at all.

## Stop: jarrow-dedication-685
The dedication stone at Jarrow gives the very day the church was dedicated in 685, carved in Latin. Writing has moved from everyday business into the church. The inscription, cut in Roman capitals and now set above the chancel arch of St Paul's, dates the dedication by the years of King Ecgfrith and Abbot Ceolfrith, which works out as 23 April. It speaks for the monastery and its royal founder, recording the event in a form meant to last. It is one of the earliest closely dated inscriptions in England.

## Stop: lindisfarne-gospels
The Lindisfarne Gospels are writing as a work of art, made in a monastery for worship and display. A note added centuries later names the scribe. The main text is the Latin of the gospels, written early in the eighth century. In the tenth century the priest Aldred added an Old English translation word by word between the lines, the earliest surviving version of the gospels in English, and in his note he named himself as well as Eadfrith, the bishop who wrote the book. In one manuscript, then, we hear the voices of two centuries and two languages.

## Stop: bede-historia-731
Bede, at Jarrow, wrote the first history of the English people. Much of what is on this map after 597 depends on him, so it is worth remembering that his voice, too, had a point of view. He wrote in Latin, usually said where his information came from, and ended the book with a short account of his own life and a list of his works, a rare personal voice from the period. He also wrote as a monk with firm views, about the correct date of Easter for example, and chose which kings and saints to praise. Two copies made within a few years of his death in 735 survive, so his words reach us almost exactly as he wrote them.

## Closing
Writing in Britain begins here with soldiers, merchants and people cursing thieves, and this journey ends with a monk writing the history of a people. Who could write changed, and so does what we can know. Most people in these centuries are still silent.
