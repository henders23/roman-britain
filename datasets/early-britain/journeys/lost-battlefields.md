# Lost battlefields
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: how-do-we-know

Where were the great battles of this period fought? Usually, nobody knows. This journey visits seven battles across a thousand years. Watch the map: most are drawn as rings, not pins, because the sources name a battle but not a place anyone can find.

## Stop: river-battle-43
Cassius Dio describes a hard two-day battle at a river crossing early in the invasion, but does not name the river. He wrote some 170 years after the event, and his is the only account. He says the Britons camped on the far bank, trusting the river to protect them, until troops trained to swim in armour crossed and attacked their chariot horses; a force under the future emperor Vespasian followed, and Hosidius Geta, nearly captured on the second day, won the battle. The Medway is a later identification, so the map draws a region. It depends on the army having landed at Richborough and marched west; scholars who favour a landing near Chichester have proposed other rivers, such as the Arun. No battlefield archaeology has been found.

## Stop: boudica-final-battle-60
The revolt of Boudica ended in a pitched battle that the Romans won. Tacitus says the governor, Suetonius Paulinus, had perhaps 10,000 men and chose a narrow valley with woodland behind him, so that he could only be attacked from the front. The Britons had brought their families in wagons to watch, and when the Roman line drove forward they were trapped against them. Neither ancient account says where it was fought, and no battlefield has been found. A site on Watling Street is often proposed, because Paulinus was coming from the north-west; Mancetter in Warwickshire is the favourite, with High Cross and Church Stowe among the others. Tacitus's figures of 80,000 British and 400 Roman dead are not taken as reliable. Even the year, 60 or 61, is debated.

## Stop: mons-graupius-83
Tacitus names Mons Graupius as the hill where Agricola defeated the Caledonians, somewhere in north-east Scotland. The battle came in Agricola's seventh season, in 83 or 84, and Tacitus describes auxiliary infantry and cavalry winning it while the legions waited in reserve. He is the only source and was writing in praise of his father-in-law, so the speech he gives the Caledonian leader Calgacus is his own, and his casualty figures are not counts. Many sites have been suggested; none is proven. The discovery of a very large Roman marching camp at Durno, below Bennachie near Inverurie, made Bennachie the favourite, while Raedykes and Kempstone Hill near Stonehaven and Duns Dish in Angus have their supporters. The map draws a ring over the main candidates without choosing one.

## Stop: dun-nechtain-685
The Irish annals call the place where the Picts destroyed a Northumbrian army Dún Nechtain. On 20 May 685 the Pictish king Bridei son of Beli defeated and killed King Ecgfrith of Northumbria, ending Northumbrian power north of the Forth. Bede, writing some forty-five years later, does not name the place but says the Picts feigned flight and drew Ecgfrith into narrow passes among inaccessible mountains. The name Nechtanesmere, the 'lake of Nechtan', first appears in a 12th-century history. It is identified with Dunnichen in Angus or with Dunachton in Badenoch, and the argument continues. Dunnichen's early name forms come from Dún Nechtain, but in 2006 Alex Woolf pointed out that Dunachton by Loch Insh has the same origin and lies among real mountains; the two candidates are about 87 km apart, and the ring covers both.

## Stop: battle-of-edington-878
Alfred's victory over Guthrum at Ethandun is well recorded. The Chronicle and Asser, writing in 893, say Alfred gathered the men of Somerset, Wiltshire and part of Hampshire at Egbert's Stone, moved on to Iley Oak, and the next day won at Ethandun, then besieged the Viking army in its fortress until it gave hostages. It is usually placed at Edington in Wiltshire, which fits the route the sources give, but no battlefield archaeology confirms it, and a Somerset Edington has also been proposed. Neither Egbert's Stone nor Iley Oak can be located for certain, and the fortress where the army sheltered is unidentified too, though Chippenham is often suggested. Even the month is worked out: the Chronicle's seventh week after Easter puts the battle in early to mid May.

## Stop: battle-of-brunanburh-937
One of the most famous battles of the age, celebrated in an Old English poem, and still without an agreed site. Æthelstan and his brother Edmund defeated Olaf of Dublin, Constantine of Alba and the Strathclyde Britons, and the poem in the Chronicle says the beaten Norse fled by ship over Dingesmere to Dublin, which suggests a western coast. Bromborough on the Wirral is the leading candidate among many, because its name is widely thought to derive from Brunanburh. A local team has reported 10th-century weapon fragments and fittings from fields nearby, but the finds are not yet fully published and their link to the battle is interpretation. Other proposals include Burnswark in Dumfriesshire, Lanchester in County Durham and sites in Yorkshire and Lancashire.

## Stop: battle-of-hastings-1066
Here, at last, a battle with a known place: the ridge at Battle, where William founded an abbey whose altar was said to mark where Harold fell. On 14 October 1066 the English held the ridge in a shield wall through a day of attacks by Norman archers, infantry and cavalry, and Harold and his brothers Gyrth and Leofwine were killed. The Chronicle, William of Poitiers and the Bayeux Tapestry all describe the battle, but the main evidence for its site is still the abbey's foundation tradition, so even here the map marks it approximate. No battle archaeology has been found on the ridge, and other sites nearby, such as Caldbec Hill and Crowhurst, have been proposed.

## Closing
Battles were remembered in writing, but rarely where they happened. Of seven famous battles on this journey, only Hastings has a battlefield anyone can stand on with reasonable confidence. A ring on the map is not a gap in the atlas; it is an honest record of what the evidence allows.
