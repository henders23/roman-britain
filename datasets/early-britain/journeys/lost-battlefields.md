# Lost battlefields
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: how-do-we-know

Where were the great battles of this period fought? Usually, nobody knows. This journey visits seven battles across a thousand years. Watch the map: most are drawn as rings, not pins, because the sources name a battle but not a place anyone can find.

## Stop: river-battle-43
Cassius Dio describes a hard two-day battle at a river crossing early in the invasion, but does not name the river. The Medway is a later identification, so the map draws a region.

## Stop: boudica-final-battle-60
The revolt of Boudica ended in a pitched battle that the Romans won. Neither ancient account says where it was fought, and no battlefield has been found. A site on Watling Street is often proposed.

## Stop: mons-graupius-83
Tacitus names Mons Graupius as the hill where Agricola defeated the Caledonians, somewhere in north-east Scotland. Many sites have been suggested; none is proven.

## Stop: dun-nechtain-685
The Irish annals call the place where the Picts destroyed a Northumbrian army Dún Nechtain. It is identified with Dunnichen in Angus or with Dunachton in Badenoch, and the argument continues.

## Stop: battle-of-edington-878
Alfred's victory over Guthrum at Ethandun is well recorded. It is usually placed at Edington in Wiltshire, which fits the route the sources give, but no battlefield archaeology confirms it, and a Somerset Edington has also been proposed.

## Stop: battle-of-brunanburh-937
One of the most famous battles of the age, celebrated in an Old English poem, and still without an agreed site. Bromborough on the Wirral is the leading candidate among many.

## Stop: battle-of-hastings-1066
Here, at last, a battle with a known place: the ridge at Battle, where William founded an abbey whose altar was said to mark where Harold fell. Even so, the map marks it approximate.

## Closing
Battles were remembered in writing, but rarely where they happened. Of seven famous battles on this journey, only Hastings has a battlefield anyone can stand on with confidence. A ring on the map is not a gap in the atlas; it is an honest record of what the evidence allows.
