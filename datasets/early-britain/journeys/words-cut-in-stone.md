# Words cut in stone
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: how-do-we-know

Writing on wood, wax and parchment usually rotted away. Stone lasted. This journey visits five stones that carry messages from their makers: soldiers honouring a god, an Irish name in a Roman town, the date a church was dedicated, Pictish carving, and a Welsh king's family history. Ask at each stop who was meant to read it.

## Stop: carrawburgh-mithraeum
At the temple of Mithras outside the fort at Carrawburgh, three altars were set up by commanders of the garrison, each carved with an inscription naming the man who dedicated it. The temple was built early in the 3rd century, found in the dry summer of 1949 and excavated the next year. The three dedicators, Lucius Antonius Proculus, Aulus Cluentius Habitus and Marcus Simplicius Simplex, were prefects of the First Cohort of Batavians, a unit first raised in the Rhine delta. Their short inscriptions address the unconquered god Mithras and give each man's name and rank. The temple was small and its cult was for initiates, so these words were for the god and fellow worshippers rather than for the public. One altar carries a carving of Mithras with a crown of rays, cut through so that a lamp behind it could shine out to worshippers in the dark temple. The altars are now in the Great North Museum in Newcastle, and copies stand on the site.

## Stop: silchester-ogham-stone
In a well at Silchester, a small Roman stone column was found carrying an ogham inscription, an Irish script, naming a man usually read as Ebicatos or Tebicatos. It is the only ogham stone found in England outside the south-west. Excavators found it upside down at the bottom of the well in 1893; it is a reused baluster from a Roman portico, with the ogham strokes cut along one edge. The text seems to name the man as 'son of the tribe of' a group whose name is lost. Ogham stones are usually dated to the 5th to 7th centuries, but radiocarbon dates from the well and a 5th-century cottage beside it suggest this one may belong to the years around 410, as Roman rule ended. If so, Irish-speaking people were living in the town, and whoever cut the name presumably expected someone there to read an Irish script.

## Stop: jarrow-dedication-685
A stone in St Paul's church at Jarrow records that the church was dedicated on 23 April 685. It still survives in the church, and gives one of the few dates of this age written at the time. Its Latin, cut in Roman capitals and now set above the chancel arch, dates the day by the fifteenth year of King Ecgfrith and the fourth of Abbot Ceolfrith, so the reader needed to know who ruled to work out the year. Ceolfrith had been sent from Wearmouth a few years earlier with a group of monks to found the new house, where Bede would live and write. The church also holds fragments of a much older inscription naming the emperor Hadrian, Roman words in stone reused in its walls.

## Stop: aberlemno-sculptured-stones
At Aberlemno in Angus, a churchyard cross-slab is carved with a battle scene and Pictish symbols. The Picts left almost no writing, so their stones carry messages we can see but not fully read. On one face of the slab, about 2.3 metres high, is a cross decorated with interlace and beasts; on the other, below a Z-rod, a triple disc and other symbols, three rows of horsemen and foot soldiers fight, and a fallen warrior lies watched by a bird. No inscription or excavation dates it, and comparison with other art places it in the 8th century. The helmeted riders have often been read as Northumbrians defeated at Dun Nechtain in 685, but that reading is less certain now that the battle site is disputed. By the roadside nearby are a symbol stone carved with a serpent, which may be older, and a second cross-slab, which blew down in a storm in March 2025.

## Stop: eliseg-pillar
Near Llangollen, a king of Powys set up a stone cross with a long Latin inscription honouring his great-grandfather Eliseg and tracing his family's claims. The king was Concenn, or Cyngen, who reigned from about 808 until he died at Rome in 854 or 855. The inscription is now illegible and is known mainly from a copy made in 1696. Edward Lhuyd, who made it, read about thirty lines praising Eliseg for winning back Powys from the English and tracing the dynasty to Vortigern and the Roman emperor Maximus; that is a king's claim about his ancestors, not evidence for them. Excavation in 2010–12 showed that the cross was set on an Early Bronze Age burial cairn, deliberately reused as its setting, perhaps at a place where people gathered. The nearby abbey of Valle Crucis, the valley of the cross, takes its name from it.

## Closing
Stones were made to be seen and to last: by a god, by a congregation, by anyone passing. They tell us what their makers wanted remembered, which is not always what happened. Some, like the Pictish carvings, we can still only partly read.
