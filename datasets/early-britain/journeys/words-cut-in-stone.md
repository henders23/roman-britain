# Words cut in stone
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: how-do-we-know

Writing on wood, wax and parchment usually rotted away. Stone lasted. This journey visits five stones that carry messages from their makers: soldiers honouring a god, an Irish name in a Roman town, the date a church was dedicated, Pictish carving, and a Welsh king's family history. Ask at each stop who was meant to read it.

## Stop: carrawburgh-mithraeum
At the temple of Mithras outside the fort at Carrawburgh, three altars were set up by commanders of the garrison, each carved with an inscription naming the man who dedicated it.

## Stop: silchester-ogham-stone
In a well at Silchester, a small Roman stone column was found carrying an ogham inscription, an Irish script, naming a man usually read as Ebicatos or Tebicatos. It is the only ogham stone found east of the Severn.

## Stop: jarrow-dedication-685
A stone in St Paul's church at Jarrow records that the church was dedicated on 23 April 685. It still survives in the church, and gives one of the few dates of this age written at the time.

## Stop: aberlemno-sculptured-stones
At Aberlemno in Angus, a churchyard cross-slab is carved with a battle scene and Pictish symbols. The Picts left almost no writing, so their stones carry messages we can see but not fully read.

## Stop: eliseg-pillar
Near Llangollen, a king of Powys set up a stone cross with a long Latin inscription honouring his great-grandfather Eliseg and tracing his family's claims. The inscription is now illegible and is known mainly from a copy made in 1696.

## Closing
Stones were made to be seen and to last: by a god, by a congregation, by anyone passing. They tell us what their makers wanted remembered, which is not always what happened. Some, like the Pictish carvings, we can still only partly read.
