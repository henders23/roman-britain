# From trading towns to fortified towns
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: towns-trade-and-everyday-life

After Rome, towns almost disappeared from Britain. Where did they come back, and why there? This journey follows the new trading towns of the seventh and eighth centuries, then the fortified towns built against the Vikings, and ends in a crowded Viking-age street in York.

## Stop: lundenwic
From the seventh to the ninth century, London's trading town lay outside the old Roman walls, along the Strand and around Covent Garden. Archaeologists call it Lundenwic.

## Stop: gipeswic-ipswich
Ipswich grew from a seventh-century landing place on the River Orwell into an eighth-century trading town, where Ipswich ware, England's first wheel-turned and kiln-fired pottery since Rome, was made.

## Stop: hamwic
At Southampton the West Saxons laid out a planned trading and craft town on the River Itchen, occupied from about 700 to the mid ninth century.

## Stop: wallingford-burh
When Viking armies came, Wessex built fortified towns, or burhs, listed in the Burghal Hidage. Wallingford on the Thames is one; its earth rampart still survives around much of the town.

## Stop: coppergate-tenements
In tenth-century York, excavation at Coppergate found property plots with timber houses and workshops preserved in wet ground. It is the best evidence for everyday life in a Viking-age town.

## Closing
Towns came back first as open trading places on rivers and coasts, built for kings who wanted the wealth of trade. The Viking wars turned towns into places to defend as well. By the tenth century, York's crowded streets show a town life that would carry on into the middle ages.
