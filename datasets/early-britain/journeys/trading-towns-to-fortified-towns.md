# From trading towns to fortified towns
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: towns-trade-and-everyday-life

After Rome, towns almost disappeared from Britain. Where did they come back, and why there? This journey follows the new trading towns of the seventh and eighth centuries, then the fortified towns built against the Vikings, and ends in a crowded Viking-age street in York.

## Stop: lundenwic
From the seventh to the ninth century, London's trading town lay outside the old Roman walls, along the Strand and around Covent Garden. Archaeologists call it Lundenwic. Bede, writing in 731, describes London as a market for many peoples coming by land and sea, yet for a long time no such town could be found inside the walls. Excavations since the 1980s, above all at the Royal Opera House, have found gravelled streets, timber buildings, debris from metal and jewellery working, and imported pottery across some 55–60 hectares. In the 9th century a ditch was dug, usually read as a defence against Viking attack, and after Alfred took control of London in 886 settlement moved back inside the Roman walls. The abandoned site became Ealdwic, the 'old settlement', a name that survives as Aldwych.

## Stop: gipeswic-ipswich
Ipswich grew from a seventh-century landing place on the River Orwell into an eighth-century trading town, where Ipswich ware, England's first wheel-turned and kiln-fired pottery since Rome, was made. By the 8th century the town covered some 50 hectares, with streets, buildings and potters working in its north-east quarter around Carr Street and Cox Lane. Ipswich ware was turned on a slow wheel, made from about 720 to about 850 on current dating, and traded across East Anglia and as far as Kent, Yorkshire and London. Its shapes and kilns resemble continental, especially Frisian, wares, and whether migrant potters brought the craft is debated. Unlike Lundenwic and Hamwic, Ipswich carried on as a town without a break.

## Stop: hamwic
At Southampton the West Saxons laid out a planned trading and craft town on the River Itchen, occupied from about 700 to the mid ninth century. Excavation since 1946 has mapped at least 42 hectares, with a regular grid of gravelled streets, closely packed timber buildings, pits and wells, and evidence of iron, bone, textile and glass working. Imported pottery and glass point to trade with northern France and the Low Countries, and many silver sceattas have been found, some perhaps struck in the town. The planned layout is commonly read as a royal foundation, perhaps under King Ine, but no source records it. The Chronicle records a Viking fleet defeated at Hamtun in 840, and by the later ninth century the town was in decline.

## Stop: wallingford-burh
When Viking armies came, Wessex built fortified towns, or burhs, listed in the Burghal Hidage. Wallingford on the Thames is one; its earth rampart still survives around much of the town. The Hidage gives each burh a number of hides, on the rule that each hide sent one man and four men held each pole of wall, and Wallingford's 2,400 hides are among the largest figures. The list is usually dated 914–919, and the defences are generally thought to be Alfred's work of the 880s or 890s, though no source records their building. Unlike the open trading towns, the burhs were walled, laid out with streets inside, and became places of markets and mints, so defence and trade now went together.

## Stop: coppergate-tenements
In tenth-century York, excavation at Coppergate found property plots with timber houses and workshops preserved in wet ground. York Archaeological Trust dug the site between 1976 and 1981 and recovered some 40,000 finds. Four long plots ran back from the street, laid out around the 930s, and their post-and-wattle buildings were later replaced by plank-built houses and workshops with semi-basements. Woodturners, metalworkers, jewellers, leatherworkers and textile workers lived and worked there, and their trade reached the Baltic and beyond. It is the best evidence for everyday life in a Viking-age town, and it shows craft and trade now packed into narrow plots along a town street rather than spread along an open shore.

## Closing
Towns came back first as open trading places on rivers and coasts, built for kings who wanted the wealth of trade. The Viking wars turned towns into places to defend as well. By the tenth century, York's crowded streets show a town life that would carry on into the middle ages.
