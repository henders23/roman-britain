# The great army and the answer
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: vikings-and-england

In the 860s and 870s a Viking army, which the Chronicle calls the great army, moved from kingdom to kingdom across England. This journey follows a few years of that campaign and asks how the English answered it: by flight, by battle, and by building fortified towns.

## Stop: repton-winter-camp-873
In the winter of 873–4 the great army camped at Repton, a royal and religious centre of Mercia, and drove King Burgred out of his kingdom. The Chronicle says the army came from Lindsey, sent Burgred overseas and gave Mercia to Ceolwulf, a king's thegn who promised to serve it. Excavation has found the camp beside the church. In the 1970s and 1980s Martin Biddle and Birthe Kjølbye-Biddle traced a ditch that used St Wystan's church as part of a D-shaped enclosure open to the Trent, with burials carrying Scandinavian weapons and ornaments. A stone building reused as a mortuary chamber held the bones of at least 264 people, and a 2018 study brought their radiocarbon dates into line with the 870s, though linking every part of the site to the army is still interpretation. From Repton the army divided, and part of it went north under Halfdan.

## Stop: cuthbert-community-leaves-lindisfarne-875
As part of the army moved north under Halfdan, the community of St Cuthbert left Lindisfarne with the saint's body. The date and the story come from the community's own later histories. They name Bishop Eardwulf and Abbot Eadred as its leaders and describe years of moving about Northumbria, including a failed attempt to sail to Ireland, before the community settled at Chester-le-Street. These accounts were written long afterwards to support the community's claims to land, and some historians think its centre had already shifted to Norham on the Tweed, so the flight may have been more gradual than the story tells. In 876, the Chronicle says, Halfdan shared out the land of the Northumbrians and his men began to plough it, as the army in the north turned to settling.

## Stop: battle-of-edington-878
Early in 878 a surprise attack drove Alfred of Wessex into the Somerset marshes. The army, now under Guthrum, had seized the royal estate at Chippenham at midwinter, and Alfred withdrew with a small band to Athelney. In early to mid May, seven weeks after Easter, he gathered the men of Somerset, Wiltshire and part of Hampshire at Egbert's Stone. Within days he defeated the army of Guthrum at Ethandun, then besieged it in its fortress until it gave hostages, and Guthrum accepted baptism and took his army to East Anglia. The battle is usually placed at Edington in Wiltshire, but no battlefield archaeology confirms it. A Somerset Edington on the Polden Hills has also been argued for, so the map shows a region rather than a point.

## Stop: wallingford-burh
To defend Wessex, Alfred and his successors built a network of fortified towns, or burhs, listed in a document called the Burghal Hidage. The Hidage gives each burh a number of hides, on the rule that each hide supplied one man and four men held each pole of wall. Wallingford, guarding a crossing of the Thames, was given 2,400 hides, one of the largest figures in the list. The list is usually dated 914–919, and no written source records when the defences were built, though they are generally put under Alfred in the 880s or 890s. Wallingford's earth rampart still surrounds much of the town, and its large rectangular plan beside the river shows that the burhs were laid out as lasting towns as well as forts.

## Stop: cuerdale-hoard
Around 905–910 a chest of about 40 kilograms of silver was buried by the River Ribble in Lancashire, the largest Viking silver hoard known from Britain and Ireland. Workmen repairing the river bank found it in May 1840: some 7,000 coins and more than 1,000 pieces of ingots, ornaments and hack-silver, packed in a lead chest. Its coins from York and East Anglia show Viking rulers now minting their own money in England, beside Anglo-Saxon, Carolingian and Islamic pieces whose latest examples give the date of burial. Why it was buried by the river is not known; a common view links it to the Norse driven out of Dublin in 902, or to a war-chest for a campaign, but that is interpretation. By then the conquests of the great army had become Scandinavian kingdoms at York and in East Anglia, linked by trade across the Irish Sea.

## Closing
Within a generation the great army had broken Mercia, driven a monastery from its island, and been beaten by Alfred. The answer to it was not one battle but a new way of defending a kingdom, with towns built to be held. Meanwhile, in the north and east, Vikings were becoming rulers and traders.
