# The great army and the answer
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: vikings-and-england

In the 860s and 870s a Viking army, which the Chronicle calls the great army, moved from kingdom to kingdom across England. This journey follows a few years of that campaign and asks how the English answered it: by flight, by battle, and by building fortified towns.

## Stop: repton-winter-camp-873
In the winter of 873–4 the great army camped at Repton, a royal and religious centre of Mercia, and drove King Burgred out of his kingdom. Excavation has found the camp beside the church.

## Stop: cuthbert-community-leaves-lindisfarne-875
As part of the army moved north under Halfdan, the community of St Cuthbert left Lindisfarne with the saint's body. The date and the story come from the community's own later histories.

## Stop: battle-of-edington-878
Early in 878 a surprise attack drove Alfred of Wessex into the Somerset marshes. Months later he defeated the army of Guthrum at Ethandun, and Guthrum accepted baptism. The battle is usually placed at Edington in Wiltshire, but no battlefield archaeology confirms it.

## Stop: wallingford-burh
To defend Wessex, Alfred and his successors built a network of fortified towns, or burhs, listed in a document called the Burghal Hidage. Wallingford's earth rampart still surrounds much of the town.

## Stop: cuerdale-hoard
Around 905–910 a chest of about 40 kilograms of silver was buried by the River Ribble in Lancashire, the largest Viking silver hoard known from Britain and Ireland. Its coins from York and East Anglia show Viking rulers now minting their own money in England.

## Closing
Within a generation the great army had broken Mercia, driven a monastery from its island, and been beaten by Alfred. The answer to it was not one battle but a new way of defending a kingdom, with towns built to be held. Meanwhile, in the north and east, Vikings were becoming rulers and traders.
