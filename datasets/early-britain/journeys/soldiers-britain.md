# Soldiers' Britain
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: conquest-and-frontiers

Roman Britain was held by one of the largest garrisons in the empire. This journey follows the army from its invasion base to the forts it built against raiders from the sea, and asks what soldiers needed and what they left behind: ditches, a fortress plan, letters, an amphitheatre and altars to their gods.

## Stop: richborough-invasion-base-43
At Richborough in east Kent, a pair of ditches fortified a stretch of shore in AD 43. They are widely taken to be the base where the invasion army came ashore, and Richborough became the main port into the province.

## Stop: inchtuthil-fortress
In the early 80s the army began a legionary fortress at Inchtuthil, above the Tay in Perthshire, then took it down unfinished a few years later. Because nothing was built over it, it gives the only complete plan of a legionary fortress known anywhere in the empire.

## Stop: vindolanda-tablets
At the fort of Vindolanda, hundreds of wooden writing tablets survived in waterlogged ground. They include strength reports, accounts, requests for leave and an invitation to a birthday party: the army's everyday life in its own words.

## Stop: caerleon-amphitheatre
Outside the legionary fortress at Caerleon, soldiers built an amphitheatre about AD 90 for displays, training and shows. A fortress was a home for thousands of men, not only a base for war.

## Stop: carrawburgh-mithraeum
Early in the third century soldiers built a small temple to Mithras outside the fort at Carrawburgh on Hadrian's Wall. Its altars were set up by officers of the garrison, who brought the god from the eastern empire.

## Stop: portchester-shore-fort
In about 285–290 a square fort was built on a promontory in Portsmouth Harbour. Its walls are the best preserved of any Roman fort in northern Europe. It faced the sea, where the new threats came from.

## Closing
Over two and a half centuries the army moved from conquest to settling in: from invasion ditches to fortresses, letters home, entertainment and private worship. By the 280s it was building forts to face the sea. Soldiers left more evidence of their daily lives than almost anyone else in Roman Britain.
