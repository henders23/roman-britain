# Soldiers' Britain
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: conquest-and-frontiers

Roman Britain was held by one of the largest garrisons in the empire. This journey follows the army from its invasion base to the forts it built against raiders from the sea, and asks what soldiers needed and what they left behind: ditches, a fortress plan, letters, an amphitheatre and altars to their gods.

## Stop: richborough-invasion-base-43
At Richborough in east Kent, a pair of ditches fortified a stretch of shore in AD 43. Richborough then stood on the Wantsum Channel, which divided the Isle of Thanet from mainland Kent and gave ships a sheltered approach. Excavations by the Society of Antiquaries in the 1920s and 1930s traced two closely spaced V-shaped ditches with a rampart, running for at least 650 metres parallel to the old shoreline; Claudian finds and their place beneath everything later on the site date them. They are widely taken to be the base where the invasion army came ashore, and Richborough became the main port into the province. No ancient writer names the landing place, though, and some scholars argue that the main landing, or a second one, came near Chichester instead. The site went on serving the army for more than three centuries, as a supply base and finally as a late Roman coastal fort.

## Stop: inchtuthil-fortress
In the early 80s the army began a legionary fortress at Inchtuthil, above the Tay in Perthshire, then took it down unfinished a few years later. Because nothing was built over it, it gives the only complete plan of a legionary fortress known anywhere in the empire. Excavations between 1952 and 1965 traced its timber foundation trenches over some 21.5 hectares: barracks for the men, granaries, a hospital, a workshop and a small headquarters, but no commander's house, which had not yet been built. The plan shows what a legion of several thousand men needed first. When the army left, it smashed pottery and glass and buried about seven tonnes of iron nails in a pit so that no enemy could reuse them. Unworn coins of AD 86 date the withdrawal, usually linked to the transfer of a legion from Britain to the Danube.

## Stop: vindolanda-tablets
At the fort of Vindolanda, hundreds of wooden writing tablets survived in waterlogged ground. The fort lay on the Stanegate, the road behind the frontier after the pull-back from Scotland, a little south of where Hadrian's Wall would later run. The tablets come from successive timber forts and date from about the 80s to the 120s, most from the years around 100. They include strength reports, accounts, requests for leave and an invitation to a birthday party: the army's everyday life in its own words. One strength report of the First Cohort of Tungrians counts 752 men, of whom only 296 were at the fort, and 31 of those were sick, wounded or suffering from inflamed eyes. The birthday invitation, from Claudia Severa to Sulpicia Lepidina, the commander's wife, shows that officers' families lived on the frontier too.

## Stop: caerleon-amphitheatre
Outside the legionary fortress at Caerleon, soldiers built an amphitheatre about AD 90 for displays, training and shows. Caerleon, Roman Isca, was the base of the Second Legion Augusta in south Wales, built a few years before Inchtuthil was abandoned and held for far longer. The amphitheatre is an oval about 82 by 68 metres, with a buttressed stone outer wall and earth banks that carried timber seats; Mortimer and Tessa Wheeler excavated it in 1926–27. Its date comes from finds and building sequence rather than an inscription, its capacity of about 6,000 is a modern estimate, and how often it was used, and for what, is inferred. A fortress was a home for thousands of men, not only a base for war.

## Stop: carrawburgh-mithraeum
Early in the third century soldiers built a small temple to Mithras outside the fort at Carrawburgh on Hadrian's Wall. Its altars were set up by officers of the garrison, the First Cohort of Batavians, recruited from the Rhine delta, who worshipped a god whose cult had spread from the eastern empire. The temple was found in the dry summer of 1949 and excavated the next year, and its waterlogged floor preserved timbers from several phases, as the small building was lengthened and refitted. One altar shows Mithras with a crown of rays, cut through so that a lamp behind could shine out. In the 4th century the cult images were damaged and the temple abandoned; this is often read as Christian hostility, but that is an interpretation. With the nearby shrine of Coventina, it shows how many gods a single frontier garrison could serve.

## Stop: portchester-shore-fort
In about 285–290 a square fort was built on a promontory in Portsmouth Harbour. Its walls are the best preserved of any Roman fort in northern Europe. They still stand almost to full height round about three and a half hectares, with projecting D-shaped towers unlike the rounded corners of earlier forts. Coins show that the fort was built after about 268, and its excavator, Barry Cunliffe, linked it to Carausius, who commanded the Channel fleet from about 285 before seizing power in Britain; that attribution is an inference. Excavation inside found uneven occupation that at times included families as well as soldiers. Around 400 a register of offices listed it, if it is rightly identified with Portus Adurni, among the forts under the Count of the Saxon Shore. It faced the sea, where the new threats came from.

## Closing
Over two and a half centuries the army moved from conquest to settling in: from invasion ditches to fortresses, letters home, entertainment and private worship. By the 280s it was building forts to face the sea. Soldiers left more evidence of their daily lives than almost anyone else in Roman Britain.
