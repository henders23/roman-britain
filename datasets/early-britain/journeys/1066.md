# 1066
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Order: chronological

One year, three claimants and three kings of England. In January 1066 Edward the Confessor died and Harold Godwinson was crowned; by the autumn both the king of Norway and the duke of Normandy had invaded to take the throne for themselves. These are among the few events on the map dated to the day, because several contemporary or near-contemporary sources agree on the dates, though English and Norman writers tell the story from opposite sides. The atlas's timeline ends in 1066, at the last stop.

## Stop: edward-dies-harold-crowned-1066
Before: Edward, son of Æthelred and Emma, grew up in exile in Normandy and became king in 1042, ending the line of Danish kings. Much of his reign was shaped by Godwin, earl of Wessex, and his sons: Edward married Godwin's daughter Edith, drove the family into exile in 1051, and had to take them back a year later, and after Godwin's death in 1053 his son Harold became earl of Wessex and the greatest man in the kingdom after the king. Edward had no children. Norman sources, written after the Conquest, say that Edward had promised the throne to his kinsman Duke William, and that Harold, on a visit to Normandy around 1064, swore an oath to support William's claim; the English sources of the time do not mention the visit, and what the oath meant, if it was sworn, cannot now be known.

What happened: Edward's new church at Westminster was consecrated on 28 December 1065 while he lay ill. He died on 5 January 1066 and was buried in the church the next day, and on the same day Harold was crowned king, probably in the abbey. The Chronicle says the dying king entrusted the kingdom to Harold, and the Life of King Edward, written for Queen Edith, describes him stretching out his hand to Harold on his deathbed; Norman writers and the Bayeux Tapestry present the coronation as usurpation, and they name Archbishop Stigand where John of Worcester names Ealdred of York as the archbishop who crowned him. Edward's church survives only as foundations beneath the present abbey, rebuilt from 1245, where his shrine still stands.

After: Harold faced two rival claimants. Harald Hardrada, king of Norway, claimed the throne through an agreement which Norse tradition says was made between his predecessor Magnus and Harthacnut, and he was joined by Harold's own brother Tostig, who had been driven out of his earldom of Northumbria in 1065 and had gone into exile. William of Normandy, whose great-aunt Emma was Edward's mother, said the throne had been promised to him and began building a fleet. Through the summer Harold kept an army and a fleet on the south coast, until on 8 September, the Chronicle says, their provisions ran out and the men were sent home.

## Stop: battle-of-stamford-bridge-1066
Before: In September Harald Hardrada sailed with a large fleet, joined forces with Tostig and came up the Humber and the Ouse towards York. On 20 September the northern earls Edwin and Morcar met him at Fulford, just south of the city, and were defeated, and York submitted. Harold, who had dismissed his southern levies less than two weeks before, gathered an army and marched north with great speed, reaching Tadcaster on the 24th.

What happened: On 25 September Harold fell on the Norwegians at the crossing of the Derwent at Stamford Bridge, east of York; both Harald Hardrada and Tostig were killed. Versions C, D and E of the Chronicle report the battle, C in most detail; the story of a lone Norwegian holding the bridge appears only in a later addition to C and is best treated as tradition. The crossing is securely identified, but where the fighting took place on either bank is reconstructed from later accounts, and the pin follows the registered battlefield east of the village.

After: Harold's victory was decisive: Hardrada's son Olaf was allowed to leave, and the survivors needed only a small part of their fleet to carry them home. The battle is often described as the last major Scandinavian bid for the English throne. Three days later, while Harold was still in the north, William landed in Sussex. How much the march north and the fighting weakened Harold's army for what followed is debated.

## Stop: william-lands-pevensey-1066
Duke William's fleet had waited weeks for a wind, first at the mouth of the Dives and then at Saint-Valery on the Somme, while Harold watched the coast. When it crossed, on the night of 27–28 September, the coast was undefended, because Harold's army had been dismissed and he had gone north. The Chronicle (version D) dates the landing at Pevensey to Michaelmas Eve, 28 September, and the Bayeux Tapestry shows the fleet arriving under a caption naming Pevensey, horses being unloaded and the army hurrying on to Hastings. In 1066 Pevensey stood beside a tidal lagoon and the coastline has since moved, so the landing beach cannot be found; the pin marks the late Roman fort of Anderitum, whose walls the Normans used for their first defences. William soon moved east to Hastings, built a castle there and ravaged the countryside around it, perhaps to draw Harold south quickly. The Tapestry was made later and from the Norman side, and the story of William stumbling as he came ashore is later tradition.

## Stop: battle-of-hastings-1066
Before: Harold heard of the landing in the north and marched back to London, gathering men as he went. He then moved quickly into Sussex, perhaps hoping to catch William by surprise as he had caught Hardrada; John of Worcester, writing later, says that not half his army had arrived when he gave battle, though how far that is true is debated. William, with his ships behind him at Hastings, had every reason to want a quick decisive battle.

What happened: On 14 October, the feast of Pope Calixtus, Harold's army formed a shield wall on a ridge north of Hastings, and the Normans attacked it all day with archers, infantry and cavalry until it broke. Harold and his brothers Gyrth and Leofwine were killed. The battle is described in versions D and E of the Chronicle, by William of Poitiers, the duke's chaplain, and in the Bayeux Tapestry; how Harold died is uncertain, since the Tapestry's figure with an arrow near the eye has been read in different ways. The battlefield is placed at Battle by the tradition of the abbey William founded there, whose high altar was said to mark where Harold fell; no battle archaeology has been found, and Caldbec Hill and Crowhurst have also been proposed, which is why the location is marked approximate.

After: Some of the English leaders in London chose the young Edgar Ætheling, Edward's great-nephew, as king, but William marched round London, crossing the Thames at Wallingford, and at Berkhamsted they submitted to him; he was crowned at Westminster on Christmas Day 1066, in the church where Edward lay. Over the next twenty years English risings were crushed, most English landholders were replaced by Normans, and castles were built across the country to hold it, among them in London and York. Here the atlas's timeline ends. How far the Conquest changed England, and how much of the old English kingdom William kept, is still argued over by historians.
