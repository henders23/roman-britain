# 1066: a kingdom worth fighting for
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: rulers-and-kingdoms

In 1066 two foreign rulers invaded England within three weeks of each other. This journey asks why England was worth the risk. It begins more than a century earlier, when a newly united English kingdom was defended in battle, passes through York, a city with deep Scandinavian roots, and ends with the three events of autumn 1066, which are among the few on the map dated to the day.

## Stop: battle-of-brunanburh-937
In 937 Æthelstan and his brother Edmund defeated an alliance of the king of Dublin, the king of Alba and the Strathclyde Britons. The victory kept the English kingdom together, though where the battle was fought is still disputed.

## Stop: coppergate-tenements
Excavation at Coppergate in York found tenth-century houses and workshops preserved in wet ground. York had been a Viking kingdom, and its Scandinavian links may help explain why a Norwegian king landed in the north.

## Stop: battle-of-stamford-bridge-1066
On 25 September King Harold defeated the Norwegian king Harald Hardrada near York. The site of the battle is identified by tradition and the name of the crossing.

## Stop: william-lands-pevensey-1066
Three days later William of Normandy landed at Pevensey, inside the walls of a Roman fort. The Bayeux Tapestry shows the landing, though it was made later and from the Norman side.

## Stop: battle-of-hastings-1066
On 14 October Harold was defeated and killed north of Hastings. The battlefield is placed at Battle by the tradition of the abbey William founded there, whose altar was said to mark where Harold fell; that is why the location is marked approximate.

## Closing
A kingdom defended at Brunanburh was lost at Hastings, nineteen days after Harold's victory at Stamford Bridge. England was worth fighting for because it was by then one wealthy kingdom, with towns like York and a single crown to claim. The timeline ends here, in 1066.
