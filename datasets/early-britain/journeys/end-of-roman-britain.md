# How did Roman Britain end?
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: how-do-we-know

Textbooks often give 410 as the end of Roman Britain. This journey tests that date against the evidence. Notice how each stop is a different kind of source, and how uncertain each one is.

## Stop: constantine-iii-proclaimed-407
In 407 the army in Britain made a soldier named Constantine emperor. He took the province's mobile troops to Gaul, and they did not come back. This is a better-recorded turning point than 410, though it comes from historians writing later.

## Stop: hoxne-hoard
Someone buried this treasure of gold, silver and coins in Suffolk after about 408. Its latest coins, with finds from many other sites, show that new money had almost stopped reaching Britain, which is some of the strongest evidence for the end of Roman government. Why the owners never came back is unknown.

## Stop: rescript-of-honorius-410
The famous date rests on one sentence by a Greek historian writing about a century later, reporting that the emperor told the cities of Britain to defend themselves. Some scholars think the sentence was about Bruttium in Italy. The map draws it as a faint region with low certainty.

## Stop: silchester-ogham-stone
A stone at Silchester carries an inscription in ogham, an Irish script, sometime in the two centuries after Rome. It is the only ogham stone found in England outside the south-west. Someone with Irish connections was commemorated in an old Roman town, which hints at a changing population that no history records.

## Stop: tintagel-trading-site
In the century after Rome, Tintagel was receiving wine and oil from the Mediterranean. Rome's government had gone, but the west of Britain was still connected to the Roman world by trade. The legend of Arthur at Tintagel is kept off the map; the imported pottery is the evidence.

## Closing
No single source marks the end. An army left in 407, new coins almost stopped arriving soon after, and the famous letter of 410 may not even be about Britain. Yet Roman connections went on in the west for two centuries. Roman Britain ended in different ways at different speeds in different places, and 410 is a convenient date rather than an event the ground can show.
