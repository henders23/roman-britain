# How did Roman Britain end?
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: how-do-we-know

Textbooks often give 410 as the end of Roman Britain. This journey tests that date against the evidence: an army that left with its emperor, a buried treasure, one sentence by a Greek historian, an Irish inscription in an old Roman town, and a Cornish headland that was still trading with the Mediterranean generations later. Notice how each stop is a different kind of source, and how uncertain each one is. The written record says little; most of what we know about the end of Roman Britain comes from what stopped being made, used and buried.

## Stop: constantine-iii-proclaimed-407
In 407 the army in Britain made a soldier named Constantine emperor. Zosimus says the troops had first raised a man called Marcus and then a local man, Gratian, killing each in turn, before choosing Constantine, whom Orosius calls a common soldier picked for the hope his name inspired. He took the province's mobile troops to Gaul, and they did not come back. There he won over the armies left in disorder by the barbarian crossing of the Rhine and was for a time recognised by the emperor Honorius, but he was captured and executed in 411. No source says where in Britain he was proclaimed, and the sources disagree on the years. This is a better-recorded turning point than 410, though it comes from historians writing later.

## Stop: hoxne-hoard
Someone buried this treasure of gold, silver and coins in Suffolk after about 408. It was found in 1992 by Eric Lawes, who was searching a field with a metal detector for a farmer's lost hammer; because he stopped and reported it, archaeologists could record that it had been packed in a wooden chest, some objects in smaller boxes or wrapped in cloth. It holds 14,865 coins, mostly silver, with gold and some bronze, and about 200 other objects, among them a gold body-chain, bracelets, and silver spoons and pepper-pots. The latest coins are two silver pieces of the usurper Constantine III, struck in 407–408, so the hoard cannot have been buried earlier. Its latest coins, with finds from many other sites, show that new money had almost stopped reaching Britain, which is some of the strongest evidence for the end of Roman government. Many of the silver coins had been clipped, which some take as a sign that burial came some years later. Why the owners never came back is unknown.

## Stop: rescript-of-honorius-410
Before: Late Roman Britain was raided by Picts, Scots and Saxons, and its army had already raised several usurpers, of whom Magnus Maximus in 383 had taken troops to the Continent. In 407 the army proclaimed Constantine III, who crossed to Gaul with the province's mobile troops and did not return. Zosimus, a Greek historian writing about a century later from an earlier account, then reports that, when barbarians from beyond the Rhine pressed on them, the Britons took up arms, freed their cities and expelled the Roman officials, governing themselves without Roman law.

What happened: The famous date rests on one sentence of Zosimus, who says that in 410 the emperor Honorius sent letters to the cities of Britain telling them to look to their own defence. It sits in a passage otherwise about Honorius's troubles in Italy, in the year Alaric's Goths sacked Rome, and no text of any letter survives. Because the Greek names for Britain and for Bruttium in southern Italy are very similar, some scholars think the sentence was about Bruttium; others defend the reading 'Britain'. The map draws it as a faint region with low certainty, over the province's cities rather than one place.

After: No Roman army or governor came back to Britain. Within a generation the archaeology shows the Roman way of life coming apart: towns emptied or shrank to a few timber buildings, villas were abandoned, coins stopped being used for everyday exchange, and the great pottery industries, such as those of Oxfordshire and the Nene valley, ceased, leaving people to make pots by hand again. How quickly this happened, and whether it was caused by the end of Roman rule or had begun in the later 4th century, is debated, and some places such as Silchester and Wroxeter show occupation continuing into the 5th century. What is clear is that by about 450 Britain no longer looked Roman on the ground.

## Stop: silchester-ogham-stone
A stone at Silchester carries an inscription in ogham, an Irish script, sometime in the two centuries after Rome. It is the only ogham stone found in England outside the south-west. It was found in 1893, upside down at the bottom of a well, during the Society of Antiquaries' excavation of the Roman town of Calleva: a reused stone baluster from a Roman portico, with ogham strokes cut along one edge. The text gives a man's name, read as Ebicatos or more recently Tebicatos, and his descent from a group whose name is lost. Ogham stones elsewhere are usually dated to the fifth to seventh centuries, but radiocarbon dates from the well suggest it may have gone out of use a little earlier, so the stone may belong to the very end of Roman rule. Someone with Irish connections was commemorated in an old Roman town, which hints at a changing population that no history records.

## Stop: tintagel-trading-site
In the century after Rome, Tintagel was receiving wine and oil from the Mediterranean. Excavations on the headland, from the 1930s to the present, have found more imported pottery of this period than at any other site in Britain or Ireland: red-slipped bowls from the Aegean and amphorae from the eastern Mediterranean, mostly of the late 5th and 6th centuries. The first excavator thought the buildings were a Celtic monastery, but they are now read as a secular high-status settlement, perhaps a centre of the rulers of the south-west, which is itself an interpretation. Rome's government had gone and the towns of the east had emptied, but the west of Britain was still connected to the Roman world by trade, now with Byzantium rather than with Rome. The legend of Arthur at Tintagel, which begins with Geoffrey of Monmouth in the 12th century, is kept off the map; the imported pottery is the evidence.

## Closing
No single source marks the end. An army left in 407, new coins almost stopped arriving soon after, and the famous letter of 410 may not even be about Britain. Yet people still lived in some old Roman towns, and Roman connections went on in the west for two centuries. Roman Britain ended in different ways at different speeds in different places, and 410 is a convenient date rather than an event the ground can show.
