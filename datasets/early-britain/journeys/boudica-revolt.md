# Boudica's revolt
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: conquest-and-frontiers
Thread: none

In AD 60 or 61 the Iceni and their neighbours rose against Roman rule. Almost everything we know about the course of the revolt comes from two Roman historians, Tacitus and Cassius Dio, writing decades or more later. The archaeology is different evidence: layers of burnt debris in three towns. Follow the revolt, and keep asking which kind of evidence each stop rests on. The camera moves between places, but no route is drawn, because nobody knows the rebels' route.

Tacitus puts the revolt under the consuls of AD 61, but many historians read his year as covering 60 and 61 and put the outbreak in 60. The atlas uses 60, marked as approximate, for every stop.

## Stop: mona-attack-60
Tacitus says the governor, Suetonius Paulinus, was far away on Anglesey, attacking a centre of resistance and of the druids, when the revolt broke out. The atlas draws the island as a region because the sources give no more precise place. He describes the infantry crossing the shallow strait in flat-bottomed boats while the cavalry forded or swam beside their horses. On the far shore stood armed men, women in black with torches, and druids calling down curses; after a moment's hesitation the Romans attacked, won, and cut down the sacred groves. The scene is vivid and may be heightened for effect, but it explains why the governor and much of the army were in north Wales when the east rose.

## Stop: camulodunum-destruction-60
The colony at Colchester, with its temple to the deified Claudius, was the first target. Tacitus says the veteran settlers had taken land from the Trinovantes, and that the town had no walls. The procurator sent only a small force to help, and the last defenders held out in the temple for two days before it was stormed; a detachment of the Ninth Legion marching to the rescue was routed on the way. Excavations across the town have found a thick burnt layer of about this date, in places up to a metre of burnt daub, charred timber, melted glass and scorched pottery. The two kinds of evidence agree, but they are not the same thing. The podium of the temple still survives beneath Colchester Castle.

## Stop: londinium-destruction-60
London was a trading town without walls. Paulinus hurried there from Wales, and Tacitus says he judged that his force was too small to hold it, so he left it to the rebels, taking with him anyone who could keep up. Those who stayed were killed. A burnt horizon of this period runs under the modern City, best known around Cornhill and Lombard Street, and it is one of the clearest dating markers in London's archaeology. Writing tablets from the Bloomberg site, one dated AD 57, show how busy the young town had become just before it burnt.

## Stop: verulamium-destruction-60
The rebels next took Verulamium, near modern St Albans, a town of the Catuvellauni rather than of Roman settlers. Tacitus names it but says almost nothing else, so most of what we know comes from the ground. Between 1957 and 1961 Sheppard Frere excavated a row of timber shops on Watling Street that had burnt at about this date, apparently set alight from the street side. The burnt deposits elsewhere in the town are patchy and some buildings escaped, so how complete the destruction was is debated. Tacitus's figures for the dead in the three towns are huge, and are best read as a Roman writer's rhetoric rather than a count.

## Stop: boudica-final-battle-60
Before: Prasutagus, king of the Iceni and an ally of Rome, had left his kingdom jointly to his two daughters and the emperor, hoping to protect it. Tacitus says that after his death the kingdom was plundered by Roman officers and officials, Boudica was flogged, her daughters were raped, and leading Iceni lost their family estates. The neighbouring Trinovantes had their own grievance in the colony at Camulodunum, whose veterans had driven them from their land and whose temple of Claudius stood for permanent Roman rule. Dio adds that money lent to British leaders was suddenly called in.

What happened: After the three towns had burnt, Paulinus, with perhaps 10,000 men, chose a narrow valley with woodland behind him, so that he could only be attacked from the front. Tacitus describes a much larger British army that had brought its families in wagons to watch; the Romans held their ground, then drove forward, and the Britons were trapped against their own wagons. Boudica died soon after, by poison in Tacitus and by illness in Dio. No ancient writer says where the battle was fought and no battlefield has been found, so the map draws a broad region along Watling Street, where Mancetter is the site most often proposed, rather than inventing a pin; compare the certainty here with the burnt towns.

After: Tacitus says Paulinus laid waste the lands of every people that had rebelled or wavered, and that famine followed because the Britons had not sown their crops. The new procurator, Julius Classicianus, reported to Rome that this harshness would only prolong the war, and Paulinus was soon replaced by Petronius Turpilianus, who avoided provoking the Britons; historians usually read this as a turn to a softer policy, though Tacitus scorned it as idleness given the honourable name of peace. London was rebuilt within a few years and grew into the province's main town, and Classicianus's own tombstone was later found there.

## Closing
Three towns burned, and both the Roman writers and the ground say so. Where the revolt ended, nobody knows: the writers name no place, and no battlefield has been found. That gap between what the ground confirms and what only the writers tell us runs through all of Roman Britain.
