# The Claudian invasion, AD 43
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Order: thematic

The emperor Claudius's invasion is described in detail by only one ancient writer, Cassius Dio, who wrote about 170 years later. This journey sets his account beside the ground, and shows how much of the famous story is interpretation. The first three stops all fall in AD 43, so they are ordered by the story, not the calendar.

A final stop moves forward a decade or so, to the new town of London. It shows what the conquest built: a Roman province with its own towns, money and paperwork.

## Stop: richborough-invasion-base-43
Richborough has Claudian defensive ditches and later became the main port of entry to the province, which is why it is usually taken as the landing place. Some historians argue for a landing near Chichester instead. The ditches are evidence of an early base, not proof of where every ship came ashore.

Dio says the army, under Aulus Plautius, crossed in three divisions, but he names no harbour. At Richborough two closely spaced V-shaped ditches, with a rampart on the landward side, ran for at least 650 metres along the old shore of the Wantsum Channel, which then cut off the Isle of Thanet. They were found in excavations of the 1920s and 1930s and are dated by Claudian finds and by lying beneath everything later on the site. The place went on to be a supply base, a port with a great monumental arch and, much later, a fort of the Saxon Shore.

## Stop: river-battle-43
Dio describes a hard two-day battle at a river crossing. He does not name the river; the Medway is a modern identification. The map draws a region around the lower Medway, marked as uncertain.

In Dio's account the Britons camped on the far bank, trusting the river to protect them. Troops trained to swim in armour, probably auxiliaries, crossed and attacked the chariot horses, and a force under the future emperor Vespasian followed. On the second day Gnaeus Hosidius Geta, who was nearly captured, won the battle and was later given triumphal honours. The Medway only follows if the army landed at Richborough; scholars who prefer a Chichester landing suggest other rivers, and no battlefield has been found.

## Stop: camulodunum-surrender-43
Before: Camulodunum had been the stronghold of Cunobelinus, the most powerful British king of the generation before the invasion, and after his death his sons led the resistance. Dio says Claudius was persuaded to intervene by a British ruler, Verica, who had been driven from the island. Historians commonly add that the new emperor, who had no military record, wanted a conquest of his own. After the river battle, Dio says, Plautius halted at the Thames and sent for him.

What happened: Claudius came with reinforcements, crossed the Thames, defeated the Britons and took Camulodunum, then won over a number of peoples, some by agreement and some by force. He stayed in Britain only a few days. His arch in Rome, completed in the early 50s, claimed in its usual restoration that eleven British kings surrendered to him without loss. The inscription survives only as fragments and the numeral is partly restored, so even the number of kings is partly a modern reading; it also does not say where they submitted, which is why the pin marks Colchester in general.

After: Claudius celebrated a triumph at Rome. Within a few years Camulodunum became a colony of retired soldiers with a temple to the deified Claudius, the first Roman town and provincial centre in Britain. Conquering the rest of the island took decades, and the resentment the colony stirred among the Trinovantes made it the rebels' first target in the revolt of AD 60; that link is Tacitus's own explanation, not only a modern one.

## Stop: bloomberg-tablets
A few years after the invasion a new town grew up where the Thames could be bridged: Londinium. Beside the Walbrook stream, waterlogged ground preserved about 405 wooden writing tablets, excavated on the Bloomberg site between 2010 and 2014. Their wax has gone, so the texts are read from the scratches the stylus left in the wood; 87 have been read. One records that a man named Tibullus owed Gratus 105 denarii, dated 8 January AD 57, the earliest dated handwritten document from Britain, and another is addressed to a man in London, the earliest written use of the town's name. Some of the tablets come from the town's first years, before Boudica's rebels burnt it, and they show the new province already lending money, making contracts and writing letters within fifteen years of Claudius's visit.
