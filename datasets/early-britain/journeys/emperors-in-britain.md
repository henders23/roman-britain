# Emperors in Britain
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: rulers-and-kingdoms

Britain was a distant province, yet emperors came here, died here and were made here. This journey follows them across four centuries, from Claudius's short visit for the conquest to the soldiers and generals whom the army in Britain raised to the purple and who then took its troops across the Channel. Notice what kind of source puts each emperor in Britain: a later biography, a historian writing long after, an inscription, or coins. Notice too how, after 383, each usurper who left Britain took more of its army with him.

## Stop: camulodunum-surrender-43
Claudius came in person for the capture of Camulodunum and stayed only days. Dio says the army halted at the Thames and waited for him, and that he arrived with reinforcements, among them elephants, crossed the river, defeated the Britons and took the stronghold of Cunobelinus's successors; he spent only sixteen days in Britain before returning to Rome. His visit was a piece of theatre for Rome, commemorated on a triumphal arch whose inscription survives in fragments. In its usual restoration it says he received the surrender of eleven British kings without loss, though the numeral is partly restored and the text does not say where they submitted. The Senate gave him and his young son the name Britannicus, and Camulodunum became the first Roman colony in the province.

## Stop: hadrian-visit-britain-122
Hadrian's visit rests mainly on one line in a much later imperial biography, which says he came to put many things right and to build a wall to separate Romans from barbarians. He came in 122 in the course of a long tour of the western provinces, after reforming the army in Germany. A military diploma dated 17 July that year helps fix the year: it shows that his friend Platorius Nepos, who probably came with him and went on to build much of the Wall, had just become governor. Where he went is not recorded, so the map draws a broad region over the north, though he will also have passed through the south. Unlike Claudius, he came to set limits to the province rather than to enlarge it.

## Stop: severus-death-york-211
Septimius Severus spent his last years campaigning in the far north and died at York in February 211. He arrived in 208 with his wife Julia Domna, his sons Caracalla and Geta and his court, so for three years York was in effect the capital of the empire. The campaigns against the Caledonians and the Maeatae left marching camps that are hard to date, a base at Carpow on the Tay and granaries at South Shields. His death is fixed by the historians of the period, the day worked out from Dio's figures for his age and reign; Dio, a senator who knew him, also reports his deathbed advice to his sons to agree with each other, enrich the soldiers and ignore everyone else. They did not agree: they took his ashes to Rome, and within a year Caracalla had Geta murdered.

## Stop: carausius-usurpation-286
Before: In the later 3rd century the empire was beset by civil wars and invasions, and Frankish and Saxon raiders were attacking the coasts of the Channel and the North Sea. Diocletian, emperor from 284, made Maximian his colleague to rule the west. Maximian gave Carausius, a Menapian from the coast of what is now Belgium and a man of humble birth, command of a fleet based at Boulogne to clear the sea of pirates.

What happened: Aurelius Victor and Eutropius, writing in the 360s, say Carausius was suspected of letting the raiders strike so that he could seize their plunder, and that when Maximian condemned him to death he took the purple and held Britain. Victor's figure of seven years of rule, ending in 293, points to 286, though 287 is also argued, and no source says where he was proclaimed. His own coins, struck at London and a second British mint, are the best contemporary evidence: some promise a returning golden age in words borrowed from Virgil, and others show him as the partner of Diocletian and Maximian, a claim they never accepted.

After: Maximian's first attempt to recover Britain failed, and in 293 Constantius, newly appointed as Caesar, took Boulogne; soon afterwards Carausius was murdered by his finance minister Allectus, who ruled in his place. In 296 Constantius's forces landed, Allectus was killed in battle, and a gold medallion struck to mark the victory shows Constantius welcomed at the gates of London. The breakaway state had lasted about ten years. Historians link the strengthening of the coastal forts later called the Saxon Shore to these years, but how far each fort was built for or against Carausius is debated.

## Stop: constantine-proclaimed-york-306
Before: Constantius, who had recovered Britain from Allectus, became senior emperor in the west in 305, when Diocletian and Maximian abdicated. His son Constantine, who had served at the eastern court, was passed over in the new succession and held in the east by Galerius. Lactantius says that Constantius, already ill, asked for his son, and that Constantine escaped and rode across the empire to join him. Father and son then campaigned against the Picts in the north of Britain.

What happened: Constantius died at York on 25 July 306, and the army there proclaimed his son emperor. The day is the anniversary Constantine himself later kept for his accession. The Origo Constantini and Eutropius name York; Lactantius and Eusebius, both close to Constantine, describe the deathbed and the acclamation without naming the place. The sources disagree on the title he was first given: Lactantius and Eusebius imply Augustus, but the Origo says Caesar, the rank Galerius was willing to accept. Where in York it happened is unknown.

After: Constantine fought his way to power over the whole empire, defeating Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge in 312 and becoming sole emperor in 324. He ended the persecution of Christians and favoured the church, and in 314 three bishops from Britain attended his council at Arles, the first record of an organised British church. He founded Constantinople in 330. York's part was brief but real: an emperor whose reign changed the religion of the empire was first acclaimed by the army in Britain.

## Stop: magnus-maximus-proclaimed-383
Before: Britain in the later 4th century faced raids from the Picts, the Scots of Ireland and the Saxons; Ammianus describes the attacks of 367 as a barbarian conspiracy, ended by Count Theodosius. Magnus Maximus, a Spaniard, was by the early 380s a senior commander in Britain. The Gallic Chronicle of 452 says he defeated the Picts and Scots in 382. The western emperor Gratian, meanwhile, had lost the army's favour; Zosimus says the soldiers resented his preference for a guard of Alan horsemen.

What happened: In 383 the army in Britain proclaimed Maximus emperor. Zosimus says he resented the promotion of others and stirred up the troops, while Orosius and Sulpicius Severus say the soldiers forced the purple on him; Prosper's chronicle enters it under 384, and no source says where in Britain it happened. Maximus took troops across the Channel, Gratian's army deserted him near Paris, and Gratian was killed at Lyon in August 383.

After: Maximus ruled Britain, Gaul and Spain from Trier and was recognised for a time by the eastern emperor Theodosius I, the son of the Count Theodosius of 367. In 387 he invaded Italy; the next year Theodosius defeated him and had him executed at Aquileia. Gildas, writing in the 6th century, blamed him for stripping Britain of its soldiers and young men, but historians regard this as an exaggeration, since Roman government and some garrisons lasted another generation. Later Welsh tradition remembered him as Macsen Wledig and claimed him as an ancestor of its kings, which is legend, not evidence for 383.

## Stop: constantine-iii-proclaimed-407
The last emperor made in Britain was a soldier named Constantine, raised by the army in 407. Zosimus says the troops first raised a man called Marcus and killed him, then a local man, Gratian, whom they killed after four months, before turning to Constantine; Orosius adds that he was a common soldier chosen for the hope his name inspired. Like Maximus before him, he took troops to Gaul, where Vandals, Alans and Sueves had crossed the Rhine, and he did not return. For a time Honorius recognised him, but his general Gerontius rebelled, and he was captured and executed in 411. No source says where in Britain any of the three was proclaimed, and the sources give different years. Within a few years Roman government in Britain had ended; Zosimus later reports that the Britons expelled Constantine's officials, which is where the journey on the end of Roman Britain picks up the story.

## Closing
Emperors came to Britain to win glory, to fight, and in the end to be made. Most of what we know of their visits comes from historians rather than the ground, with coins and an inscription or two beside them. When the army in Britain made Magnus Maximus emperor in 383, and Constantine III in 407, and followed them to Gaul, the last imperial story here was one of leaving.
