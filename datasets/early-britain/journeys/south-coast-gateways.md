# Gateways on the south coast
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: towns-trade-and-everyday-life

Where did invaders, traders and settlers come ashore? This journey follows the south coast across a thousand years, from the Roman invasion base in Kent to the Norman landing in Sussex. The same stretch of shore kept being a gateway, for armies and for trade.

## Stop: richborough-invasion-base-43
At Richborough in east Kent, Claudian ditches fortified a stretch of shore in AD 43. Richborough then lay on the Wantsum Channel, a sheltered waterway that cut off the Isle of Thanet from the rest of Kent; the coast has changed so much that the site now stands inland. Excavators in the 1920s and 1930s traced two parallel V-shaped ditches with a rampart, running for at least 650 metres along the old shoreline. They are widely taken to be the invasion army's base, and Richborough later became the main port of entry to the province, marked by a great monumental arch. No ancient writer names the landing place, and some scholars argue for a landing, or a second one, near Chichester, the setting of the next stop. In the late Roman period Richborough became one of the coastal forts built against raiders, like Portchester further on.

## Stop: fishbourne-palace
A generation later, near Chichester harbour, a vast palace was built with mosaics and a formal garden. The site was found in 1960 during trenching for a water main, and Barry Cunliffe excavated it through the 1960s. Beneath the palace lay military and timber buildings from the time of the conquest, then an early stone house; the great four-winged building round its garden came later, in the 70s on Cunliffe's dating, though a date in the 90s has also been argued. Some of the earliest mosaics in Britain survive there, and it is the largest Roman residence known north of the Alps. Its owner is unknown, but whoever it was lived on a coast open to the continent. Cunliffe suggested the client king Togidubnus, and others a Roman governor; neither is proven.

## Stop: portchester-shore-fort
In about 285–290 a square fort was built in Portsmouth Harbour to guard against raiders from the sea. Its walls are the best preserved of any Roman fort in northern Europe. They still stand almost to full height round about three and a half hectares, with projecting D-shaped towers. Coins show that it was built after about 268, and its excavator linked it to Carausius, who commanded the Channel fleet from about 285 before seizing power in Britain. It belongs to a chain of coastal forts, from Norfolk round to Hampshire, that a register of about 400 places under the Count of the Saxon Shore. The coast that had welcomed Roman ships now had to be defended against others. The walls were reused for a thousand years, as a Saxon settlement, a Norman castle and a royal fortress.

## Stop: hamwic
Four centuries later, at Southampton on the River Itchen, the West Saxons laid out the trading town archaeologists call Hamwic. It was founded about 700 on a new site, away from the Roman settlement at Bitterne across the river, and covered at least 42 hectares, with a regular grid of gravelled streets and closely packed timber buildings. Excavations since 1946 have found evidence of iron, bone, textile and glass working, imported pottery and glass from northern France and the Low Countries, and many small silver coins. The coast was now a gateway for merchants. The missionary Willibald sailed for the continent from a market called Hamwih in 720. That a West Saxon king, perhaps Ine, planned the town is a common interpretation of its regular layout, not a recorded fact. The Chronicle records a Viking fleet defeated there in 840, and by the later 9th century Hamwic was in decline.

## Stop: william-lands-pevensey-1066
On 28 September 1066 Duke William's fleet landed at Pevensey, and the Normans occupied the old Roman fort there before moving to Hastings. The date comes from the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, which puts the landing on Michaelmas Eve, and the Bayeux Tapestry names Pevensey as it shows the ships arriving and horses being unloaded. Pevensey then stood beside a tidal lagoon, and the coastline has moved so far since that the landing beach cannot be found, so the pin marks the fort of Anderitum. That fort was one of the same late Roman chain of coastal defences as Portchester. The landing was unopposed, because Harold's army was in Yorkshire, which gave William time to settle in before the battle. The story that he stumbled as he came ashore is later tradition, not found in the contemporary sources.

## Closing
For a thousand years the south coast faced the continent, and what came across changed: Roman armies, raiders, merchants, and at last the Normans. Fittingly, William came ashore inside a fort the Romans had built to keep invaders out.
