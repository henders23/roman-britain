# Gateways on the south coast
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: towns-trade-and-everyday-life

Where did invaders, traders and settlers come ashore? This journey follows the south coast across a thousand years, from the Roman invasion base in Kent to the Norman landing in Sussex. The same stretch of shore kept being a gateway, for armies and for trade.

## Stop: richborough-invasion-base-43
At Richborough in east Kent, Claudian ditches fortified a stretch of shore in AD 43. They are widely taken to be the invasion army's base, and Richborough later became the main port of entry to the province.

## Stop: fishbourne-palace
A generation later, near Chichester harbour, a vast palace was built with mosaics and a formal garden. Its owner is unknown, but whoever it was lived on a coast open to the continent.

## Stop: portchester-shore-fort
In about 285–290 a square fort was built in Portsmouth Harbour to guard against raiders from the sea. Its walls are the best preserved of any Roman fort in northern Europe.

## Stop: hamwic
Four centuries later, at Southampton on the River Itchen, the West Saxons laid out the trading town archaeologists call Hamwic. The coast was now a gateway for merchants.

## Stop: william-lands-pevensey-1066
On 28 September 1066 Duke William's fleet landed at Pevensey, and the Normans occupied the old Roman fort there before moving to Hastings.

## Closing
For a thousand years the south coast faced the continent, and what came across changed: Roman armies, raiders, merchants, and at last the Normans. Fittingly, William came ashore inside a fort the Romans had built to keep invaders out.
