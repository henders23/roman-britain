# Britain and the wider world
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: towns-trade-and-everyday-life

Britain is an island, but it was never cut off. Who was it trading with after Rome left? This journey follows goods that travelled a long way: Mediterranean pottery in the west, eastern silver in a royal grave, an English king's copy of an Islamic coin, and a Viking hoard gathered from half of Europe.

## Stop: tintagel-trading-site
On the Tintagel headland, excavation found buildings and a large quantity of pottery imported from the eastern Mediterranean in the centuries after Rome. The finds include red-slipped bowls from the Aegean and amphorae, the storage jars of the eastern Mediterranean, in larger quantity than at any other site in Britain or Ireland. Most are conventionally dated to the late fifth and sixth centuries, though some types have recently been re-dated later. Ralegh Radford, digging in the 1930s, took the buildings for a Celtic monastery; excavations since, including Glasgow University's in the 1990s, point instead to a secular centre of power, which is itself an interpretation. Wine and oil were still reaching Cornwall by sea. The castle ruins visitors see are 13th-century, and the Arthur stories belong to later literature, not to this evidence.

## Stop: dinas-powys-hillfort
Near Cardiff, a wealthy household imported wine, pottery and glass from the Mediterranean and Gaul in the fifth to seventh centuries. Leslie Alcock dug the small hillfort between 1954 and 1958, expecting an Iron Age site, and found sherds like those from Tintagel, fragments of Frankish and Anglo-Saxon glass, and moulds and crucibles from making fine metalwork. He read it as the court of a local ruler, but no text names the place, and links with the kingdom of Glywysing are only a suggestion. New radiocarbon dates published in 2023 have changed the site's story, showing that ramparts Alcock thought Norman were built in the sixth and seventh centuries. Long-distance trade was a mark of status, and imported wine and fine metalwork were probably among the gifts with which a lord bound followers to him.

## Stop: sutton-hoo-mound1
The ship burial at Sutton Hoo held silver from the eastern Mediterranean and a purse of Frankish gold coins, alongside gold and garnet made closer to home. The silver includes a great dish stamped in the reign of the emperor Anastasius, who died in 518, so it was old when it was buried. The purse held 37 gold coins from Frankish mints, and they date the burial: it cannot be earlier than the latest of them, about 613 or the 620s. The burial is often linked to King Rædwald of East Anglia, who died about 624, but that is an interpretation. Treasure probably buried with an East Anglian king came from across Europe. The routes ran east and south now, across the North Sea and through Francia, rather than along the Atlantic coast that served Tintagel and Dinas Powys.

## Stop: offa-gold-dinar
A unique gold coin copies an Islamic dinar of 773–774, Arabic inscription and all, but adds the Latin words OFFA REX for the Mercian king Offa. Its model was a dinar of the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur, and the Latin is set upside down relative to the Arabic, which suggests the engraver could not read the script he copied. It names no mint, and it was first recorded in Rome in 1841, so where it was struck and how it reached Italy are unknown. It may have been made for trade, since gold dinars were the leading currency of the Mediterranean, or for the 365 mancuses (a gold unit of account) a year that Offa promised the pope, or simply for display. Why it was made is debated, but its model came from the Islamic world. Charlemagne wrote to Offa about the merchants and goods crossing the Channel between their kingdoms, a reminder that Mercia's trading towns faced the continent.

## Stop: cuerdale-hoard
Around 905–910 about 40 kilograms of silver was buried by the Ribble in Lancashire. Workmen repairing the riverbank found it in May 1840 in a lead chest: some 7,000 coins and more than 1,000 pieces of ingots, ornaments and cut-up silver. Its coins come from Viking York, East Anglia, the Frankish empire and the Islamic world: the Viking world's trade, in one chest. The hacked jewellery and ingots show silver being used by weight as well as by coin, and much of it seems to have reached England across the Irish Sea. A common view links the hoard to the Norse driven out of Dublin in 902, or to a war-chest for a campaign, but the hoard itself cannot say whose it was.

## Closing
After Rome, Britain's links to the wider world changed but never broke: first to the Mediterranean by sea, then across the Frankish lands, and later through Viking trade routes reaching as far as the Islamic world. Pottery, silver and coins are the evidence; few written sources mention this trade at all.
