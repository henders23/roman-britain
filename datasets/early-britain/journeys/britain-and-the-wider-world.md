# Britain and the wider world
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: towns-trade-and-everyday-life

Britain is an island, but it was never cut off. Who was it trading with after Rome left? This journey follows goods that travelled a long way: Mediterranean pottery in the west, eastern silver in a royal grave, an English king's copy of an Islamic coin, and a Viking hoard gathered from half of Europe.

## Stop: tintagel-trading-site
On the Tintagel headland, excavation found buildings and a large quantity of pottery imported from the eastern Mediterranean in the centuries after Rome. Wine and oil were still reaching Cornwall by sea.

## Stop: dinas-powys-hillfort
Near Cardiff, a wealthy household imported wine, pottery and glass from the Mediterranean and Gaul in the fifth to seventh centuries. Long-distance trade was a mark of status.

## Stop: sutton-hoo-mound1
The ship burial at Sutton Hoo held silver from the eastern Mediterranean and a purse of Frankish gold coins, alongside gold and garnet made closer to home. An East Anglian king's treasure came from across Europe.

## Stop: offa-gold-dinar
A unique gold coin copies an Islamic dinar of 773–774, Arabic inscription and all, but adds the Latin words OFFA REX for the Mercian king Offa. Why it was made is debated, but its model came from the Islamic world.

## Stop: cuerdale-hoard
Around 905–910 about 40 kilograms of silver was buried by the Ribble in Lancashire. Its coins come from Viking York, East Anglia, the Frankish empire and the Islamic world: the Viking world's trade, in one chest.

## Closing
After Rome, Britain's links to the wider world changed but never broke: first to the Mediterranean by sea, then across the Frankish lands, and later through Viking trade routes reaching as far as the Islamic world. Pottery, silver and coins are the evidence; few written sources mention this trade at all.
