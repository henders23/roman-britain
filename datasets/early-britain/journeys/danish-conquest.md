# From Æthelred to Cnut
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: vikings-and-england

How did England fall to a Danish king a generation after Edgar's peace? When Edgar died in 975 the English kingdom was united, wealthy and well governed. Within forty years it had paid huge sums to Viking armies, its king had fled abroad, and a Danish king ruled it. This journey follows that collapse through a defeat in Essex, a massacre ordered by the king, a Danish king's arrival on the Trent and the battle that decided the war, and ends with the return of the English line that led to 1066.

Almost all of the written story comes from one set of annals in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, probably written up after Æthelred's reign had ended in failure. Its gloomy tone has shaped how he has been judged ever since, so look out for where the verdict is the chronicler's rather than the evidence's.

## Stop: battle-of-maldon-991
Before: Edgar's death in 975 was followed by a disputed succession: his elder son Edward was murdered at Corfe in 978, and his younger son Æthelred, still a boy, became king. Viking raids on England began again in 980, at first small attacks on places such as Southampton, Thanet and Cheshire. In 991 a larger fleet arrived; version A of the Chronicle says Olaf came with 93 ships to Folkestone, Sandwich and Ipswich, and his identification with Olaf Tryggvason, later king of Norway, is an inference.

What happened: The Chronicle records that Ealdorman Byrhtnoth of Essex fought the raiders at Maldon on the Blackwater estuary and was killed. The day, 10 or 11 August, comes from church calendars that commemorate him. The Old English poem The Battle of Maldon, which survives only in a transcript made before the manuscript burned in 1731, has Byrhtnoth allowing the Vikings to cross a tidal causeway to fight on the mainland and his retainers dying beside him; it is a literary work, and its speeches are not a record. Its causeway is usually identified with the one to Northey Island, and the mainland opposite is a registered battlefield, but that is widely accepted rather than proved.

After: In the same year, on the advice of Archbishop Sigeric, the English for the first time paid the Danes to go away: 10,000 pounds. The Chronicle records the payments rising over the next twenty years, to 48,000 pounds in 1012, raised by a national tax later called Danegeld; the raiders took the money and came back. Whether paying was weakness or a sensible way to buy time is debated by historians, and much of the gloom comes from the chronicler, writing with hindsight. The poem has shaped how later generations imagined Anglo-Saxon loyalty and heroism, which is a literary legacy rather than evidence for the battle.

## Stop: st-brices-day-massacre-1002
Eleven years after the first tribute, Æthelred turned on Danes inside his kingdom. On St Brice's Day, 13 November 1002, the Chronicle says, he ordered all the Danish men in England to be killed, having been told they meant to kill him and his councillors and seize the kingdom; it names no places and gives no numbers. The fullest account is a charter of 1004 renewing the lost title deeds of St Frideswide's minster in Oxford: Danes there, trying to escape, broke into the church and barricaded themselves in, and the church was burned with its books and ornaments. How widely the order was carried out elsewhere is unknown, and it cannot have been aimed at the whole long-settled population of the Danelaw. In 2008 excavation at St John's College, about a kilometre north of the cathedral, found the remains of some 35 young men thrown into the ditch of a prehistoric henge, many stabbed repeatedly and some bones charred. Isotope analysis suggests they were of Scandinavian origin, but the radiocarbon dates favour the 10th century and they may have been captured raiders who were executed, so the link to the massacre is possible, not proven. That the massacre provoked King Swein of Denmark's later attacks is an interpretation, not something the Chronicle states.

## Stop: sweyn-forkbeard-gainsborough-1013
The attacks grew worse. From 1009 to 1012 Thorkell's army ravaged the south and east, and in 1012 it killed Archbishop Ælfheah of Canterbury at Greenwich after he refused to let himself be ransomed. In the summer of 1013 King Swein of Denmark sailed into the Humber and up the Trent to Gainsborough, where Earl Uhtred and the Northumbrians, the people of Lindsey and the Five Boroughs submitted to him. Leaving the ships and hostages with his son Cnut, he marched south; Oxford, Winchester and the western thegns at Bath submitted, and in the end London too. Æthelred sent his queen Emma and their sons to her brother in Normandy and followed them after Christmas, and the Chronicle says the whole nation regarded Swein as full king. He died at Gainsborough on 3 February 1014, after about five weeks as king. Where in or near the town he was based is not known; local traditions of a camp at Castle Hills and a royal hall on the site of the Old Hall are unconfirmed, so the pin marks the town only.

## Stop: battle-of-assandun-1016
Before: After Swein's death the fleet chose Cnut as king, but the English councillors recalled Æthelred, and Cnut sailed back to Denmark. He returned in 1015, and the kingdom split: Ealdorman Eadric of Mercia went over to him, and Æthelred's son Edmund, later called Ironside, raised forces of his own. Æthelred died in April 1016, and Edmund, chosen king by those in London, fought Cnut through the summer in a series of battles, at Penselwood, Sherston and elsewhere.

What happened: On 18 October 1016 Edmund overtook the Danish army at Assandun in Essex. The Chronicle, written within a few years, says Eadric fled the field with his men, as he had done before, and Cnut won; among the many English leaders killed it names Bishop Eadnoth and Ulfcytel of East Anglia. Where Assandun was is disputed: Ashingdon near Rochford is the long-standing identification, Ashdon near Saffron Walden has also been argued for, and other candidates outside Essex are being tested, so the map draws a halo over both Essex sites.

After: The two kings met at Alney near Deerhurst and divided the kingdom, Edmund keeping Wessex, but Edmund died on 30 November 1016 and Cnut was accepted as king of all England in 1017. He married Æthelred's widow Emma, raised a vast tax to pay off his fleet, and later ruled Denmark and Norway as well, so that for two decades England was the centre of what historians call a North Sea empire; most regard this as the culmination of the attacks that had begun again in the 980s. After Cnut died in 1035 his sons Harold and Harthacnut reigned briefly, and in 1042 Edward, Æthelred and Emma's son, who had grown up in exile in Normandy, became king. The return of the English line brought with it the Norman connection that would matter in 1066.

## Stop: edward-dies-harold-crowned-1066
Edward reigned for almost twenty-four years, and the great men around him were largely made under Cnut: above all Godwin, earl of Wessex, whose daughter Edith Edward married. Edward had no children. He died at Westminster on 5 January 1066, a few days after his new abbey church there was consecrated, and was buried in it the next day; on the same day Harold Godwinson, earl of Wessex, was crowned king. English sources say the dying king entrusted the kingdom to Harold, while Norman writers say Edward had long before promised it to Duke William of Normandy, Emma's great-nephew. The Scandinavian past had not gone either: Harald Hardrada of Norway also claimed the throne, by Norse tradition through an agreement made between Harthacnut and the Norwegian king Magnus. The journey 1066 follows what happened next.

## Closing
The stops showed a united, wealthy kingdom worn down over a generation: a defeat at Maldon followed by tribute, a massacre that made enemies without removing them, a Danish king accepted from the Trent to London, and the battle at Assandun that gave England to Cnut. The answer to the opening question is that renewed attack, heavy taxes and divided English leaders together brought the kingdom down, but much of the blame laid on Æthelred is the chronicler's hindsight. Under Cnut the kingdom itself survived intact, and the return of Æthelred's son Edward in 1042 brought back the English line, and with it the Norman tie, that led to 1066.
