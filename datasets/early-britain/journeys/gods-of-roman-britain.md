# Gods of Roman Britain
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Order: chronological

Roman Britain had no single religion. Local gods were given Roman names, soldiers brought cults from the eastern empire, and in the fourth century Christianity arrived in villas and treasures. This journey visits seven sacred places and asks, at each one, what the evidence shows people actually did there.

## Stop: aquae-sulis
At Bath a British goddess, Sulis, was joined to the Roman Minerva at the hot spring. Worshippers left altars, coins and curse tablets: Roman forms of devotion around a native sacred place.

## Stop: uley-mercury-temple
On a Cotswold hilltop, a temple of Mercury stood on a site already sacred before the Romans came. Over a hundred curse tablets ask the god to recover stolen property and punish the thieves.

## Stop: carrawburgh-mithraeum
Beside a fort on Hadrian's Wall, officers built a small, dark temple to Mithras, a god whose mysteries were open only to initiated men. Three altars set up by commanding officers stood at its far end.

## Stop: walbrook-mithraeum
In London a larger Mithraeum stood by the Walbrook stream. Its discovery in 1954 drew huge crowds, and its sculptures, including the head of Mithras, are among the finest from Roman Britain.

## Stop: lydney-nodens-temple
At Lydney, above the Severn, a temple to Nodens, a British god of healing, had a guest house and baths for visitors. Its date is argued over, which matters: some place it in the late fourth century, when Christianity was the emperors' religion.

## Stop: water-newton-treasure
Silver vessels and plaques marked with the chi-rho show Christians worshipping together in the fourth century. Some plaques are shaped like the votive leaves offered to pagan gods: old habits in a new faith.

## Stop: lullingstone-house-church
In a villa in Kent, a room was painted with Christian symbols and figures at prayer, while a pagan shrine elsewhere in the house may still have been in use. Belief in Roman Britain was rarely one thing at a time.
