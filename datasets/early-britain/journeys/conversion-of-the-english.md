# The conversion of the English
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Order: chronological

How did the English kingdoms become Christian, and whose Christianity was it? Missionaries came from Rome to Kent and from Iona to Northumbria, Frankish clergy worked at royal courts, and kings were baptised, fell back, fought and changed sides. The British Christians already living in the island play almost no part in the story as it has come down to us. This journey follows the process from Augustine's landing in Kent to the book that tells it.

That book is Bede's Ecclesiastical History, the source for nearly every stop, so look out at each one for what rests on his word and what on letters, laws, burials and the record of church councils.

## Stop: augustine-canterbury-597
Before: Pope Gregory the Great sent the mission in 596, choosing Augustine, prior of his own monastery in Rome, to lead about forty monks. Bede says they lost heart on the way through Gaul and that Gregory wrote urging them on. In Kent, King Æthelberht was the strongest ruler among the southern English, and he was married to Bertha, a Christian daughter of the Frankish king Charibert, who had come with her own bishop, Liudhard. A Christian queen at a pagan court, and close ties with Christian Francia, made Kent the obvious place to begin.

What happened: The party landed on the Isle of Thanet in 597. Bede says Æthelberht met them in the open air, heard them out and gave them a house in Canterbury, where they worshipped in Bertha's church of St Martin east of the city. The king was baptised soon after, though Bede gives no date, and Gregory wrote in 598 that more than ten thousand English had been baptised at Christmas. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle also enters the arrival under 597; the outline is secure, but details such as the meeting on Thanet come only through Bede, who drew on Canterbury's letters and traditions.

After: Augustine founded a cathedral in Canterbury and a monastery outside the walls, and new bishoprics followed at Rochester and London in 604. Æthelberht issued a law code, the earliest law written in English, which opens with compensation for theft from the church; it survives only in a 12th-century copy. Gregory had planned archbishops at London and York, but Canterbury kept the senior see. The new faith depended on kings: when Æthelberht died in 616 his son Eadbald at first refused baptism, the pagan sons of the East Saxon king drove Bishop Mellitus out of London, and Mellitus and Justus of Rochester fled to Gaul. Eadbald was soon converted and Justus returned, but London had no bishop again for decades.

## Stop: sutton-hoo-mound1
In East Anglia the conversion was half-hearted at best. Bede says King Rædwald was baptised in Kent, probably at Æthelberht's court, but on returning home was persuaded by his wife and advisers to keep one temple with two altars, one for Christ and a small one for sacrifices to the old gods; a later East Anglian king told Bede he had seen the temple as a boy. The great ship burial under Mound 1, dated by its Frankish coins to around 613 or the 620s, is often linked to Rædwald, who died about 624, but nothing found names him. The rite itself, a ship hauled into a mound with weapons, feasting gear and treasure, belongs to the old ways. Among the grave goods are silver bowls decorated with crosses and a pair of silver spoons inscribed in Greek letters, which some read as the names Saul and Paul and connect with baptism, though that reading is disputed. Christian and pagan objects lying side by side fit Bede's picture of a court that had taken Christ on without giving up its gods.

## Stop: aidan-lindisfarne-635
Northumbria's first conversion came from Kent. Edwin, the king Rædwald had helped to the throne, married Æthelberht's Christian daughter Æthelburh, who brought the Roman missionary Paulinus north, and Edwin was baptised at York in 627. When Edwin was killed in 633 by Cadwallon of Gwynedd and Penda of Mercia, Paulinus fled south with the queen and the mission fell apart. Oswald, who had spent his exile among the Irish and been baptised in Iona's tradition, won the kingdom at Heavenfield in 634 and sent to Iona, not Canterbury, for a bishop. Aidan was given Lindisfarne, a tidal island in sight of the royal fortress at Bamburgh, and Bede describes him walking from village to village with the king as his interpreter. Bede gives no year, so 635 is worked out from Oswald's accession and from Aidan's death in 651. The northern English were converted largely by Irish-trained monks, who kept Iona's customs, including its way of dating Easter.

## Stop: battle-of-the-winwaed-655
Before: Penda of Mercia, a pagan, had helped to kill Edwin in 633 and killed Oswald in battle in 642, and for years afterwards he raided Northumbria. Yet he did not forbid Christianity: his son Peada, ruler of the Middle Angles, was baptised in 653 on marrying a daughter of Oswiu of Northumbria and brought four priests home with him, among them Cedd and Diuma, and Bede says Penda did not stop them preaching even in Mercia. Oswiu, Oswald's brother, tried to buy Penda off with treasure, and when that failed vowed that if he won he would give his infant daughter Ælfflæd to the religious life and twelve estates for monasteries.

What happened: On 15 November 655 Oswiu's much smaller army defeated Penda beside a river Bede calls the Winwaed, in the district of Loidis around Leeds. Bede says Penda and nearly all his thirty royal commanders were killed, among them Æthelhere of the East Angles, and that more of the fleeing men drowned in the flooded river than died in the fighting. The Welsh Historia Brittonum tells it from the British side as the slaughter of the field of Gai, where the British kings who had marched with Penda died, except Cadafael of Gwynedd, who slipped away the night before. The river has never been identified, so the map shows a halo over the Leeds district, and 656 is also possible for the year.

After: Oswiu became overlord of Mercia and much of southern England, and Peada ruled southern Mercia as a Christian king, with Diuma as the first bishop of the Mercians. Peada was murdered in 656 and Mercian nobles made Wulfhere, another of Penda's sons, king in 658, but Wulfhere too was a Christian, and Mercia did not return to the old gods. Ælfflæd was given to Abbess Hild, whose monastery at Whitby is the next stop. Historians generally treat the battle as the high point of Northumbrian power and as decisive for the conversion of the Midlands, but that is a reading of Bede's narrative.

## Stop: synod-of-whitby-664
Before: By the 660s Northumbria held two Christian traditions under one roof. Lindisfarne and its daughter houses followed Iona's reckoning of Easter, while Oswiu's queen Eanflæd, Edwin's daughter, brought up in Kent, followed Rome's, so that Bede says the king could be feasting at Easter while the queen was still fasting for Lent. Wilfrid, a young Northumbrian who had studied in Rome and Gaul, had won the support of Oswiu's son Alhfrith, who gave him the monastery at Ripon after its Irish-trained monks refused to change their ways.

What happened: In 664 Oswiu called the two sides to Abbess Hild's monastery at Streanæshalch, generally identified with Whitby. Bishop Colman of Lindisfarne spoke for Iona's practice and Wilfrid for Rome's, on behalf of the Frankish bishop Agilbert, who did not speak English. Bede says Oswiu chose Rome because its tradition claimed the authority of St Peter, who held the keys of heaven. The two accounts, by Stephen of Ripon in his life of Wilfrid and by Bede in 731, were written two and three generations later, and the speeches are their reconstructions.

After: Colman withdrew to Iona and then Ireland with the Irish monks and about thirty English followers, and Northumbria's bishops were from then on consecrated in the Roman way. The same year a great plague killed many, among them Archbishop Deusdedit of Canterbury, which left the southern church without a leader. Iona itself accepted the Roman Easter only in 716. Bede made Whitby a turning point, and it is often used, perhaps too simply, as the moment the English chose Rome over Iona; historians still debate how far it was about church custom and how far about rivalry within the royal family.

## Stop: synod-of-hertford-672
Before: Deusdedit's chosen successor died in Rome before he could be consecrated, and Pope Vitalian chose instead Theodore, a Greek monk from Tarsus in Asia Minor, then about sixty-six. He was consecrated in Rome in 668, and arrived in Canterbury in May 669 with the African-born abbot Hadrian. Bede says Theodore travelled through every part of the island where the English lived, putting right irregular consecrations, and that together the two men ran a school at Canterbury where pupils learned Greek as well as Latin.

What happened: On 24 September, probably in 672, Theodore met the bishops of the English kingdoms at Herutford, usually identified with Hertford. Bede copies what appears to be the synod's own record: the bishops of the East Angles, Rochester, the West Saxons and the Mercians were present, and Wilfrid of the Northumbrians sent representatives. Theodore read out ten chapters from a book of church canons, which required Easter to be kept on the Roman reckoning, kept bishops to their own dioceses and monks to their own monasteries, called for a synod each year at Clofesho, and made marriage lawful and permanent. The record's own reckoning points to 672, though Bede puts it in 673; nothing has been found on the ground at Hertford.

After: A proposal to make more bishoprics as the number of Christians grew was put off at Hertford, but Theodore carried it out over the next decade: he divided East Anglia, split Mercia and, in 678, divided Northumbria, against Wilfrid's bitter opposition. He died in 690, having given the English church a single structure of bishops, dioceses and councils under Canterbury. Historians treat Hertford as the start of one English church across several kingdoms, two and a half centuries before there was one English kingdom; that is an interpretation, but a widely shared one.

## Stop: bede-historia-731
Bede finished his Ecclesiastical History of the English People at Jarrow in 731, and almost everything on this journey comes to us through it: Augustine's landing, Rædwald's two altars, Aidan's mission, the Winwaed, Whitby and the record of Hertford. Two copies made within a few years of his death in 735 survive, so the text is secure, and he usually says where his information came from. His answer to the question of whose Christianity it was is plain in the book's shape: the conversion begins with Gregory and Canterbury and is completed by unity with Rome, while he writes of Aidan and the Irish monks with real warmth and blames the Britons for never having preached to the English. That shape is Bede's interpretation, and historians now give more weight to the Irish, Frankish and British contributions he played down. In 734 he wrote to Bishop Ecgberht of York urging more bishops and complaining of false monasteries, and in 735 Ecgberht became archbishop, so that Gregory's plan for a northern archbishopric was finally carried out.
