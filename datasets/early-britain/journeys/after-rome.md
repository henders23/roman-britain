# From Britannia to the English kingdoms
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: rulers-and-kingdoms

In 410 Britain was a Roman province with towns, villas and a Latin-speaking elite. By about 600 the east and south were ruled by English kings and the west by British ones, speaking different languages and, at first, following different religions. How did Roman Britain become a land of English and British kingdoms, and how little do we know about it?

This is the least documented stretch of British history. The writers who describe it, Gildas in the sixth century and Bede in the eighth, wrote long after and with their own purposes, so this journey leans on graves, stones and pottery as much as on texts. At each stop, notice how wide the dates and places on the map are: they are a fair measure of what we do not know.

## Stop: rescript-of-honorius-410
The story starts with the end of Roman government. Zosimus, writing about a century later, says that the Britons had expelled the Roman officials after the usurper Constantine III took the army to Gaul, and that in 410 the emperor Honorius told the cities of Britain to look to their own defence. Even that sentence may be about Bruttium in Italy rather than Britain, so the map draws it faintly. What is certain is that no Roman army or governor returned, and that within a generation towns, villas, coin use and the mass-produced pottery of the province had all but disappeared. The Britons were left to govern and defend themselves, and the following stops show some of who was living in Britain as they did.

## Stop: silchester-ogham-stone
At Silchester, the Roman town of Calleva, a stone column from a Roman portico was cut with ogham, an alphabet of strokes devised in Ireland, and later dropped upside down into a well. It was found in 1893 and names a man read as Ebicatos or Tebicatos. Ogham stones are common in Ireland and in western Britain, but this is the only one found east of the Severn, which suggests that Irish-speaking people lived in the town as Roman rule ended. Its date is debated: ogham is usually placed in the 5th to 7th centuries, but radiocarbon dates from the well point to the years around 400, and later excavation showed a cottage beside it still occupied in the 5th century. Either way, Silchester shows a Roman town still lived in after 410, by a population more mixed than the written sources suggest.

## Stop: spong-hill-cemetery
At Spong Hill in Norfolk, a community began burying its dead in a new way within a generation of the end of Roman rule. Excavation between 1972 and 1984 uncovered the whole cemetery: more than two thousand cremations in pottery urns, often stamped and decorated, and 57 inhumation graves. The urns and the combs and brooches burnt with the dead have close parallels in cemeteries on the North Sea coast of northern Germany, which links the people here with that region. Recent analysis dates the first burials to the early decades of the 5th century, earlier than Bede's date of 449 for the coming of the Saxons, though cremations cannot be closely dated and the start rests on the styles of the objects. Who these people were, incomers, Britons adopting new customs or a mixture of both, is exactly the question the next stop raises.

## Stop: gallic-chronicle-saxons-441
Before: After 410 the Britons governed themselves. Gildas, writing in the 6th century, says that under attack from the Picts and Scots a 'proud tyrant' and his council invited Saxons into Britain as paid defenders, and that these later turned on their hosts. Bede, writing in the 8th century, names the ruler Vortigern and the Saxon leaders Hengest and Horsa, and dates their arrival to about 449. Graves like those at Spong Hill suggest that people from the North Sea coast were settling in eastern Britain well before that.

What happened: An anonymous chronicler writing in southern Gaul, who finished his work in 452, entered under a year equal to about 440–441 that the British provinces, long troubled by disasters, had been brought under the power of the Saxons. It is the only near-contemporary statement that Saxons had taken control in Britain, but it is one line, its year depends on how the manuscripts are read, and some scholars think the chronicle was reworked later. A writer in Gaul probably knew most about the parts of Britain facing the Continent, so few historians read it as the fall of the whole island.

After: The entry stands here for the Anglo-Saxon settlement as a whole, and almost everything about that settlement is debated. Historians have argued for a mass migration that replaced the Britons in the east, and for a small warrior elite whose language and customs the Britons adopted; most now see something between the two that varied from region to region. Ancient DNA studies, the largest published in 2022, show substantial migration from the North Sea coast of the Continent into eastern England, with incomers and local people living, marrying and being buried side by side, but how many came, and how many Britons remained, is still argued. Some field systems and estates seem to continue from Roman times, and British speech and kingdoms lasted longest in the west, which is where the journey goes next.

## Stop: tintagel-trading-site
On the north coast of Cornwall, British rulers lived in a very different world from the cremating communities of the east. Tintagel received more pottery from the eastern Mediterranean than any other site in Britain or Ireland, mostly in the late 5th and 6th centuries: fine red bowls from the Aegean and amphorae that had carried wine and oil. Its buildings are now read as a secular high-status settlement, perhaps a centre of the kingdom of Dumnonia in the south-west, though that is interpretation. Gildas, writing in this period, rebukes five British kings by name, one of them Constantine of Dumnonia, which shows that British kingdoms were already established in the west. The legend that Arthur was born here begins with Geoffrey of Monmouth in the 12th century and is kept off the map.

## Stop: battle-of-mons-badonicus
Before: Gildas describes the Saxon revolt as a disaster: towns burned, Britons killed or enslaved, and some fleeing overseas. Then, he says, the survivors rallied under Ambrosius Aurelianus, a man of Roman family, and after that victory went now to one side and now to the other. Gildas is a preacher condemning the sins of his people, not a historian, so this outline is the shape he gives the story rather than a record of events.

What happened: The struggle ended, Gildas says, with the siege of Mount Badon, the Britons' last and greatest victory, in the year of his own birth, forty-four years before he wrote. His Latin is ambiguous and he probably wrote between about 500 and 550, so the battle is placed somewhere between about 480 and 520; the Welsh annals give 516 or 518, but that entry was compiled in the 10th century. Nobody knows where Badon was: the hills around Bath, Liddington Castle in Wiltshire and Badbury Rings in Dorset are among many proposals, none with battle archaeology, so the map draws a large region over the south. Gildas names no commander. Arthur is first made the victor in the Historia Brittonum, written in Gwynedd about 829–30, and in the later Welsh annals; the pack treats this as later legend and excludes it.

After: Gildas wrote in a time of peace from foreign wars, though he complains of civil wars and wicked British kings. Historians often take Badon to explain why English expansion seems to have paused in the early 6th century, but that is an inference from one sentence and from patterns in the archaeology. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, compiled in the late 9th century, records English victories over the Britons later in the 6th century, among them Dyrham in 577, after which it says Gloucester, Cirencester and Bath were taken. By then the map of Britain was a map of kingdoms.

## Stop: prittlewell-burial
By about 600 the English kingdoms had emerged, with kings rich enough to be buried like this. At Prittlewell in Essex, in 2003, archaeologists found an undisturbed timber chamber dated by radiocarbon and coins to about 580–605. The man buried there had a lyre, a folding stool, drinking horns, a gold buckle, two Frankish gold coins, a hanging bowl and a flagon from the eastern Mediterranean, and two small gold-foil crosses lay where his eyes would have been. He is usually thought to have belonged to the East Saxon ruling family, though who he was is unknown. In 597 Augustine's mission reached the neighbouring kingdom of Kent, and from then on Bede's history lets us name kings and count years again. The two centuries this journey has crossed are known mostly from graves like this one.

## Closing
The stops showed the end of Roman government in 410, a Roman town still lived in by a mixed population, new burial customs brought from the North Sea coast, a single line from a chronicler in Gaul, British rulers in the west trading with the Mediterranean, a victory remembered by Gildas, and by about 600 an English king buried in a timber chamber full of treasure. The honest answer to the opening question is that we know the outcome far better than the process: Britain was divided between English and British kingdoms by 600, but how many incomers there were, how the Britons of the east fared, and what part battles played are still argued over, and most of the evidence is graves, stones and pottery rather than written history.
