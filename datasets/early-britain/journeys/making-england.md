# The making of England
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: vikings-and-england

When, and how, did England become one kingdom? In 878 the kings of Wessex were fighting for survival; by 973 Alfred's great-grandson Edgar was consecrated at Bath as king of the English and received pledges from the other rulers of Britain. This journey follows that change through a battle, a fortified town, a meeting of kings, a second battle, an expulsion and a coronation.

There is no single answer. Historians have pointed to 927, when Æthelstan took York, to 954, when the last king of York was driven out, and to Edgar's reign, when the kingdom was governed from the centre; look at each stop for what was gained, what was lost again and what lasted.

## Stop: battle-of-edington-878
The story begins with Wessex close to defeat. Since 865 the great army had overthrown Northumbria and East Anglia and divided Mercia, and Guthrum's attack on Chippenham at midwinter 878 drove Alfred into the Somerset marshes. In May he gathered the men of Somerset, Wiltshire and part of Hampshire and defeated Guthrum at Ethandun, usually identified as Edington in Wiltshire, though no battlefield finds confirm it. Guthrum was baptised and settled in East Anglia, and a treaty later set a frontier along the Thames, the Lea and Watling Street. In 886 Alfred took control of London, and the Chronicle says that all the English not under Danish rule then submitted to him; his charters began to call him king of the Anglo-Saxons, a new title for a new kind of kingdom.

## Stop: wallingford-burh
After Edington Alfred built a network of fortified places, or burhs, so that no part of Wessex lay far from a refuge and a garrison. The Burghal Hidage lists some thirty of them with the hides of land assigned to man their walls; Wallingford, at a crossing of the Thames, was given 2,400, one of the largest figures, and its rectangular bank and ditch still survive best on the west side of the town. Neither the building of the defences nor the date of the list is recorded, so the map shows a window from 878 to 919. The burhs became towns with markets and mints. They also became a way to advance: in the 910s Alfred's son Edward the Elder and his daughter Æthelflæd, Lady of the Mercians, built new burhs as they pushed into the lands under Scandinavian rule, the next step on this journey.

## Stop: eamont-submission-927
Before: Edward and Æthelflæd defeated a Northumbrian Viking army at Tettenhall in 910, and over the next decade took the Scandinavian strongholds south of the Humber one by one: Æthelflæd took Derby and Leicester, and Edward the armies of East Anglia and the southern Danelaw. By 920 the Chronicle says the rulers of York, Bamburgh, the Scots and the Strathclyde Britons had chosen Edward as father and lord. His son Æthelstan, king from 924, married his sister to Sihtric, king of York, and when Sihtric died in 927 Æthelstan took the city.

What happened: On 12 July 927 Æthelstan met the kings of the north and west at Eamont, near Penrith, where they made peace with pledges and oaths, renounced idolatry and accepted his overlordship. The only near-contemporary record is the northern D version of the Chronicle, which names Hywel of the West Welsh, Constantine of the Scots, Owain of Gwent and Ealdred of Bamburgh; some of these identifications are uncertain, and William of Malmesbury, writing in the 12th century, names Owain as king of the Cumbrians and places part of the meeting at Dacre. Eamont lay on the frontier between Northumbria and Cumbria, and the exact meeting place is unknown, so the map draws a halo over Eamont Bridge and Dacre.

After: Æthelstan was the first king to rule all the English kingdoms, and his charters and coins soon called him king of the English and, at times, king of all Britain. Many historians treat 927 as the moment a single English kingdom was formed. That is a judgement on what followed, and the rulers who swore at Eamont did not stay bound for long: Æthelstan invaded Scotland in 934, and three years later the next stop's coalition marched against him.

## Stop: battle-of-brunanburh-937
Before: Æthelstan's claim to lordship over Britain pressed hard on his neighbours, and his invasion of Scotland by land and sea in 934 showed what it could mean. Olaf Guthfrithson, king of Dublin, whose father had been driven from York in 927, wanted the city back. In 937 he combined with Constantine II of Alba, one of the kings who had sworn at Eamont, and with the Strathclyde Britons.

What happened: Æthelstan and his brother Edmund defeated the allies at Brunanburh. The Chronicle marks the victory with a poem, which says that five young kings and seven of Olaf's earls lay dead, that Constantine lost his son, and that Olaf fled by ship over Dingesmere to Dublin; Irish, Welsh and later English chronicles also record the battle. None locates it securely. Bromborough on the Wirral, whose name is widely thought to derive from Brunanburh, is the leading candidate, and 10th-century finds from nearby fields have been offered in support but are not yet fully published; Burnswark, Lanchester and sites in Yorkshire and Lancashire have also been proposed.

After: The victory confirmed Æthelstan's dominance, but it did not settle the north. When he died in 939 Olaf was accepted as king at York and took the Five Boroughs of the east Midlands, and Edmund needed until 944 to win them back. Later writers have presented Brunanburh as the battle that made England, which is an interpretation of its long-term effects.

## Stop: eric-bloodaxe-expelled-954
Before: In the fifteen years after Æthelstan's death York changed hands several times between Norse kings from Dublin and the English kings Edmund and Eadred. The Northumbrians accepted Eric as king around 947–8, gave him up when Eadred harried their country, and took him back about 952. York's own leaders, among them its archbishop, Wulfstan, seem to have chosen between the rival kings as it suited them.

What happened: In 954, the Chronicle says in a single sentence, the Northumbrians drove Eric out and Eadred succeeded to the kingdom of the Northumbrians. It gives no place, so the map draws a halo over the kingdom of York. Roger of Wendover, writing in the 13th century and probably drawing on lost northern annals, adds that Eric was betrayed by Oswulf of Bamburgh and killed at Stainmore, the high pass over the Pennines; because he is late, that killing is told but not mapped. Eric is usually identified with Eric Bloodaxe of the sagas, son of Harald Finehair of Norway, but the identification is debated.

After: York was never again ruled by an independent king, and Northumbria was governed for the West Saxon king by earls, Oswulf first among them; from now on one king ruled the whole of England. Historians therefore often treat 954 rather than 927 as the end of the process by which the kings of Wessex became kings of the English. The north kept its own laws, customs and Scandinavian character for long afterwards, and later kings had to accept that.

## Stop: edgar-coronation-bath-973
Before: After Eadred's death in 955 the kingdom passed to his nephew Eadwig, and in 957 the Mercians and Northumbrians chose Eadwig's younger brother Edgar as their king; on Eadwig's death in 959 Edgar ruled the whole. His reign is remembered for peace at home and for the Benedictine reform led by Dunstan, Archbishop of Canterbury, Æthelwold, Bishop of Winchester, and Oswald, Bishop of Worcester and later Archbishop of York. With the king's backing they replaced communities of clergy with monks, refounded monasteries and drew up a common rule for English monks and nuns, the Regularis Concordia.

What happened: At Pentecost, 11 May 973, fourteen years into his reign, Edgar was consecrated king at Bath by Archbishops Dunstan and Oswald, and his queen Ælfthryth was anointed too. The Chronicle marks it with a short poem, and Byrhtferth of Ramsey, writing within about thirty years, describes the ceremony at length; the order of service is the ancestor of the rite used at later English coronations. The Anglo-Saxon minster has not been found but probably lay partly beneath the present abbey, where the map puts its pin. Why Edgar waited so long is debated: suggestions include a link with his age or a wish to proclaim a new, imperial style of kingship.

After: Edgar then took his fleet to Chester, where, the Chronicle says, six kings pledged to be his allies; the 12th-century story that eight kings rowed him on the Dee is a later embellishment, and the pack excludes it. At about this time, or a little later, the coinage was reformed: a single design was struck at every mint in the kingdom, each coin naming its moneyer and mint, and the design was changed at intervals with the old coin called in. Together the ceremony, the pledges and the coinage show a kingdom governed from the centre and claiming supremacy over Britain. Some historians see Edgar's reign as the time when the kingdom later inherited by William the Conqueror took shape, which is an interpretation of the wider evidence.

## Closing
The stops showed a kingdom made in stages, and unmade more than once along the way: Wessex saved at Edington, defended and extended by burhs, the northern kings' submission at Eamont, a coalition beaten at Brunanburh, York lost and won until Eric was driven out, and Edgar consecrated at Bath as king of the English with a single coinage across the land. The honest answer to when England became one kingdom is that there is no single date: 927 gave it a king of all the English, 954 ended the last rival kingdom at York, and Edgar's reign gave it the government that made the unity last, though the north kept its own customs long after.
