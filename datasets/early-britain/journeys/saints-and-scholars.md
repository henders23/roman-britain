# Saints and scholars of the north
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Order: chronological

In the seventh century the north of Britain became one of the great centres of learning in Europe. This journey follows the monks who made it so, from Iona to Lindisfarne, Wearmouth and Jarrow, and ends with the raid that shook their world. Much of the story is told by Bede, who belonged to it, so look out at each stop for what rests on his word and what on stones, books and excavation.

## Stop: columba-iona-563
Before: Columba (Colum Cille) was an Irish abbot of royal descent, already the founder of churches in Ireland. Across the sea, the west of what is now Scotland belonged to the Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata, and beyond it lay the lands of the Picts. Adomnán, a later abbot of Iona, places Columba's voyage two years after the battle of Cúl Drebene in Ireland.

What happened: Columba sailed to Britain with a small group of companions and founded a monastery on Iona, off Mull. The traditional date of 563 comes from the Irish annals; Bede gives 565 and says the Pictish king Bridei granted the island, while Irish tradition credits the king of Dál Riata. The early monastery lay inside a great earthwork enclosure around the later abbey, and charcoal from a hut on the knoll of Tòrr an Aba has been radiocarbon dated to about AD 540–650. That this hut was Columba's own writing cell is an attractive interpretation, not a proven fact.

After: Iona became the head of a family of monasteries in Ireland and northern Britain, and Columba died there in 597. Adomnán's Life of Columba, written about a century after the founding, is a book of miracles and visions rather than a chronicle, but it shows a working monastery with its books, boats and visitors. Iona's monks carried Christianity among the Picts, and in the next generation, through Aidan, to the Northumbrians.

## Stop: aidan-lindisfarne-635
Before: Northumbria's first conversion, under King Edwin and the Roman missionary Paulinus, collapsed when Edwin was killed in 633 and Paulinus fled south. Oswald, son of Edwin's rival Æthelfrith, had spent his exile among the Irish and had been baptised in Iona's tradition. When he won the kingdom at Heavenfield in 634, he looked to Iona, not to Rome or Canterbury, for a bishop.

What happened: Bede tells how a first, harsh missionary returned to Iona having failed, and how Aidan was sent in his place. Oswald gave him Lindisfarne, a tidal island within sight of the royal fortress at Bamburgh, for his see and monastery. Bede describes Aidan walking from village to village and the king himself acting as his interpreter. He gives no year, so 635 is worked out from Oswald's accession and from Aidan's death in 651, and 634 is also possible.

After: Lindisfarne became the chief church of Northumbria and a school for English monks and bishops, and Aidan's mission, more than Rome's, converted the northern English. Its monks kept Iona's way of dating Easter, which brought them into conflict with followers of Rome within a generation. Bede, writing nearly a century later, admired Aidan's life while disapproving of his Easter reckoning, so his portrait is warm but shaped by that later argument. No building of Aidan's time has been securely identified beneath the later priory.

## Stop: synod-of-whitby-664
At Whitby the Northumbrian church chose Roman over Irish ways of dating Easter. King Oswiu, Oswald's brother, heard the case at Abbess Hild's double monastery, with Bishop Colman of Lindisfarne speaking for Iona's practice and Wilfrid for Rome's. Bede says Oswiu chose Rome because its tradition claimed the authority of St Peter, and that Colman then withdrew to Iona and Ireland. Bede's account is vivid, but it was written two generations later, and the speeches are reconstructions by him and by Wilfrid's biographer Stephen of Ripon; historians still debate how far the meeting was about church custom and how far about royal politics.

## Stop: wearmouth-foundation-674
Benedict Biscop, a Northumbrian nobleman who had travelled to Rome many times, founded the monastery at Wearmouth on land given by King Ecgfrith, and filled it with books, glass and paintings from the continent. Bede, who joined the monastery as a boy, says Biscop brought masons from Gaul to build the church of St Peter in stone in the Roman manner, and glaziers to make its windows. The west wall and porch of that church still stand at Monkwearmouth. Excavations in the 1960s and 1970s uncovered parts of the monastic buildings, with fragments of coloured window glass.

## Stop: jarrow-dedication-685
Its twin house at Jarrow was dedicated on a day recorded on a stone that still survives in the church. The Latin inscription, now above the chancel arch of St Paul's, dates the dedication by the fifteenth year of King Ecgfrith and the fourth of Abbot Ceolfrith, which works out as 23 April 685. Wearmouth and Jarrow, some 10 km apart, were run as one monastery on two sites, sharing the library Biscop had gathered. Bede entered the community as a child and spent almost his whole life there.

## Stop: lindisfarne-gospels
Back on Lindisfarne, the Lindisfarne Gospels were made in the early eighth century, combining Irish, Anglo-Saxon and Mediterranean styles. The book, now in the British Library, has portraits of the evangelists, full-page cross designs and great decorated initials. It belongs to the community's devotion to St Cuthbert, the Lindisfarne bishop who died in 687. A note added in the tenth century names Eadfrith, bishop of Lindisfarne, as its maker; most scholars accept it and date the work to about 710–725, but the note was written some two and a half centuries after the book.

## Stop: bede-historia-731
Before: Bede had entered Wearmouth–Jarrow at the age of seven and spent his life inside it, writing on the Bible, on time and on the calendar. The library that Biscop and Ceolfrith had built up gave him the books he needed. For his history of the English church he also gathered material by letter from Canterbury and other churches, and he usually says where it came from.

What happened: In 731 Bede finished his Ecclesiastical History of the English People, in Latin, closing it with a year-by-year summary, a short note on his own life and a list of his works. He says when he finished, but not in which of the twin houses he wrote; he lived at Jarrow, so the pin is placed there. Two copies made within a few years of his death in 735 still survive, which is why the text is so securely known.

After: The History was copied widely in England and on the continent, and in the late ninth century it was translated into Old English. Most of what we know of the seventh-century kings, bishops and conversions, including Columba, Aidan and Whitby on this journey, comes from it, and it helped spread the habit of counting years from the birth of Christ. Bede wrote as a monk with firm views on what mattered, such as the correct Easter, so historians read him as an interpreter as well as a witness. It is the main reason this journey can be told at all.

## Stop: lindisfarne-raid-793
In Bede's time Lindisfarne had been at the centre of this world of learning; in 793 raiders from the sea struck it, killing and carrying off members of the community and plundering the church. The northern versions of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle record the attack, and Alcuin of York, then at Charlemagne's court, wrote soon afterwards to the king of Northumbria and to the monks. To him and to the scholars of the north it seemed the end of an age, a judgement on Northumbrian sins, which is his reading rather than a report. The Chronicle's day reads as 8 January, which historians usually correct to 8 June, the raiding season; the community survived and stayed on the island for another eighty years.
