# Christianity in Roman Britain
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Order: thematic

No written account tells us how Christianity spread in Roman Britain. We know that three bishops from Britain attended a church council at Arles in 314, soon after Constantine ended the persecution, but beyond that what survives are objects and buildings, most of them from the fourth century, and each has to be interpreted. This journey visits four of them and asks, at each stop, how we know the object is Christian at all. The first three are dated only to the fourth century, so they are ordered by argument rather than by date. The last stop leaves the province: the faith of late Roman Britain carried to Ireland by one of its own people, in the only words a Christian Briton of the time has left us.

## Stop: water-newton-treasure
The clearest case. The hoard was ploughed up in 1975 inside the walled Roman town of Durobrivae, beside the modern village of Water Newton. It comprises nine silver vessels, among them a jug, bowls and a two-handled cup, and a set of thin triangular silver plaques shaped like leaves. Several of the vessels and plaques carry the chi-rho monogram, and some have Latin inscriptions that are plainly Christian: on one bowl a man called Publianus says that he honours the Lord's holy altar, and a plaque records that a woman named Anicilla fulfilled her vow. That makes the hoard some of the earliest Christian church plate known from anywhere in the Roman empire. The leaf-shaped plaques, though, look like the votive offerings left at pagan shrines, a sign that older habits of worship carried over into the new religion. When and why the silver was buried is not known.

## Stop: hinton-st-mary-mosaic
Here the argument is harder. The floor was found in 1963 in a building at Hinton St Mary in Dorset and lifted to the British Museum in 1965; its style links it with a workshop at or near Dorchester. It covered two connected rooms: the smaller shows the hero Bellerophon on Pegasus killing the Chimaera, and the larger has at its centre the head and shoulders of a clean-shaven man before a chi-rho, flanked by pomegranates. The bust is usually read as Christ, which would make it one of the earliest images of him in Britain or anywhere. Some scholars argue it could be an emperor such as Constantine, since emperors used the chi-rho on their coins too, and how far the owners meant the pagan hero and the Christian symbol to be read together is also debated. The pack keeps that as interpretation, not fact.

## Stop: mildenhall-treasure
A great silver treasure with mainly pagan imagery, but a few spoons carry Christian symbols. Its centrepiece, the Great Dish, weighs over 8 kg and shows the sea-god Oceanus at its centre surrounded by a riotous procession of Bacchus and his followers; the 34 or so pieces include platters, bowls, goblets and spoons, a few marked with the chi-rho. Its owners' beliefs are not straightforward: a household could own Christian spoons and pagan display silver, perhaps because the classical scenes were valued as culture rather than worship. The circumstances of its discovery have themselves been questioned. A ploughman found the silver in 1942, but his employer kept it at home until a visitor recognised it in 1946, so there was no record of the findspot, and the treasure was fully published only in 2016.

## Stop: lullingstone-house-church
At Lullingstone, a room in a villa was painted in the later fourth century with chi-rho monograms and figures at prayer: a Christian space inside a private house, found by excavation and pieced back together from fallen plaster. The villa in the Darent valley in Kent was excavated from 1949, and the painted fragments had fallen from an upper room into the cellar beneath. Once reassembled, they show a row of figures standing between columns with their arms raised in the early Christian posture of prayer, and on other walls large chi-rho monograms within wreaths. The rooms, with what seems to be an ante-room, are read as a house church for the household and perhaps its neighbours, a widely accepted interpretation. Earlier in the villa's life the room below had been used for pagan rites, so here, as at Mildenhall, the old and new religions sat close together. The villa was abandoned after a fire in the 5th century.

## Stop: patrick-mission-ireland
The Christianity of these villas and towns did not end with Roman rule, and its clearest witness is a Briton who took it beyond the old frontier. In his Confessio Patrick says that his father Calpurnius was a deacon and a town councillor with a small estate, and his grandfather a priest: a Christian family of the kind that might have owned the Hinton St Mary floor or worshipped at Lullingstone. He was taken captive by Irish raiders at about sixteen, escaped after six years, and later returned to Ireland as a bishop, baptising thousands and ordaining clergy. His two writings, the Confessio and a letter rebuking the British ruler Coroticus, whose soldiers had killed and enslaved new converts, are the only texts written in Ireland in the fifth century. They give no dates, and the traditional ones (arrival in 432) come from much later annals that seem to merge him with Palladius, sent to the Irish Christians in 431; scholars place him somewhere in the fifth century. The later belief that he converted the whole island alone belongs to the 7th-century church of Armagh.
