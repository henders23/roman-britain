# How do we know when?
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: how-do-we-know

Every date on this map rests on some kind of evidence, and the kind of evidence decides how precise the date can be. Each stop on this journey is dated in a different way. Watch the timeline: most of these events are drawn as windows of years, not single dates, and the width of each window is the evidence speaking.

## Stop: vindolanda-tablets
Dating by layers. The tablets survived in waterlogged deposits laid down in successive rebuildings of the fort. The order of the layers, tied to coins, pottery and a few datable references, gives each group of letters a window of years rather than a single date. The texts help date themselves too: they name commanders and units, such as the prefect Flavius Cerialis, whose time at the fort can be fixed within a few years. Together these place the tablets between about the 80s and the 120s, with most from the years around 100. The method gives a secure order and a span of decades, but almost never a day or a year for a single letter.

## Stop: hoxne-hoard
Dating by the latest coin. A hoard cannot have been buried before its newest coin was struck, so the Hoxne coins of Constantine III give a date after which it went into the ground. How long after is a judgement, here based on how worn and clipped the coins are. Archaeologists call such a date a terminus post quem, a limit after which, and it fixes only one end of the window. The hoard was excavated by archaeologists the day after it was found in 1992, so the way it was packed in its chest was recorded too. Even so, the map's window runs from 408 to the middle of the fifth century, because nothing in the ground says how long the coins had circulated before they were buried.

## Stop: sutton-hoo-mound1
The same method, a different argument. The gold coins in the Mound 1 purse give a date after which the burial happened, but scholars disagree about how quickly such a collection was assembled. That disagreement is why the window runs across three decades. There are 37 Frankish gold coins, and none bears a date; they are dated by comparing their designs and the purity of their gold, which fell over time, with other coins of the period. Studies of their gold content suggest the collection had been gathered by around 613, while earlier work put them in the 620s. Linking the burial to King Rædwald, who died about 624, would narrow the date, but that link is itself an interpretation, so the window stays wide.

## Stop: lindisfarne-gospels
Dating by style and by a note added later. The book's decoration and script place it early in the eighth century. Its maker is named only in a colophon written about 250 years later, which is evidence of a different kind: what the community believed about its own treasure. The note names Eadfrith, bishop of Lindisfarne, as the scribe, and his career, together with the style of the book, lies behind the window of about 710–725. Most scholars accept the note, but because it is not contemporary some have questioned parts of it. Here the date is an argument built from several kinds of evidence, each of which supports the others.

## Stop: battle-of-hastings-1066
Dating by a contemporary chronicle. By 1066 the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle records events year by year, and several sources agree on the day. Here, for once, the map can show a single date with confidence. One version of the Chronicle gives the day as the feast of Pope Calixtus, 14 October, and the Norman account by William of Poitiers and the Bayeux Tapestry describe the same battle from the winners' side. Precision in date is not precision in place, though: the site rests on the tradition that Battle Abbey's altar marks where Harold fell, and no battle archaeology has yet been found there.

## Closing
Each kind of evidence gives its own precision: layers and coins give windows of years, style gives decades or centuries, and written sources can give a day. A date on the map is only as good as the evidence behind it, which is why so many events are drawn as windows.
