# St Cuthbert's community on the move
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: gods-saints-and-the-church

For more than three centuries one community kept its identity: the monks of Lindisfarne, who later guarded St Cuthbert's body, carried it from the island and settled at last at Durham. This journey begins with the founding of their monastery and the great book made there, then follows the community through the raid of 793 and more than a century on the move. The later story is told by the community's own histories, which are sometimes more concerned with the saint's authority than with exact dates. Only the stopping places are shown; nothing is drawn between them.

## Stop: aidan-lindisfarne-635
King Oswald of Northumbria invited Aidan, a monk of Iona, to found a monastery on Lindisfarne. Cuthbert would later be its bishop, and after his death in 687 his tomb made the island a place of pilgrimage. Aidan was abbot and bishop together, and his island, cut off by the tide twice a day and within sight of the royal fortress at Bamburgh, became the chief church of Northumbria. Cuthbert, trained at Melrose in Aidan's tradition, came to Lindisfarne as prior after the Synod of Whitby, was made bishop in 685 and died two years later on the island of Inner Farne. When his tomb was opened in 698 his body was said to be undecayed, and the cult that grew from that report gave the community the identity it carried through the rest of this journey.

## Stop: lindisfarne-gospels
The community's great book was made in the early eighth century. A note added in the tenth century says it was written by Eadfrith, bishop of Lindisfarne. By tradition the book went with the community on its travels. The same note says the book was made for God and St Cuthbert; most scholars accept it and date the work to about 710–725, a generation after Cuthbert's death. It adds that Æthelwald, a later bishop, bound the book and the hermit Billfrith adorned its cover with gold and gems, though that binding is long lost. Symeon of Durham, writing in the early twelfth century, tells of a gospel book, usually taken to be this one, washed overboard on the community's failed voyage to Ireland and found unharmed on the shore; it is a story about the saint's protection rather than a record.

## Stop: lindisfarne-raid-793
Lindisfarne, Cuthbert's monastery, was the first major English church that Viking raiders are recorded to have attacked. News of it shocked scholars as far away as Charlemagne's court. The northern versions of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle enter it under 793, and Alcuin of York wrote soon afterwards to the king of Northumbria and to Bishop Higbald and his monks, treating the attack as a judgement on Northumbrian sins. The Chronicle gives the day as 8 January, an unlikely season for sea raids, and the often-quoted 8 June is a modern emendation, so the atlas dates the raid to the year only. The community was not destroyed: it stayed on the island with Cuthbert's shrine for another eighty years.

## Stop: cuthbert-community-leaves-lindisfarne-875
With Viking armies in the north, the community left the island in 875, carrying Cuthbert's body. The histories name Bishop Eardwulf and Abbot Eadred as its leaders and say they left as Halfdan's army moved into Northumbria. They tell of years of wandering, including a failed attempt to sail to Ireland, before the community found a new home. The date and the story come from the community's own later histories, written at Chester-le-Street and Durham between the tenth and twelfth centuries. Some historians point out that the community already had an important church at Norham on the Tweed and may have shifted its centre there earlier, so a single dramatic departure may be a later simplification.

## Stop: cuthbert-community-chester-le-street-883
After some years of wandering, the community settled at Chester-le-Street, where it stayed for more than a century. Its histories say that Abbot Eadred, guided by the saint in a vision, had a young man called Guthred made king, and that this Viking king gave the community land. The church was built inside the walls of the old Roman fort, and the present church of St Mary and St Cuthbert is taken to stand on its site. Here, in the tenth century, the priest Aldred added an Old English translation between the lines of the Lindisfarne Gospels, which the community had carried with it.

## Stop: cuthbert-community-durham-995
In 995 the community moved to Durham, where Cuthbert's shrine still lies. Symeon of Durham, writing in the early twelfth century, says that Bishop Aldhun, fearing another Viking attack, first took the body to Ripon, and on the way back the community settled on the rocky peninsula in a loop of the River Wear. A stone church, often called the White Church, was dedicated in 998, and the present cathedral replaced it from 1093; where exactly the earlier church stood is not known. A later story says they found the place by following a woman looking for her cow; the pack keeps that tale off the map.

## Closing
The community survived raids, years of wandering and two moves by keeping two things: Cuthbert's body and its own story about him. That story is also the main source for its journey, so the dates are the community's own. Durham Cathedral, built over the shrine, is the end of the road.
