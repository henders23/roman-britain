# Bede's world
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: gods-saints-and-the-church

Bede entered the monastery at Wearmouth as a child and spent his life at its twin house at Jarrow. This journey follows the half-century he lived through: the founding of his monasteries, a battle that shook Northumbria, a great gospel book, and his own history. Much of what we know of these years, we know because he wrote it down.

## Stop: wearmouth-foundation-674
In 674 Benedict Biscop founded a monastery at the mouth of the Wear, on land given by King Ecgfrith, and built its church in stone with craftsmen from Gaul. Biscop was a Northumbrian nobleman turned monk who had been to Rome several times, and he filled his new house with books, relics and pictures brought back from his journeys. Bede tells the story in his Lives of the Abbots, and the date rests on his account. The west wall and porch of that first church still stand in St Peter's, Monkwearmouth, and excavations by Rosemary Cramp in the 1960s and 1970s found parts of the monastic buildings and fragments of coloured window glass. Bede was given to this monastery as a boy, at the age of seven.

## Stop: jarrow-dedication-685
A stone inscription in St Paul's church at Jarrow records that it was dedicated on 23 April 685. Bede says that Ecgfrith, pleased with Wearmouth, gave more land on the Tyne, and Biscop sent Ceolfrith there with a group of monks in 681 or 682. The Latin, cut in Roman capitals and now set above the chancel arch, dates the dedication by the fifteenth year of Ecgfrith and the fourth of Abbot Ceolfrith. The chancel of the present church is the smaller of the two original churches on the site, and Cramp's excavations found stone buildings and window glass to its south. The two houses, some 10 km apart, were run as one monastery and shared Biscop's library, and Bede spent most of his life at Jarrow. It is one of the few dates of this age written at the time rather than by a later historian.

## Stop: dun-nechtain-685
A month later, on 20 May 685, a Pictish army destroyed the army of the same King Ecgfrith at Dún Nechtain, and the king was killed. Bede says that Ecgfrith led his army into Pictland against the advice of his friends, and that the Picts under their king Bridei son of Beli feigned flight and drew him into narrow places among inaccessible mountains. The Annals of Ulster give the battle's name and date independently of Bede. Northumbria's power over the Picts collapsed: Bede tells how Trumwine, the bishop Ecgfrith had set over them at Abercorn, withdrew south with his monks, and he saw the battle as the start of Northumbria's decline. Where the battle was fought is disputed: it has long been placed at Dunnichen in Angus, but Dunachton in Badenoch, a place with the same name among real mountain passes, has also been argued for, so the map draws a region over both.

## Stop: lindisfarne-gospels
In the early eighth century the Lindisfarne Gospels were made in Northumbria, combining Irish, Anglo-Saxon and Mediterranean styles. The book, now in the British Library, holds the four gospels with portraits of the evangelists, full-page cross designs known as carpet pages and great decorated initials. In the tenth century a priest named Aldred added an Old English translation between the lines, the earliest surviving English version of the gospels, and a note naming Eadfrith, bishop of Lindisfarne, as its maker. Most scholars accept the note and date the book to about 710–725, but it was written some two and a half centuries later. The book shows the same world of learning and craft that produced Bede, who wrote a Life of St Cuthbert, Lindisfarne's own saint, for the monks of the island.

## Stop: bede-historia-731
In 731 Bede finished his Ecclesiastical History of the English People. He lived at Jarrow, so the pin is placed there, though he does not say in which of the twin houses he wrote. He closed it with a year-by-year summary, a note on his own life and a list of his works, and he usually names the people and churches, Canterbury above all, that sent him material by letter. Two copies made within a few years of his death in 735 still survive, which is why the text is so securely known. It is the main written source for the conversion of the English, and for the battle at Dún Nechtain. It also helped spread the habit of counting years from the birth of Christ.

## Closing
Bede's world was small in space, two monasteries a few miles apart, but it reached Rome, Gaul and Ireland through books, builders and teachers. The battle at Dún Nechtain is known mainly through Bede's account and the Irish annals. When we read this period, we often read it through his eyes.
