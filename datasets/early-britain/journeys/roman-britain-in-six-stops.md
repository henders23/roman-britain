# Roman Britain in six stops
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: start-here

In AD 43 British kings submitted to a Roman emperor who had come to see Colchester taken. Less than four centuries later, Britain was no longer governed from Rome. This short journey is a first look at the whole Roman period: how it began, how it was held, and how it ended. Ask at each stop how we know.

## Stop: camulodunum-surrender-43
The emperor Claudius came to Britain for only a few days to see Camulodunum taken and to receive the submission of British rulers. Dio says the invasion army halted at the Thames and waited for him; he arrived with reinforcements, crossed the river, defeated the Britons and took Camulodunum, the stronghold of the successors of Cunobelinus. That stronghold was a spread-out complex of dykes and settlements west of the later town, so the pin marks Colchester in general. His arch in Rome records kings who surrendered to him; the number, probably eleven, is partly restored, and neither the arch nor Dio says where they submitted. Camulodunum then became the first Roman colony in Britain, which made it the rebels' first target when Boudica rose seventeen years later. Rome's rule in Britain begins with a ceremony as much as a battle.

## Stop: hadrian-visit-britain-122
Eighty years on, the emperor Hadrian visited the province. By then Rome had already advanced into Scotland and pulled back, and the army held a line across the north of England. His biography, written in the late 4th century, links the visit to the building of a wall, eighty miles long, to separate the Romans from the barbarians. Where he went in Britain is not recorded, so the map draws a region rather than a pin. The year comes from a military diploma of 17 July 122, which shows that his friend Platorius Nepos had just become governor; Nepos built much of the Wall, which ran from the Tyne to the Solway. The Wall itself is a line the atlas cannot draw, so it is listed in the pack but not on the map, and why Hadrian built it is still argued over.

## Stop: barbarian-conspiracy-367
In 367 Picts, Scots and Attacotti attacked Britain while Franks and Saxons raided Gaul. The historian Ammianus calls it a conspiracy of barbarians. He says the commander of the coast was killed, a general was ambushed, and the scouts meant to watch the peoples beyond the frontier had joined the enemy. Count Theodosius crossed from Boulogne, cleared raiding bands near London and restored cities and forts, probably by the end of 368. Ammianus wrote some twenty years later, under the count's son, the emperor Theodosius I, so he had reason to make both the disaster and the rescue look large. Whether the attacks were really planned together is debated, but the province was badly shaken. Burnt layers once dated to 367 at sites across Britain are now mostly not accepted as evidence.

## Stop: constantine-iii-proclaimed-407
In 407 the army in Britain made a soldier named Constantine emperor. Zosimus says the troops first raised a man called Marcus and killed him, then a local man, Gratian, and killed him too, before turning to Constantine; Orosius adds that he was chosen for the hope his name inspired. No source says where in Britain any of them was proclaimed, so the map draws a halo over the province. He took the province's mobile troops to Gaul to fight for the western empire, and for a time the emperor Honorius recognised him, until he was captured and executed in 411. They did not come back. Zosimus later says the Britons threw out Constantine's officials, which belongs to the end of Roman rule at the last stop.

## Stop: hoxne-hoard
Some time after 408 someone in Suffolk buried a chest of gold and silver with almost fifteen thousand coins. Eric Lawes found it in 1992 while searching with a metal detector for a farmer's lost hammer, and because he reported it at once, archaeologists could record how the coins, spoons, pepper-pots and jewellery had been packed in a wooden chest. The latest coins are two silver pieces of Constantine III, the usurper of the last stop, so the hoard cannot have been buried before 407–408. Many of the silver coins are heavily clipped, which some take as a sign that the burial came some years later. Its latest coins, with finds from many other sites, show that new money had almost stopped reaching Britain. The owners never came back for it, and who they were and why they buried it are unknown.

## Stop: rescript-of-honorius-410
A Greek historian writing a century later says that in 410 the emperor Honorius told the cities of Britain to look to their own defence. The report is one clause in the history of Zosimus, set among Honorius's troubles in Italy in the year Alaric's Goths sacked Rome, and no text of any letter survives. It is the famous end date, but it rests on one sentence that may not even refer to Britain. The Greek names for Britain and for Bruttium, the toe of Italy, differ by only a few letters, so some scholars think the letter went to Bruttium, while others defend the reading 'Britain'. Either way, by about this time Roman government in Britain had ended, and the atlas draws the event as a halo over the province with low certainty.

## Closing
Roman Britain began with an emperor's few days at Colchester and ended without any single moment the evidence can fix. Emperors came and went, the frontier was built and defended, and in the end the army left. Each of the other journeys looks more closely at part of this story.
