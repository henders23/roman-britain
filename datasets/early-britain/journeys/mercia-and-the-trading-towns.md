# Mercia and the trading towns
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Order: chronological

In the seventh and eighth centuries new trading towns grew up on rivers and coasts, and the kingdom of Mercia became the strongest power in southern Britain. This journey moves between the towns, where archaeology has found the evidence of everyday trade, and the treasures, coins, churches and councils of Mercian kings, with Offa's council at Chelsea at its centre. Look for how little is written down: most of the towns are known almost entirely from excavation.

Offa's Dyke, the great earthwork along the Welsh border that Asser says Offa had built from sea to sea, is a line the atlas cannot draw, so it appears in the pack as an excluded row and is not on the map.

## Stop: lundenwic
Not inside the old Roman walls but along the Strand, Saxon London grew into a busy trading town. Bede, writing about the mission of 604, called London a market for many peoples coming by land and sea, but for a long time no such town could be found inside the walls. Excavations since the 1980s, above all at the Royal Opera House between 1989 and 1999, found it to the west, stretching from about Trafalgar Square to Aldwych: gravelled streets, timber houses, workshops for metal and jewellery, and pottery from across the Channel. London passed between the kings of Kent, Essex and Mercia, and from the 730s Mercian kings were granting freedom from tolls on ships in its port, a sign of who profited from the trade. A ditch dug in the 9th century is usually read as a defence against Vikings; after Alfred took control of London in 886 settlement moved back inside the walls, and the old site became Ealdwic, the 'old settlement' that gives Aldwych its name.

## Stop: gipeswic-ipswich
Ipswich, in the kingdom of the East Angles, was another early trading town, and its potters made Ipswich ware, the first pottery turned on a wheel and fired in kilns in England since Roman times. The town grew from a small 7th-century landing place on the Orwell into a settlement of some 50 hectares in the 8th century, with streets, buildings and a potters' quarter around Carr Street and Cox Lane. Recent study dates the pottery from about 720 to about 850, later than was once thought. Finds of it across East Anglia and as far as Kent, London and Yorkshire map where Ipswich traded. Its shapes and kilns resemble Frisian wares, and whether migrant potters brought the craft is debated. Unlike London and Southampton, Ipswich never moved, and has been a town ever since.

## Stop: staffordshire-hoard
In the heart of Mercia, near Hammerwich and a few miles from Lichfield, someone buried thousands of gold and garnet fittings stripped from weapons. A metal detectorist found the first pieces in 2009, and the hoard now numbers nearly 4,600 fragments, mostly from sword hilts and a helmet, together with a few Christian objects, among them crosses and a gold strip inscribed with a biblical verse calling on God to scatter his enemies. The objects are dated by their style and manufacture, not by coins, to between the 6th century and about 660, and the hoard was probably buried about 650–675, in the years of Penda and his sons. Why the pieces were stripped, bent and buried is debated: spoils of battle, tribute and an offering have all been proposed, and no link to any known battle is proved. It shows the wealth of war that underpinned Mercian power.

## Stop: brixworth-all-saints
Brixworth is one of the largest buildings to survive from Anglo-Saxon England, an aisled church whose arches are turned in brick and tile taken from Roman buildings nearby. A 12th-century Peterborough writer says that Cuthbald, abbot of Medeshamstede (Peterborough) from about 675, founded a monastery here, and the church was long dated to the 7th century on his word. A research programme begun in 1972 changed the picture: radiocarbon dates from charcoal in the mortar point to the later 8th century for the main body of the church, with the tower and apse added before about 900. That would place it around Offa's time, but whether an earlier church stood here and who paid for this one are still open questions. Either way it shows the ambition of the Mercian church, on a scale close to churches on the continent.

## Stop: hamwic
The West Saxons had their own trading town at Hamwic, on the Itchen in the St Mary's district of Southampton, laid out with gravelled streets on a regular grid and crowded with craftworkers. Excavations since 1946 have mapped at least 42 hectares, with timber buildings, wells and rubbish pits, and evidence of iron, bone, textile and glass working. Imported pottery and glass point to trade with northern France and the Low Countries, and many silver sceattas were found, some perhaps struck on the spot. The finds suggest a foundation about 700, and the planned layout is often linked to a West Saxon king such as Ine, though that is interpretation. Written sources are few: the missionary Willibald sailed from a market called Hamwih in 720, and by the later 9th century the town was in decline.

## Stop: offa-gold-dinar
Offa of Mercia had a gold coin made that copies an Islamic dinar, Arabic writing and all, with his own name added. Its die-cutter copied a dinar of the caliph al-Mansur struck in 773–774 so closely that the model can be identified, but set OFFA REX upside down to the Arabic, which suggests he could not read it. Only one example is known; it was recorded in Rome and published in 1841, and it has no mint name. Gold dinars were the leading coin of the Mediterranean, so it may have been meant for trade, or for the 365 mancuses of gold a year that Offa promised to the papacy; why he had it made is unknown. The modern claim that it shows Offa had become a Muslim has no support and is excluded in the pack.

## Stop: council-of-chelsea-787
Before: Mercia's supremacy in the south was built by two long-lived kings. Æthelbald, king from 716, was by Bede's account overlord of all the southern English by 731, and a charter of 736 calls him king of Britain; he was murdered by his own bodyguard in 757. Offa won the kingdom after a short civil war and, over the next thirty years, made himself overlord of most of southern England, by the mid 780s ruling Kent and Sussex directly. He issued a new broad silver penny in his own name, which became the basic English coin, and later exchanged letters and gifts with Charlemagne. But the archbishop of the southern church sat at Canterbury in Kent, and Archbishop Jænberht was no friend of Mercia.

What happened: In 787 a 'contentious synod' met at Cealchyth, usually identified with Chelsea on the Thames. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle reports it in one sentence, under 785 because its dates for these decades run two years early: Jænberht gave up part of his province, Hygeberht was chosen by King Offa, and Offa's son Ecgfrith was consecrated king. Papal letters show that Pope Hadrian I had agreed to make Lichfield an archbishopric for the Mercian dioceses under Hygeberht, already its bishop. Ecgfrith's is the first recorded consecration of an English king, probably copying the pope's anointing of Charlemagne's sons in 781, and Nicholas Brooks argued that the two decisions were linked: Jænberht would not consecrate Ecgfrith, so Offa had an archbishop made who would.

After: Offa died in 796 and Ecgfrith only a few months later; Alcuin wrote that the son had paid for the blood his father shed to secure the succession. Coenwulf, a distant kinsman, took the throne, and in 803 a council abolished Lichfield's archbishopric with the pope's consent. Mercia stayed strong under Coenwulf, but after his death in 821 it was weakened by rival claimants, and in 825 Ecgberht of Wessex defeated the Mercians at Ellendun, in Wiltshire, after which Kent, Surrey, Sussex and Essex submitted to Wessex. Calling Offa overlord of the English south, and the consecration the start of English royal anointing, are historians' readings of thin evidence, but the balance of power in the south had passed to Wessex.

## Stop: lichfield-angel
Under the floor of Lichfield Cathedral, excavators found a carved and painted angel of about 800, carved within a few years of the time Lichfield was briefly an archbishopric. Three fragments of the limestone panel were found in 2003, during excavation in the nave that also uncovered remains of earlier churches on the site. The angel raises his right hand in blessing and holds a leafy sceptre, and unusually traces of the original paint survive, showing how brightly such carving was once coloured. The panel formed one end of a box with a sloping lid, usually taken to be a shrine for St Chad, Lichfield's first bishop, who died in 672, and the angel is usually identified as Gabriel at the Annunciation. Both identifications are widely accepted, and the date of about 800 comes from comparison with other Mercian sculpture and manuscripts, not from scientific dating.

## Stop: repton-winter-camp-873
Mercian power ended with the Viking great army, which wintered at Repton, a royal and religious centre of Mercia, and drove out its king. Repton was where Æthelbald was buried in 757 and the murdered Mercian prince Wigstan in the 9th century, and the crypt beneath St Wystan's church survives. The Chronicle records that the army came from Lindsey in 873, drove King Burgred overseas, where he went to Rome and died, and set up Ceolwulf in his place. Excavations in the 1970s and 1980s found a ditch that used the church as part of a D-shaped enclosure open to the Trent, graves with Scandinavian weapons, and a charnel deposit of at least 264 people whose radiocarbon dates, once corrected for a fish-rich diet, fit the 870s. That they all belong to the army is interpretation, strengthened but not proved by the dating. Within a few years the army divided Mercia, taking the east for itself and leaving the west to Ceolwulf.
