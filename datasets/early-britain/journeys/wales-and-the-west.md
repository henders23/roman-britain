# Wales and the west after Rome
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Order: chronological

The west of Britain kept Roman connections and British kingship long after Rome left. This journey follows that world for nearly seven centuries: from a Roman usurper whom Welsh kings later claimed as an ancestor, through hilltop courts trading with the Mediterranean and kings who wrote their claims on stone and in law, to Gruffudd ap Llywelyn, the one king who ruled all of Wales, and his fall on the eve of the Norman Conquest. Much of the written evidence survives only in much later copies, so look at each stop for the gap between the event and the record of it.

## Stop: magnus-maximus-proclaimed-383
The journey begins with a Roman story that Wales later made its own. In 383 the army in Britain proclaimed its commander, Magnus Maximus, emperor; he crossed to Gaul, where the emperor Gratian was killed, ruled the western provinces from Trier, and was defeated and executed by Theodosius in 388. Gildas, writing in the 6th century, blamed him for taking Britain's soldiers away for good, which historians think an exaggeration. In Welsh tradition he became Macsen Wledig: the medieval tale of the Dream of Macsen Wledig makes him a hero who dreams of a British princess, Elen, and finds her at Segontium, the Roman fort at Caernarfon. Several Welsh royal lines claimed descent from him, and the Pillar of Eliseg, later on this journey, carves that claim in stone. None of this is evidence for the events of 383, but it shows how the Roman past became part of Welsh kingship.

## Stop: dinas-powys-hillfort
On a small hill near Cardiff, a high-status household drank imported wine and used pottery from the Mediterranean and Gaul. Leslie Alcock dug the site in the 1950s, expecting an Iron Age fort, and found instead imported pottery like that from Tintagel, fragments of Frankish and Anglo-Saxon glass, moulds and crucibles for making fine metalwork, and a great quantity of animal bones from feasting. He read it as the court of a local ruler and thought its ramparts were Norman. A new radiocarbon study published in 2023 has overturned that: after a 5th-century phase of hearths and timber buildings, the ramparts were built in the sixth and seventh centuries, and activity ended in the 7th century. No text names the place, so whether its lords belonged to the kingdom of Glywysing is suggestion only.

## Stop: tintagel-trading-site
On the Cornish coast, Tintagel received far more Mediterranean pottery than any other site in Britain or Ireland, mostly in the late 5th and 6th centuries: fine red bowls from the Aegean and amphorae that had carried wine and oil. The same western sea routes brought imports to Dinas Powys across the Bristol Channel and to sites in Ireland and western Scotland, linking the British west with the eastern Mediterranean long after Roman rule had ended. Excavations since the 1990s have shown the buildings on the headland to be a secular high-status settlement rather than the monastery its first excavator imagined, perhaps a centre of the kings of Dumnonia, which is interpretation. It was a place of power and trade, whatever later legend made of it: the story that Arthur was born here begins with Geoffrey of Monmouth in the 12th century and is kept off the map.

## Stop: eliseg-pillar
In the ninth century a king of Powys set up a stone cross near Llangollen with a long inscription tracing his family back to Roman and British rulers. The king was Concenn (Cyngen), and the cross honoured his great-grandfather Eliseg, who, the inscription said, had won back Powys from the English. It traced the dynasty to Vortigern and to Maximus, the emperor of the journey's first stop, which is dynastic claim rather than history. The text is now illegible and survives because the antiquarian Edward Lhuyd copied it in 1696, apparently with care. Excavations in 2010–12 showed that the mound beneath it is an Early Bronze Age burial cairn, deliberately reused, and the later abbey of Valle Crucis, the valley of the cross, takes its name from the pillar. Concenn died at Rome in 854 or 855, and Powys afterwards came under the kings of Gwynedd.

## Stop: hywel-dda-laws
Welsh law was traditionally said to have been set down under Hywel the Good in the tenth century. Hywel ap Cadell built up rule over much of Wales, visited Rome in 928, and attended the courts of English kings, where he witnessed their charters; a silver penny struck at Chester in his name is the only coin known for a Welsh king. The lawbooks say he gathered men from every district at the White House on the Taf, near Whitland, where the laws were examined, amended and written down. The earliest surviving law books are about 300 years later, the oldest a small Latin manuscript of the mid-13th century, and they differ a good deal, so the map marks this event with low certainty. Historians agree that Hywel's name gave the law its authority, but how much he actually did cannot be shown.

## Stop: gruffudd-sacks-hereford-1055
Before: Gruffudd ap Llywelyn became king of Gwynedd and Powys in 1039, and in the same year defeated a Mercian army at Rhyd-y-groes on the Severn, where Earl Leofric's brother Edwin was killed. For years he fought Gruffudd ap Rhydderch, king of Deheubarth in the south, until in 1055 he killed his rival and took his kingdom. That same year Ælfgar, son of Earl Leofric of Mercia, was outlawed by King Edward, raised ships and men in Ireland and joined him.

What happened: On 24 October 1055 Gruffudd and Ælfgar met the army of Earl Ralph, King Edward's nephew, outside Hereford. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle says the English fled before a spear was thrown, because they had been made to fight on horseback, and that the raiders burned the town and the minster Bishop Æthelstan had built, stripped it of relics and treasures, and killed or carried off many people. John of Worcester, writing in the next century, adds the date and that seven canons were killed defending the church doors, and the Welsh chronicle also records the attack, so the event is secure.

After: Earl Harold led an army to the border and had a ditch dug round Hereford, peace was made, and Ælfgar was restored to his earldom; Gruffudd married Ælfgar's daughter Ealdgyth. By about 1057 he was accepted as king over the whole of Wales, the only ruler ever to achieve that. In 1063 Harold and his brother Tostig invaded Wales by sea and by land, and in August Gruffudd was killed by his own men and his head sent to Harold; Wales then divided again into separate kingdoms. When the Normans reached the border after 1066 they met a divided Wales, which is why historians often treat Gruffudd's death, rather than the Norman Conquest, as the turning point in Welsh history, an interpretation rather than a fact.
