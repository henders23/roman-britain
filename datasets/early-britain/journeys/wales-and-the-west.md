# Wales and the west
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: rulers-and-kingdoms

The west of Britain was conquered by Rome but kept British kingship long after Rome left. This journey begins with the Roman army in Wales, then follows the world that came after, from hilltop courts trading with the Mediterranean to Welsh kings who wrote their claims on stone and in law. Much of the written evidence survives only in much later copies.

## Stop: mona-attack-60
Tacitus says the governor Suetonius Paulinus attacked Anglesey, a refuge for Rome's enemies and a centre of the druids. The map draws the island and strait as a region, because the account names no more precise place.

## Stop: caerleon-amphitheatre
At Caerleon the Second Legion had its fortress, and about AD 90 its soldiers built an amphitheatre outside the walls for displays, training and shows. It is a sign that the army meant to stay.

## Stop: dinas-powys-hillfort
On a small hill near Cardiff, a high-status household drank imported wine and used pottery from the Mediterranean and Gaul. Recent dating places its ramparts in the sixth and seventh centuries.

## Stop: tintagel-trading-site
On the Cornish coast, Tintagel received far more Mediterranean pottery than any other site in Britain. It was a place of power and trade, whatever later legend made of it.

## Stop: eliseg-pillar
In the ninth century a king of Powys set up a stone cross near Llangollen with a long inscription tracing his family back to Roman and British rulers. The text is now illegible and survives because an antiquarian copied it in 1696.

## Stop: hywel-dda-laws
Welsh law was traditionally said to have been set down under Hywel the Good in the tenth century. The earliest surviving law books are about 300 years later, so the map marks this event with low certainty.

## Closing
Rome ruled Wales through its army, and when the army went, British rulers stayed. The ground shows their wealth and their links to the Mediterranean; their own words survive in a copied inscription and much later law books. That is why the west after Rome is told mostly by archaeology.
