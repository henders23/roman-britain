# Vikings in the north
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: vikings-and-england

After the raid on Lindisfarne in 793, what did Scandinavians do in northern Britain: raid, trade or settle? This journey follows them for nearly three centuries, from a monastery on an island to a Norwegian king's last battle near York.

## Stop: lindisfarne-raid-793
In 793 raiders from the sea attacked the monastery on Lindisfarne. It is the first Viking raid on a major English church that the sources describe in detail. The northern versions of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle record portents and famine over Northumbria, then heathen men destroying God's church on the island with plunder and slaughter. Alcuin of York, then at Charlemagne's court, wrote soon afterwards to the king of Northumbria and to the community, and read the attack as a judgement on Northumbrian sins, which is his view rather than a report. No source names the raiders' homeland, though they are generally thought to have come from Norway, and the community survived on the island for another eighty years.

## Stop: dumbarton-siege-870
In 870 Scandinavian kings from Dublin besieged Alt Clut, the rock fortress of the Britons on the Clyde, for four months and destroyed it. The Annals of Ulster name the kings as Amlaíb and Ímar, and another Irish annal account adds that the defenders gave in when their water failed. The next year the two kings returned to Dublin with two hundred ships and a great haul of captives, English, British and Pictish. Excavation on the rock in 1974–75 found an early medieval rampart with sherds of 6th-century Mediterranean amphorae, signs of a long-lived royal centre. Vikings in Ireland were now reaching deep into northern Britain, and afterwards the British kings seem to have centred further up the Clyde around Govan, though how quickly that happened is debated.

## Stop: cuthbert-community-leaves-lindisfarne-875
Five years later, as a Viking army moved north, the community of St Cuthbert left Lindisfarne with the saint's body. The army was Halfdan's part of the great army, which had taken York in 866 and wintered at Repton in 873–4. The community's own later histories say Bishop Eardwulf and Abbot Eadred carried the body about Northumbria for some years before settling at Chester-le-Street, the first step on the road to Durham. Those histories were written long afterwards to support the community's claims, and its centre may already have shifted to Norham on the Tweed, so the departure may have been less sudden than the story tells. Even so, raiding had become conquest.

## Stop: cuerdale-hoard
Around 905–910 a chest of about 40 kilograms of silver was buried at a crossing of the Ribble in Lancashire. Workmen repairing the embankment found it in May 1840: about 7,000 coins and more than 1,000 pieces of ingots, ornaments and hack-silver. Its coins come from Viking York, East Anglia, the Frankish lands and the Islamic world. The cut-up jewellery and ingots show silver being used by weight as well as by coin, in a trade that ran across the Irish Sea. Who buried it, and why, are not known; a common view links it to the Norse driven out of Dublin in 902, which would tie it to the same Dublin world whose kings had taken Alt Clut a generation earlier.

## Stop: coppergate-tenements
In York, excavation at Coppergate found tenth-century houses and workshops preserved in wet ground. York Archaeological Trust dug the site between 1976 and 1981, under Richard Hall, and recovered some 40,000 finds from four long plots laid out around the 930s. The finds show woodturning, metalworking, jewellery-making, leatherworking and textile work, with trade contacts reaching the Baltic and beyond. The Vikings who had come as raiders were now townspeople, craftsmen and traders, though the finds point to a mixed Anglo-Scandinavian population, so calling the street simply 'Viking' is an interpretation. The kingdom of York ended in 954, when the Northumbrians drove out its last king, Eric, but the town and its people carried on.

## Stop: battle-of-stamford-bridge-1066
In September 1066 the Norwegian king Harald Hardrada invaded and was killed by King Harold at Stamford Bridge, near York. Joined by Harold's exiled brother Tostig, Hardrada landed on the Humber, beat the northern earls at Fulford on 20 September and received the submission of York. Harold marched north at great speed and surprised the Norwegians at the crossing of the Derwent on 25 September; Tostig was killed too, and the survivors left in a small part of their fleet. The story of a lone Norwegian holding the bridge comes from a later addition to the Chronicle and is best treated as tradition, and no battle archaeology has confirmed where the fighting took place. It was the last great Norwegian invasion of England.

## Closing
In the north, Vikings came first as raiders, then as armies and kings, and then as settlers and traders whose silver and streets archaeologists still find. The story ends where it was strongest, near York, with a Norwegian king's defeat. Three days later another descendant of Scandinavians, William of Normandy, landed in the south.
