# Defending the late Roman province
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: conquest-and-frontiers

In its last century Roman Britain faced raiders by sea and from the north, and its own commanders made themselves emperors. This journey follows how the province was defended, and how it slipped out of Roman control.

## Stop: portchester-shore-fort
Portchester's walls still stand almost to their full height, with projecting D-shaped towers round about three and a half hectares on a promontory in Portsmouth Harbour. It is one of the forts built along the south and east coasts in the late third century, later grouped under a commander called the Count of the Saxon Shore. Coins show it was built after about 268, and its excavator, Barry Cunliffe, concluded that it was founded under Carausius, the next stop; that is an inference, not a record. Its Roman name is usually given as Portus Adurni, from a list of commands compiled around 400, but even that identification is uncertain. Who the forts were meant to keep out is still debated.

## Stop: carausius-usurpation-286
Carausius, a Menapian from the Low Countries, was given a fleet based at Boulogne to clear the Channel of Frankish and Saxon raiders, and used it to make himself emperor of Britain. Writers of the 360s say he was suspected of letting the raiders strike so that he could seize their plunder, and that when Maximian condemned him he took the purple. He held Britain and part of northern Gaul until his own finance minister, Allectus, murdered him in 293. His coins, minted in London and at a second mint, are the best contemporary evidence: they promise a returning golden age in words borrowed from Virgil, and claim a partnership with the emperors Diocletian and Maximian that they never accepted. Some historians think the shore forts were his work.

## Stop: barbarian-conspiracy-367
Ammianus Marcellinus describes Picts, Scots and Attacotti attacking Britain together in 367, while Franks and Saxons raided the coasts of Gaul. One senior commander, Nectaridus, count of the coastal region, was killed and another, the general Fullofaudes, ambushed; the areani, scouts meant to watch the peoples beyond the frontier, had joined in. Count Theodosius crossed from Boulogne, cleared raiding bands near London, disbanded the areani and restored cities and forts. Whether the raids were really co-ordinated, or a story that made the recovery look more heroic, is debated: Ammianus wrote about twenty years later, under an emperor who was Theodosius's son. Burnt layers at sites across Britain that were once dated to 367 are now mostly not accepted as evidence of the attacks.

## Stop: constantine-iii-proclaimed-407
In 407 the army in Britain raised its own emperor, who took troops to Gaul. Zosimus says the soldiers first proclaimed a man called Marcus and killed him, then a local man, Gratian, whom they killed after four months, before choosing Constantine, a common soldier who, Orosius says, was picked for the hope his name inspired. He made himself master of Gaul and Spain and was for a time recognised by the emperor Honorius, but he was captured and executed in 411. The sources disagree on the years and say nothing of where in Britain he was proclaimed. For the province, defending itself and choosing its own rulers had become the same thing.

## Stop: hoxne-hoard
Around this time someone in Suffolk buried a fortune and never returned for it. Found by a metal detectorist in 1992 and excavated the next day, it had been packed in a wooden chest: 14,865 gold, silver and bronze coins, most of them silver, with about 200 pieces of gold jewellery and silver tableware, among them a gold body-chain and pepper-pots. The latest coins are two silver pieces of Constantine III, so the hoard cannot have been buried before 407–408, and the heavy clipping of many coins suggests some years may have passed. Hoards like this are often read as signs of insecurity, but they cannot tell us why their owners never came back.

## Closing
The province was defended with new forts, and its commanders more than once turned its army into a throne. Each usurper took troops away, and after 407 they did not come back. The Hoxne hoard, buried some time after 408, belongs to a Britain that Rome no longer defended.
