# Vikings in England
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: vikings-and-england

How did raiders from Scandinavia become rulers and settlers in England, and how long did their kingdom at York last? This journey follows the Scandinavian presence from the raid on Lindisfarne in 793, the first recorded attack on a major English church, to the expulsion of the last king of York in 954: the shift from raids to an army that conquered, the settlement and trade that followed, and the English kings' slow recovery of the north. Most of the dates come from the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, written from the West Saxon point of view; the archaeology at Repton, Cuerdale and York adds evidence the chroniclers did not write down.

The story of how Wessex grew into a kingdom of all the English is told in the journey The making of England, and the second wave of attacks under Æthelred and Cnut in From Æthelred to Cnut.

## Stop: lindisfarne-raid-793
Before: In the late 8th century Scandinavia was a land of farming communities ruled by chieftains and local kings, already linked by trade to the markets of Frisia and England. Its shipbuilders made long, shallow, clinker-built vessels with oars and sail, able to cross the North Sea and be run up on a beach. The Chronicle records that in the days of Beorhtric of Wessex, around 789, three ships of Northmen came to the Dorset coast, at Portland according to later West Saxon writers, and killed the king's reeve, who had ridden out to meet them thinking they were traders. The northern versions say the ships came from Hordaland in western Norway, and the Chronicle calls them the first ships of Danish men to reach England.

What happened: The northern versions of the Chronicle (D and E) enter the raid under 793, after portents over Northumbria and a famine: heathen men destroyed God's church on Lindisfarne with plunder and slaughter. Alcuin of York, then at Charlemagne's court, confirms it in letters written soon afterwards to King Æthelred of Northumbria and to Bishop Higbald and the community. The Chronicle also gives a day, but it falls in January, an unlikely season for a sea raid, and the summer day often quoted instead is a modern correction rather than a reading of the text; so the atlas dates the raid to the year only, a reminder that even a precise date can rest on judgement. No source names the raiders' homeland, though they are generally taken to have come from Norway.

After: Alcuin wrote that such a terror had never before appeared in Britain and that no one had thought such a voyage possible; he read the attack as a judgement on Northumbrian sins, which is his interpretation rather than a report. The next year raiders plundered a monastery at the mouth of the Don, usually taken to be Jarrow, and Irish annals record attacks on Iona from 795. For some forty years the attacks recorded in England were sporadic hit-and-run raids on coasts and churches; from the 830s the fleets grew larger, and by the 850s they had begun to stay over the winter. That 793 began a new age is a convention, made sharper by the shock that Alcuin's letters record.

## Stop: great-army-takes-york-866
Before: In 865 a much larger force, which the Chronicle calls the great heathen army, landed in East Anglia, wintered there and took horses from the East Angles in return for peace. Its leaders are named by later sources as Ivar and Halfdan; the Chronicle names Halfdan among its kings a few years later. Northumbria, the obvious next target, was divided against itself: its people had deposed King Osberht and accepted Ælla, a king not of royal birth.

What happened: In late 866 the army crossed the Humber and seized York. The Chronicle, under 867 because its year began in the autumn, and Asser's Life of Alfred give the story; the day, 1 November, comes from later northern tradition, and some modern accounts give 21 November, so the map shows the month only. Only then did Osberht and Ælla join forces: on 21 March 867, by the same northern tradition, they broke into the city, but both kings were killed with many of their men and the survivors made peace. The later Scandinavian story that Ælla was put to death by the 'blood eagle' is saga tradition, and the pack excludes it.

After: York gave the army a base in the richest city of the north. Within a few years East Anglia fell, its king Edmund killed in 869, and Mercia followed, as the next stop shows, leaving Wessex the only English kingdom standing. In 876, the Chronicle says, Halfdan shared out the land of the Northumbrians, and they began to plough and to support themselves: York became the centre of a Scandinavian kingdom that lasted, with interruptions, until 954. Historians commonly treat 866–7 as the point at which Viking activity in England turned from raiding to conquest and settlement.

## Stop: repton-winter-camp-873
Seven years after taking York, the great army moved from Lindsey to Repton, a Mercian royal centre and the burial place of its kings, and wintered there in 873–4. The Chronicle says it drove King Burgred overseas, where he went to Rome and died, and gave the kingdom to Ceolwulf, a king's thegn who promised to serve the army. Excavation by Martin Biddle and Birthe Kjølbye-Biddle in the 1970s and 1980s found a ditch that used St Wystan's church as part of a D-shaped enclosure open to the Trent, and burials with Scandinavian weapons and ornaments, one of them a man buried with a sword and a Thor's hammer pendant. Nearby, a stone building reused as a mortuary chamber held the bones of at least 264 people. Their dating was argued over for decades because earlier radiocarbon results came out too old; a 2018 study correcting for the marine diet of the dead brought them into line with the 870s. That the enclosure, the burials and the charnel all belong to the army is interpretation, strengthened but not proved by the dating. From Repton the army split: Halfdan took his men north to Northumbria, while others under Guthrum went to Cambridge and then turned on Wessex.

## Stop: battle-of-edington-878
Guthrum's attack on the royal estate at Chippenham at midwinter 878 nearly ended Wessex, and Alfred withdrew into the Somerset marshes at Athelney. In early to mid May he gathered the men of Somerset, Wiltshire and part of Hampshire at Egbert's Stone and defeated Guthrum's army at Ethandun, then besieged it in its fortress until it gave hostages. Guthrum was baptised soon afterwards with Alfred as his godfather and led his army to East Anglia, where he ruled as a Christian king. A treaty between the two kings later set a frontier along the Thames, the Lea and Watling Street, with Scandinavian rule beyond it in the region later called the Danelaw. Ethandun is usually identified as Edington in Wiltshire, but no battlefield finds confirm it and a Somerset Edington has also been proposed, so the map draws a small region.

## Stop: cuerdale-hoard
In May 1840 workmen repairing the bank of the Ribble at Cuerdale, near Preston, found a lead chest holding about 40 kg of silver: some 7,000 coins and more than 1,000 pieces of ingots, ornaments and hack-silver. It is the largest Viking silver hoard known from Britain and Ireland. Thousands of the coins were struck by the Scandinavian rulers of York and East Anglia, beside Anglo-Saxon, Carolingian and Islamic pieces, and the latest of them date the burial to about 905–910. The cut-up jewellery and ingots show silver being used by weight as well as by coin, much of it reaching England across the Irish Sea. Why so much silver was buried by the river is interpreted rather than known: a common view links it to the Norse expelled from Dublin in 902, or to a war-chest for a campaign, but the hoard cannot say whose it was.

## Stop: coppergate-tenements
In the generations after the conquest, York grew into a busy manufacturing and trading town. Excavation by York Archaeological Trust at 16–22 Coppergate between 1976 and 1981, under Richard Hall, recovered some 40,000 finds from four long property plots running back from the street, laid out around the 930s. Their post-and-wattle buildings were replaced later in the century by plank-built houses and workshops with semi-basements, and the wet ground preserved wood, leather, textiles, plants and even insects. They show woodturning, metalworking, jewellery-making, leatherworking and textile production, and trade contacts reaching the Baltic and beyond, dated by tree-rings, coins and the order of the layers. This is how Viking-age York is known, far better than from any written source. The site is often presented as 'Viking' York, but the finds point to a mixed Anglo-Scandinavian population, so labels of ethnic identity are interpretation.

## Stop: battle-of-brunanburh-937
By now the kings of Wessex had pushed north. Æthelstan took York in 927 and claimed lordship over the other rulers of Britain, and in 937 Olaf Guthfrithson of Dublin, whose father had been driven from York, combined with Constantine II of Alba and the Strathclyde Britons against him. Æthelstan and his brother Edmund defeated them, and the Chronicle celebrates the victory with a poem, which says the defeated Norse fled by ship over Dingesmere to Dublin. Where it was fought is still debated. Bromborough on the Wirral, whose name is widely thought to derive from Brunanburh, is the leading candidate, so the map draws only that area; the card names the others, among them Burnswark in Dumfriesshire and Lanchester in County Durham. The victory did not end Scandinavian York: after Æthelstan's death in 939 Olaf was accepted as king there.

## Stop: eric-bloodaxe-expelled-954
The end of Scandinavian York came quietly. After 939 the city changed hands several times between Norse kings from Dublin and the English kings Edmund and Eadred, and Eric, who ruled York briefly around 947–8 and again from about 952, was its last independent king. In 954, the Chronicle says in a single sentence, the Northumbrians drove Eric out and Eadred succeeded to their kingdom; it gives no place, so the map draws a halo over the kingdom of York. Roger of Wendover, writing in the 13th century, adds that Eric was betrayed by Oswulf of Bamburgh and killed by one Maccus at Stainmore, the high pass over the Pennines; because he is late, that killing is told here but not mapped. Eric of York is usually identified with Eric Bloodaxe of the Norse sagas, son of Harald Finehair of Norway, but that identification has been questioned and is still debated. After 954 Northumbria was governed by earls for the English king, though the north kept its own laws and its Scandinavian character for long afterwards.

## Closing
Raids became armies, and armies became settlers, kings and traders. The raid on Lindisfarne shocked the Christian world; the great army that took York in 866 came to stay, and the archaeology of Repton, Cuerdale and Coppergate shows a conquering army, silver moving by weight across the Irish Sea, and a manufacturing town of mixed Anglo-Scandinavian people. Wessex survived and pushed north, winning at Brunanburh in 937, but the kingdom of York outlived that victory and ended only in 954, when the Northumbrians drove out Eric. The answer to the opening question is that Scandinavian rule at York lasted, on and off, for nearly ninety years, and its people, place-names and customs lasted far longer.
