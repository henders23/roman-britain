# Vikings and the making of England
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Order: chronological

This journey follows the Scandinavian presence in England from the first raid to the battle that is often said to have made England one kingdom. Most of the dates come from the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, written from the West Saxon point of view; the archaeology at Repton, Cuerdale and York adds evidence the chroniclers did not write down.

## Stop: lindisfarne-raid-793
The Chronicle records the raid on Lindisfarne in 793. Its exact day is a scholarly correction of the manuscript text, which is a reminder that even a precise date can rest on judgement.

## Stop: repton-winter-camp-873
The Chronicle says the great army wintered at Repton in 873–4. Excavation found an earthwork enclosure by the church and a mass burial of more than 260 people, whose dating has been argued over for decades.

## Stop: battle-of-edington-878
Alfred's victory at Ethandun forced the Viking leader Guthrum to terms. Ethandun is usually identified as Edington in Wiltshire, but not everyone agrees, so the map draws a small region.

## Stop: cuerdale-hoard
Over 8,500 pieces of silver, buried beside the Ribble in the early tenth century: coins from Scandinavian York, from Wessex and from the Islamic world, and cut-up jewellery used as bullion.

## Stop: coppergate-tenements
Excavation in York found timber houses and workshops of the tenth century, preserved in wet ground, with the debris of everyday crafts. This is how Viking-age York is known, far better than from any written source.

## Stop: battle-of-brunanburh-937
Athelstan's victory over a coalition of Norse and Scottish kings was celebrated in a poem in the Chronicle. Where it was fought is still debated; the map draws only one of the candidate areas, the Wirral, and names the others in the card.
