# Why bury treasure?
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: how-do-we-know

Hoards are usually found by chance, by ploughmen, builders or metal detectorists. But why were they buried in the first place: for safety, as an offering, or as loot? This journey visits five hoards from the fourth to the tenth century and asks what each can and cannot tell us.

## Stop: mildenhall-treasure
About 34 pieces of fine fourth-century silver tableware, led by the Great Dish, were reported as ploughed up near Mildenhall in 1942. How and where it was found has itself been questioned, which makes it harder still to say why it was buried.

## Stop: water-newton-treasure
Silver vessels and thin plaques, many marked with the chi-rho, were ploughed up inside the Roman town of Durobrivae. They look like the property of a church. Why a congregation buried them is unknown.

## Stop: hoxne-hoard
A chest packed with gold and silver and almost fifteen thousand coins was buried at Hoxne after about 408. It is tempting to read it as wealth hidden for safety as Roman rule ended, but that is an interpretation; the chest cannot say.

## Stop: staffordshire-hoard
In the heart of Mercia, someone buried a mass of gold and silver war-gear in the mid seventh century, most of it broken or stripped from weapons. It may be loot from battles, but nobody knows.

## Stop: cuerdale-hoard
Around 905–910 a lead chest of about 40 kilograms of silver was buried beside the Ribble in Lancashire. It is the largest Viking silver hoard from Britain and Ireland, and its coins come from across the Viking world.

## Closing
A hoard tells us when it was buried, from its latest coins or objects, and what wealth looked like. It almost never tells us why. Safety, offering, loot or a store of pay are all possible, and most hoards keep that secret. That is why the atlas marks so many of them as interpretation.
