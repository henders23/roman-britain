# Why bury treasure?
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: how-do-we-know

Hoards are usually found by chance, by ploughmen, builders or metal detectorists. But why were they buried in the first place: for safety, as an offering, or as loot? This journey visits five hoards from the fourth to the tenth century and asks what each can and cannot tell us.

## Stop: mildenhall-treasure
About 34 pieces of fine fourth-century silver tableware, led by the Great Dish, were reported as ploughed up near Mildenhall in 1942. By the accepted account, Gordon Butcher struck the silver while ploughing at West Row that January, and his employer, Sydney Ford, kept it at home until a visitor recognised it in 1946. An inquest then declared it treasure trove, and it went to the British Museum. The Great Dish, over 8 kilograms of silver, shows a mask of Oceanus and a Bacchic procession, while a few pieces carry the chi-rho. How and where it was found has itself been questioned, which makes it harder still to say why it was buried; it is dated only by style, and its owner is unknown.

## Stop: water-newton-treasure
Silver vessels and thin plaques, many marked with the chi-rho, were ploughed up inside the Roman town of Durobrivae in the mid 1970s. There are nine vessels, among them a jug, bowls and a two-handled cup, with a set of leaf-shaped plaques and one small gold disc. Some pieces name the people who gave them, such as Publianus on a bowl. They look like the property of a church, the earliest known group of probable church silver from anywhere in the Roman Empire, though that use is inferred from the inscriptions and symbols. The leaf-shaped plaques resemble offerings left at pagan shrines, a sign of older habits carried into the new religion. Why a congregation buried them is unknown.

## Stop: hoxne-hoard
A chest packed with gold and silver and almost fifteen thousand coins was buried at Hoxne after about 408. Eric Lawes found it in 1992 while searching with a metal detector for a farmer's lost hammer, and because he stopped and reported it, archaeologists excavated it the next day. They recorded the remains of the wooden chest, with some objects in smaller boxes or wrapped in cloth, among them a gold body-chain, bracelets, and silver spoons and pepper-pots. The latest coins, two silver siliquae of Constantine III struck in 407–408, give the earliest date it could have been buried, and many silver coins are heavily clipped, which some take as a sign that burial came years later. It is tempting to read it as wealth hidden for safety as Roman rule ended, but that is an interpretation; the chest cannot say.

## Stop: staffordshire-hoard
In the heart of Mercia, someone buried a mass of gold and silver war-gear in the mid seventh century, most of it broken or stripped from weapons. The metal detectorist Terry Herbert found the first pieces near Hammerwich in July 2009, and excavation and later ploughing brought the total to nearly 4,600 fragments. Most come from sword hilts, a helmet and other fittings decorated with garnets and filigree, but there are also a few Christian objects, such as crosses and an inscribed strip. There are no coins, so the date comes from style and manufacture: the published study puts the latest pieces around 660 and the burial at about 650–675. It may be loot from battles, tribute or an offering, but nobody knows.

## Stop: cuerdale-hoard
Around 905–910 a lead chest of about 40 kilograms of silver was buried beside the Ribble in Lancashire. It is the largest Viking silver hoard from Britain and Ireland, and its coins come from across the Viking world. Workmen repairing the river bank found it in May 1840: some 7,000 coins and more than 1,000 pieces of ingots, ornaments and hack-silver, with bone pins thought to have fastened cloth bags. The latest coins date the burial, but its purpose is guessed: a common view links it to the Norse driven out of Dublin in 902, and others see a war-chest for a campaign. Nobody knows whose it was.

## Closing
A hoard tells us when it was buried, from its latest coins or objects, and what wealth looked like. It almost never tells us why. Safety, offering, loot or a store of pay are all possible, and most hoards keep that secret. That is why the atlas marks so many of them as interpretation.
