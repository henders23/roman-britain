# Early medieval Britain in six stops
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: start-here

In the early 600s a man was buried in a ship under a mound at Sutton Hoo. By 1066 England was a single kingdom that two foreign rulers thought worth invading. This short journey is a first look at the centuries in between: kings, conversion, a great historian, the Vikings, and the end of the period.

## Stop: sutton-hoo-mound1
Under the largest mound at Sutton Hoo, a man was buried in a chamber built inside a ship with some of the richest goods known from early medieval Europe. He is often identified as King Rædwald of East Anglia, but that is an interpretation. Kings of this age are known more from what they were buried with than from writing.

## Stop: synod-of-whitby-664
In 664 the king of Northumbria held a meeting at Whitby to settle how to date Easter, and chose the Roman way over the Irish. It was a sign of how far Christianity, newly brought by missions from Iona and Rome, now shaped the kingdoms.

## Stop: bede-historia-731
At Jarrow in 731 the monk Bede finished his Ecclesiastical History of the English People. Much of what we know about the two centuries before him comes from this one book.

## Stop: lindisfarne-raid-793
In 793 raiders from the sea attacked the monastery on Lindisfarne. It is the first Viking raid on a major English church that the sources describe in detail, and the start of two centuries of raids, armies and settlement.

## Stop: battle-of-edington-878
In 878 Alfred of Wessex, nearly beaten earlier that year, defeated the Viking army at Ethandun. Its leader, Guthrum, accepted baptism. Wessex survived, and Alfred's family went on to rule much of England.

## Stop: battle-of-hastings-1066
On 14 October 1066 William of Normandy defeated and killed King Harold near Hastings. The English kingdom that had grown out of these centuries passed to a Norman king, and the atlas's timeline ends here.

## Closing
In under five hundred years, scattered kingdoms became one English kingdom, and pagan kings became Christian ones. Much of the early story depends on one writer, Bede; much of the later story on chronicles kept for kings. Each of the other journeys looks more closely at part of it.
