# Early medieval Britain in six stops
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: start-here

In the early 600s a man was buried in a ship under a mound at Sutton Hoo. By 1066 England was a single kingdom that two foreign rulers thought worth invading. This short journey is a first look at the centuries in between: kings, conversion, a great historian, the Vikings, and the end of the period.

## Stop: sutton-hoo-mound1
Under the largest mound at Sutton Hoo, a man was buried in a chamber built inside a ship with some of the richest goods known from early medieval Europe. Basil Brown, digging for the landowner Edith Pretty, reached the mound in 1939 and found the outline of a ship about 27 metres long, its timbers rotted away but its rivets still in place in the sand. No body was found, probably because the acid soil dissolved it, but the chamber held a helmet, a great gold buckle, gold and garnet fittings, silver from the eastern Mediterranean and a purse of Frankish gold coins. The coins show that the burial took place after about 613 or the 620s. He is often identified as King Rædwald of East Anglia, who died about 624, but no inscription names him, so that is an interpretation. Kings of this age are known more from what they were buried with than from writing. Bede says Rædwald was baptised in Kent but kept a pagan altar beside a Christian one, a sign of a kingdom halfway between two faiths.

## Stop: synod-of-whitby-664
In 664 the king of Northumbria held a meeting at Whitby to settle how to date Easter, and chose the Roman way over the Irish. King Oswiu heard the case at Abbess Hild's monastery, with Bishop Colman of Lindisfarne speaking for the practice of Iona and Wilfrid for that of Rome. The difference was not academic: his own queen followed Rome, so the court sometimes kept Easter on different days. Bede says Oswiu chose Rome because its tradition claimed the authority of St Peter, and that Colman then left for Iona and Ireland; the speeches are reconstructions written a generation or two later. It was a sign of how far Christianity, newly brought by missions from Iona and Rome, now shaped the kingdoms.

## Stop: bede-historia-731
In 731 the monk Bede finished his Ecclesiastical History of the English People. He had entered the twin monastery of Wearmouth–Jarrow at the age of seven and lived at Jarrow, though he does not say in which house he wrote. He gathered material by letter from Canterbury and other churches and usually says where it came from, but he wrote as a churchman with firm views on what mattered, including the correct date of Easter. Two copies made within a few years of his death in 735 still survive, so the text is securely known. Much of what we know about the two centuries before him comes from this one book, including the Synod of Whitby at the last stop.

## Stop: lindisfarne-raid-793
In 793 raiders from the sea attacked the monastery on Lindisfarne. It is the first Viking raid on a major English church that the sources describe in detail, and the start of two centuries of raids, armies and settlement. The northern versions of the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle record it, and Alcuin of York, then at Charlemagne's court, wrote soon afterwards that such a terror had never been seen in Britain. The Chronicle's day falls in January, an unlikely season for a sea raid, and the summer day often quoted is a modern correction, so the atlas dates the raid to the year only. The community survived and stayed on the island for another eighty years. For decades the attacks were raids on coasts and churches; from 865 a great army came to conquer kingdoms, and only Wessex was left standing.

## Stop: battle-of-edington-878
In 878 Alfred of Wessex, nearly beaten earlier that year, defeated the Viking army at Ethandun. At midwinter Guthrum's army had seized the royal estate at Chippenham, and Alfred withdrew into the Somerset marshes at Athelney. In early to mid May he gathered the men of Somerset, Wiltshire and part of Hampshire, won the battle and besieged the army in its fortress until it gave hostages. Its leader, Guthrum, accepted baptism with Alfred as his godfather and went on to rule East Anglia as a Christian king. Ethandun is usually taken to be Edington in Wiltshire, but no battlefield finds confirm it, so the map draws a region. Wessex survived, and Alfred's family went on to rule much of England; his grandson Æthelstan was the first to rule all the English.

## Stop: battle-of-hastings-1066
On 14 October 1066 William of Normandy defeated and killed King Harold near Hastings. Harold had been crowned in January, the day after Edward the Confessor died childless, and in September he defeated the Norwegian king Harald Hardrada at Stamford Bridge, only to hear that William had landed at Pevensey. The battle lasted all day, and Harold's brothers Gyrth and Leofwine died with him; how Harold himself died is uncertain. William founded Battle Abbey on the site, and that tradition, rather than any battle archaeology, is the main evidence for where the fighting took place. The English kingdom that had grown out of these centuries passed to a Norman king, and the atlas's timeline ends here.

## Closing
In under five hundred years, scattered kingdoms became one English kingdom, and pagan kings became Christian ones. Much of the early story depends on one writer, Bede; much of the later story on chronicles kept for kings. Each of the other journeys looks more closely at part of it.
