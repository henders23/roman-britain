# Ireland: saints, Vikings and high kings
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Order: chronological

How was Ireland's history bound up with Britain's across the Irish Sea, from a British slave who became Ireland's bishop to the Norse kings of Dublin who fought for York? This journey follows those links through saints, Viking raiders and the kings who tried to rule all Ireland.

Be warned that it has only a few stops. The atlas is built around Britain, and only a handful of Irish events are on the map, chosen where Ireland's story touches Britain's; two of the stops here are in Britain itself. This is a route through the links between the islands, not a history of Ireland, and at several stops the story most people know is a later legend that the pack sets aside.

## Stop: patrick-mission-ireland
Before: By the 5th century Britain had a Christian church, and Irish raiders were taking captives from its coasts. Patrick, the son of a deacon and town councillor with a small estate somewhere in Britain, was carried off to Ireland at about sixteen and spent six years tending flocks before escaping. Christianity was already reaching Ireland by other routes: a contemporary chronicle records that in 431 Pope Celestine sent Palladius as first bishop to the Irish believing in Christ.

What happened: Years after his escape, Patrick says, he dreamed he heard the people of the wood of Foclut, near the western sea, calling him back, and he returned as a bishop, baptising thousands and ordaining clergy among peoples he describes as living at the edge of the world. All this comes from his own two writings, the Confessio and an angry letter to the soldiers of a British ruler, Coroticus, who had killed and enslaved newly baptised converts. They are the only 5th-century texts from Ireland, but they give no dates and name no other place in Ireland. Scholars place the mission somewhere in the 5th century, several favouring the second half, and the map draws a halo over the north and west because that is all the evidence supports.

After: In the later 7th century writers at Armagh made Patrick the apostle of all Ireland and founder of their church, in support of Armagh's claim to lead the Irish church; the banishing of the snakes and the shamrock come later still. These legends are excluded in the pack. Historians see Patrick's mission as one part of a gradual conversion, out of which grew an Irish church rich in monasteries, the church that would send Columba to Britain.

## Stop: columba-iona-563
A century or so after Patrick the traffic ran the other way, as an Irish prince-monk founded a monastery in Britain. Columba, of the ruling Uí Néill kindred of the north of Ireland and already the founder of churches at home, sailed to Iona, by tradition in 563, two years after the battle of Cúl Drebene, according to his biographer Adomnán. Iona became the head of a family of monasteries in Ireland and northern Britain, and its monks carried Christianity to the Picts and, through Aidan, to the Northumbrian English. Columba died there in 597, and Iona stayed an Irish monastery in its language, learning and links, with its abbots coming and going between the islands.

## Stop: dublin-longphort-841
Before: The first Viking raid on Ireland recorded in the annals came in 795, when the heathens burned Rechru, an island off the coast. Iona was attacked the same year, burned in 802, and in 806 sixty-eight of its community were killed; the next year work began on a new monastery of Columba at Kells, in the Irish midlands, which in time became the community's chief house. For forty years the raiders came and went with the seasons, but in the 830s the fleets grew larger, and in 837 the annals report sixty ships on the Boyne and sixty on the Liffey.

What happened: In 841 the Annals of Ulster record Viking ship-camps, longphoirt, at Linn Duachaill in County Louth and at Duiblinn on the Liffey, and say the Laigin and the Uí Néill were plundered from Duiblinn as far as the Slieve Bloom mountains; in 842 the heathens were still there. This marks the change from summer raids to camps kept through the winter. The annals were kept by contemporary clerics, so the year is secure, but the site is not: many scholars place the camp by the 'black pool' just south of today's Dublin Castle, others upstream at Kilmainham and Islandbridge, where the largest group of Viking-age graves outside Scandinavia was found, and the map shows a halo over both.

After: Duiblinn became the main Scandinavian base in Ireland, and its kings were soon active right around the Irish Sea, as the next stop shows. The Irish expelled them in 902, but they returned in 917, and Dublin grew into a town that lived by trade, including the trade in slaves. Historians generally treat 841 as the start of permanent Viking settlement in Ireland and of Dublin as a town, although how town-like the first camp was is debated.

## Stop: dumbarton-siege-870
From Dublin, its kings struck deep into northern Britain. In 870 Amlaíb and Ímar, whom the Annals of Ulster call kings of the Northmen, besieged Dumbarton Rock, the fortress of the Britons of the Clyde, for four months, until the defenders gave in when their water failed. The next year they returned to Dublin with two hundred ships carrying English, British and Pictish captives, a sign of how central slaving was to Dublin's wealth. When Ímar died in 873 the annals called him king of the Northmen of all Ireland and Britain, and his descendants went on to rule Dublin and to compete for York for the next eighty years.

## Stop: battle-of-brunanburh-937
Ímar's dynasty brought Dublin into the wars for England. Olaf Guthfrithson, king of Dublin, was the son of Guthfrith, who had briefly taken York in 927 before Æthelstan drove him out, and in 937 Olaf crossed to Britain in alliance with Constantín of Alba and the Strathclyde Britons. Æthelstan and his brother Edmund defeated them at Brunanburh, and the Chronicle's poem on the battle says the surviving Norse fled by ship over Dingesmere back to Dublin. Where the battle was fought is still disputed; the map shows a halo on the Wirral, the leading candidate. The defeat did not end Dublin's ambitions: after Æthelstan died in 939 Olaf was accepted as king at York, though he died two years later.

## Stop: battle-of-clontarf-1014
Before: Brian Bóruma came from the Dál Cais, a minor dynasty of north Munster, and with his brother Mathgamain fought his way to power; after Mathgamain was killed in 976 Brian became king of Munster. In 997 he and Máel Sechnaill, the Uí Néill king of Tara, divided Ireland between them, and two years later Brian defeated Leinster and the Dublin Norse at Glenn Máma and took Dublin. By 1002 Máel Sechnaill had submitted to him, and in 1005 a note in the Book of Armagh styles him emperor of the Irish. In 1013 Leinster and Dublin rose against him.

What happened: On Good Friday, 23 April 1014, Brian's army met the men of Leinster, the Norse of Dublin and their overseas allies, among them Sigurd, earl of Orkney, at Clontarf on the north shore of Dublin Bay. The Annals of Ulster and of Inisfallen, kept close to the time, record a hard-fought battle in which the Leinstermen and foreigners were routed with great slaughter, but in which Brian, his son Murchad and his grandson Tairdelbach were killed. The day comes from a later and more colourful account, and the exact field is unknown, so the map shows a small halo rather than a pin.

After: Brian's attempt to make the kingship of Ireland real for his own dynasty died with him, and Máel Sechnaill took up the high kingship again. Dublin was not conquered: its king, Sigtrygg Silkbeard, had not fought, kept his throne until 1036, and the town stayed under Norse rule until the English took it in 1170. In the early 12th century Brian's descendants, the Uí Briain, probably had his story told in Cogadh Gáedel re Gallaib, 'The War of the Irish against the Foreigners', which cast Clontarf as the climax of a national struggle; the belief that the battle drove the Vikings out of Ireland grew from it and is excluded in the pack. Historians now usually treat Clontarf as a war over Brian's overkingship, with Leinster and Dublin resisting him with Scandinavian help.
