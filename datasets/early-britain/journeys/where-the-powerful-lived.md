# Where the powerful lived
By: UK Atlas (atlas synthesis)
Collection: towns-trade-and-everyday-life

How did the wealthy show their power? This journey visits five homes of the powerful, from a Roman palace to an early English royal settlement, and asks what changed and what stayed the same once Rome had gone.

## Stop: fishbourne-palace
Near Chichester, a vast courtyard residence was built in the later first century, with mosaics, painted walls and a formal garden. It was found by workmen trenching for a water main, and Barry Cunliffe excavated it between 1961 and 1969. Beneath it lay military and timber buildings from the time of the conquest; then came four great wings around a garden whose planting beds could still be traced in the soil, and some of the earliest mosaics in Britain. It is the largest Roman residence known north of the Alps. Cunliffe dated it to the 70s, while Miles Russell has argued for the 90s. Who lived there is argued: perhaps a British king who ruled as Rome's ally, Togidubnus, known from an inscription at Chichester, perhaps a Roman official such as the governor Sallustius Lucullus.

## Stop: hinton-st-mary-mosaic
In a villa building in Dorset, a fourth-century mosaic floor shows a man's head before a chi-rho, usually read as Christ. Found in 1963 and lifted to the British Museum, it covered two connected rooms. One panel shows the hero Bellerophon on Pegasus killing the Chimaera; the other has the clean-shaven bust before the chi-rho, flanked by pomegranates and surrounded by hunting scenes. Some have suggested the bust is an emperor, since emperors used the chi-rho on their coins, and how far the pagan and Christian scenes were meant to be read together is debated. Wealthy owners displayed their beliefs, as well as their taste, on their floors. The style links the floor to a workshop at or near Durnovaria, Roman Dorchester, which supplied the villas of the region.

## Stop: lullingstone-house-church
At Lullingstone in Kent, the owners of a villa had an upper room painted with figures at prayer and chi-rho monograms in the later fourth century. The plaster had fallen into the cellar below and was pieced together after G. W. Meates's excavations, which began in 1949. Like the owners at Hinton St Mary, this household also had a mosaic floor showing Bellerophon killing the Chimaera. Reading the upper rooms as a house church is an interpretation, though a widely accepted one, and a room below had earlier been used for pagan rites. A great house could contain its own place of worship. The villa was abandoned after a fire in the fifth century, and across Britain the villa way of life did not outlast Roman rule for long.

## Stop: dinas-powys-hillfort
After Rome, a wealthy household near Cardiff lived inside a small hillfort and imported wine, pottery and glass from the Mediterranean and Gaul. Leslie Alcock's excavations of 1954–58 found the imported pottery and glass, moulds and crucibles from making fine metalwork, and a large quantity of animal bones, perhaps from feasting. He read it as the court of a local ruler, but no text names the place, and a link with the kingdom of Glywysing is only a suggestion. Radiocarbon dates published in 2023 show that the ramparts he thought Norman were built in the sixth and seventh centuries. Power had moved from villas to defended hilltops.

## Stop: rendlesham-royal-settlement
Bede names Rendlesham in Suffolk as a royal settlement of the East Anglian kings, and fieldwork since 2008 has found a large and wealthy site there. Bede mentions it only once, as the place where Cedd baptised Swithhelm, king of the East Saxons, with King Æthelwold of East Anglia as his sponsor, between 655 and 664. Metal-detecting, geophysics and excavation have traced activity over about 50 hectares: burials, workshops, imported goods, many coins and the plan of a great hall, with occupation from the fifth to the eighth century. That this is the place Bede meant is the prevailing view, based on the name and the wealth of the site, but it remains an interpretation. It lies about 6 km from Sutton Hoo, so the kings of East Anglia now have a place to live as well as a place to be buried. It is a new kind of power, built around a king and his hall.

## Closing
Across six centuries the powerful went on displaying wealth, imported goods and belief in their homes. What changed was the setting: from Roman palaces and villas to defended hilltops and royal halls. The ground shows the homes far better than any written source shows the people in them.
